Overview

This section explains what T1591 covers, why it matters, and where it sits in an attack. Read it first if you are new to reconnaissance. Every later part of this guide builds on the ideas here.

MITRE ATT&CK is a free public catalog that describes how real attackers behave. T1591 - Gather Victim Org Information describes a simple idea: before touching any system, an attacker learns how the victim organization works. They map divisions and business units. They list offices and other physical locations. They record partners, suppliers, and other business relationships. They learn the working rhythm of the company: when it hires, when it ships, when it is busy or quiet. And they identify the people in key roles, such as help desk staff, executives, and system owners. ATT&CK also notes where this leads: the finished profile points the attacker at next steps, such as more reconnaissance, fake accounts, phishing, or entry through a trusted partner.

MITRE lists the main collection channels for this technique: public sources, social platforms, and disclosure channels. Public sources include the corporate website, press releases, and regulatory filings. Social platforms include professional networks where staff list their job titles. Disclosure channels include support forums, contact forms, and question-and-answer sites where the organization itself answers questions. The attacker never has to hack anything to collect these facts.

This kind of collection is called open-source intelligence (OSINT: facts anyone can collect from public sources). No exploit runs. No malware lands. The attacker simply reads what the organization and its people publish. That is the core reason this technique is hard to detect: most of the activity never touches a system that you can log.

Why it matters

A profile of your organization is the raw material for almost every targeted attack. With good organization facts, an attacker can build convincing pretexts. A pretext is a fake story that makes the target trust the attacker. Examples: a recruiter with a real job offer, or an IT support call that quotes real system names. The profile feeds:

  • Phishing - fake messages that trick people into opening links or giving away data, aimed at the right person with the right words.
  • Vishing - voice phishing: phone calls that trick people, for example a caller pretending to be from the help desk.
  • Business email compromise (BEC) - criminals impersonate staff or partners over email to steal money or data.
  • Third-party entry - entering through a supplier or partner who already has trusted access to your systems.

Public facts lower the attacker's cost and risk. Reading a website is free and leaves no trace in your logs. So attackers do this work first, and they do it thoroughly. The quality of the profile often decides whether the attack succeeds.

Where it fits in ATT&CK

T1591 belongs to the Reconnaissance tactic, which has the tactic ID TA0043. A tactic is the "why" of an attack stage; techniques are the "how". Reconnaissance covers everything an attacker does to learn about the target before the first real intrusion attempt.

The platform for this technique is PRE. In ATT&CK, PRE means the action happens before the attacker touches the victim's environment at all. Nothing arrives on your network, so your usual endpoint and network alerts stay silent. This is the defining property of the whole Reconnaissance tactic. It shapes the defense advice in this guide: reduce exposure, and watch the few faint signals that do exist. ATT&CK's detection note for this technique is direct: much of the activity happens outside the visibility of the target organization. So defenders focus detection on the related stages that follow, such as Initial Access. The rules in section 5.5 follow that advice: they watch the edges where recon becomes contact.

Good practice

You cannot stop someone from reading your website. You can shrink what is exposed, watch the few signals that exist, and make the next stage of the attack fail. That is the whole strategy of this guide: accept the gap, manage the exposure, and detect the hand-off.

Animated Simulation

The animation at the top of this section plays the whole story of T1591 in eight steps. Below the animation you will find the same story written out in full. Use whichever format works for you; both carry the same information.

The story follows one attacker who profiles a fictional company called Grandview Resorts. The attacker only uses public sources. Each step shows which nodes are active, which data flows move, and which pieces of evidence reach defenders. The key lesson runs through all eight steps: the attacker works outside your network, so your evidence arrives late, faint, and indirect.

How to read it

Node colors have meaning. Red is the attacker, blue is a target service such as the corporate website, violet is attacker infrastructure, and green is the defender side. The green box marked SOC is the Security Operations Center (SOC: the team that watches alerts and responds to incidents). A node glows when it is active in the current step. Moving dots are data flows between nodes. Green tags next to a node, such as "Web log alert", are defender evidence: the signals that reach your team at that moment.

If the animation does not run, for example because JavaScript is off, read the written version below. It covers every step with the same titles and the same defender notes.

The story, step by step

  1. Step 1 - Pick the target

    The attacker chooses a company and starts reading everything public about it. This choice may come from a list: companies of a certain size, in a certain industry, or holding certain kinds of data. Nothing has been sent to the victim yet, so nothing has been logged either.

    Defender view: recon happens outside your network, so your first job is knowing what is public about your own organization.

  2. Step 2 - Scrape organization pages

    The attacker quickly copies the About, Leadership, and Locations pages. A script can pull dozens of pages in seconds, often with a tool that announces itself in the user agent string, such as a Python requests library. The copied pages later become the base for fake login pages and phishing content.

    Defender view: CDN and WAF (web application firewall: a filter that sits in front of a website and blocks suspicious requests) logs can show one source pulling many organization-info pages. A short, busy window from one address is the sign. This is one of the few places where recon touches infrastructure you control.

  3. Step 3 - Mine professional networks

    The attacker lists employees, their titles, and who reports to whom. Public profiles, job changes, and comments all help. The attacker looks for help desk staff, finance staff, executives, and anyone who posts about systems or processes.

    Defender view: some employees will receive strange recruiter messages or odd connection requests during this phase. Reward every report, because these reports are early warnings.

  4. Step 4 - Probe disclosure channels

    The attacker asks innocent-looking questions in public support spaces: forums, contact forms, and question-and-answer threads. Each answer confirms one more fact about vendors, systems, or procedures. This method is called elicitation: getting information from people through normal-looking conversation.

    Defender view: repeated similar questions from one source are a classic elicitation pattern, and your support portal records can prove it.

  5. Step 5 - Assemble the org map

    The attacker joins all facts into one profile of the company and its people: sites, suppliers, schedules, and roles. This step happens entirely on the attacker's side, often in a spreadsheet or a note-taking tool. There is no traffic to detect because there is no traffic at all.

    Defender view: nothing reaches you at this step. The profile now exists outside your network, and your only move is to have shrunk what was available to collect.

  6. Step 6 - Clone the brand

    The attacker copies logos and text onto a staging site with a look-alike domain. The domain name might swap a letter or add a hyphen so that it reads almost the same as the real one. A TLS certificate for the new domain gets issued, and issuing it writes a public record.

    Defender view: brand monitoring and CT log watching can reveal the new domain early, sometimes days before the first phishing message is sent.

  7. Step 7 - Prepare the approach

    The attacker links the profile to a phishing plan: who gets contacted, how, and when. The plan picks roles with access, moments with weak coverage such as holidays, and a pretext that matches real projects. The staging site is armed, and test emails may be sent to accounts the attacker controls.

    Defender view: watch for the hand-off to phishing (T1566) in mail gateway logs. New sender domains and odd recruiter themes are the visible edge of this step.

  8. Step 8 - Hand-off to initial access

    The finished profile is handed to the access phase. From here the guide on T1566 - Phishing continues the story, because the attacker now sends real messages to real people. Everything collected in steps 1 to 7 rides along inside those messages.

    Defender view: from now on your own network and mail detections take over. Report, correlate, and connect the downstream alerts back to the recon signals you saw earlier.

Note

Compare the evidence tags across the steps. They appear only when recon touches something observable: your web logs, your support portal, public domain records, or your people. Steps 1 and 5 carry no tags at all. That silence is the central detection problem of PRE techniques, and section 4 shows how to work with it.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains how attackers think when they profile an organization. The goal is defensive: if you understand what the attacker wants and how they work, you can predict what they will do next and remove what they feed on. All exercises here stay strictly on your own property.

When and why attackers profile organizations

Profiling is the cheapest and safest step of the whole attack chain. It costs nothing but time. It carries no legal risk of "hacking" because reading public pages is not intrusion. And it shapes everything that follows: the target list, the story, the timing, and the tools. A crew that spends a week on profiling writes better phishing messages than a crew that starts on day one.

Attackers usually profile an organization when three things are true. First, they have chosen a target, either from a list or after buying access leads from another criminal. Second, the attack is targeted, which means one specific organization, not a spray of random spam. Third, they plan to touch people, not only machines: help desk staff, finance staff, executives, or new hires. The more human contact the plan needs, the more organization facts it consumes.

Profiling also answers practical questions. Does this company use an external help desk? Who signs invoices on Fridays? When did the last big acquisition happen, and did it leave two identity systems running in parallel? Which branch offices have small IT teams? Each answer removes one point of uncertainty for the attacker, and uncertainty is what gets attackers caught.

How the collection works, conceptually

MITRE names the main channels for T1591: public sources, social platforms, and disclosure channels. In practice, four kinds of places carry most of the value.

The public website

About pages, Leadership pages, Locations pages, and press rooms describe the structure of the company in the company's own words. The attacker copies these pages wholesale, then extracts facts: site names, executive names, brand assets, and partner logos. A fast script can mirror a mid-size website in minutes.

Professional networks

Public professional profiles are the single richest source of role information. Staff list job titles, projects, tools, and sometimes grievances. Attackers build a staff list with titles and reporting lines, then mark the roles they need: identity administrators, help desk agents, treasury staff, and executive assistants.

Press, filings, and public records

Press releases reveal tempo: product launches, expansions, and leadership changes. Regulatory filings and procurement notices reveal suppliers and budgets. Court records, property records, and permits reveal physical sites. Job ads reveal the technology stack and the gaps a company is trying to fill.

Support and disclosure channels

Public support forums, contact forms, and community threads let the attacker ask questions directly. Questions look harmless one at a time: "Which SSO (single sign-on: one login service that covers many systems) product do you support?" or "Do contractors use the same portal?". Over weeks, the answers confirm the attacker's map. MITRE explicitly lists victim disclosure channels as a collection path for this technique.

What the attacker needs before starting

  • Public exposure. The technique feeds on what the organization and its people publish. Every company has some.
  • Patience and discipline. Good profiles take days to weeks. The attacker keeps notes, deduplicates facts, and tracks which facts are confirmed versus guessed.
  • Basic tools. A headless browser, a note tool, and sometimes a scraping script. Nothing exotic is required, which is exactly why the technique is so common.
  • A use for the data. Profiling without a plan wastes time. Skilled attackers profile toward a specific next step: a phishing kit, a vishing script, or a supplier route.

Lab-only exercises for defenders

The best way to understand this technique is to profile your own organization and see what an attacker would see. The three exercises below are safe because they only touch your own public pages, your own job ads, and your own documents. Do not run any exercise against other companies.

1

Lab only: inventory your own public pages. From a private browser window that is not signed in anywhere, walk your corporate website the way an outsider would. List every page that names people, sites, systems, partners, or processes. Count how many pages reveal an internal team name or a product codename. Hand the list to the page owners and ask one question for each item: does this need to be public?

2

Lab only: read your own job ads like an attacker. Collect the last three months of postings. Highlight every technology name, every mention of the security team size, and every phrase like "fast-paced environment without heavy process". Together, the ads sketch your stack and your weaknesses. This is the same reading an attacker does before writing a pretext.

3

Lab only: audit metadata in your own documents. Download a handful of PDFs, DOCX, and PPTX files from your own public download area. Check their properties for author names, internal email addresses, software versions, and file paths, for example paths that leak internal server names. Metadata is data about the file itself, stored inside the file. Strip it before publishing, or publish a flattened copy.

Warning

Keep these exercises scoped to your own organization. Profiling a company you do not own may break its terms of service, local privacy expectations, or employment rules. This is true even when you only read public data, and it is worse from a work account. If you want a target for practice, use one of the many deliberately vulnerable public training sites made for this purpose.

Mistakes attackers make while profiling

Over-collecting leaves patterns

Greed shows in logs. A script that pulls every page, every document, and every image in one sweep is easy to spot, because no human reads that fast. Skilled defenders use this: thresholds on pages per minute, unusual user agent strings, and document download counts all catch careless collectors. Attackers who pace their requests and vary their tools are rarer and quieter, but many crews skip that discipline.

Reusing infrastructure

Attackers are creatures of habit. The same registrar, the same hosting provider, the same staging server, and the same look-alike domain patterns appear across campaigns. Once a blue team tracks one clone domain, the rest of the set often falls out of public records. Two sources help: Certificate Transparency logs, and passive DNS (DNS: domain name system, the internet's address book; passive DNS: public records of which domains pointed to which servers). This is why section 5 asks you to keep brand alerts for months, not days: patterns repeat.

Danger

Using these methods against real systems, organizations, or people that you do not own is illegal in most countries. Laws against unauthorized access, fraud, and data protection violations apply even when only public pages are involved, and probing people through fake recruiter contact can constitute harassment or fraud. This guide describes attacker behavior only so defenders can recognize and disrupt it. Practice only on your own organization or on sites that explicitly allow testing.

4

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

This section is the mirror image of section 3: what the defending team actually sees, and does not see, while an attacker profiles the organization. Expect a theme of faint and indirect signals, then a concrete triage flow you can adapt.

Why detection is hard here

Two properties make T1591 different from most techniques you detect. First, it happens off-perimeter. The attacker reads public pages, sits on public platforms, and asks questions in public channels. Your endpoint agents see nothing, your network sensors see nothing, and your identity provider sees nothing, because nothing has connected to your systems yet. Second, most signals are indirect. The attacker's activity touches public infrastructure that belongs to other parties: social platforms, search engines, and public record sites. You do not own those systems, so you cannot log them directly.

The practical result is uncomfortable but manageable: you will almost never get an alert that says "reconnaissance is happening". Instead you get scattered hints, each of which looks small alone. The job of a SOC analyst is to recognize the hints, connect them, and slow the attack down before the first real message arrives.

Note

Measure success differently for this technique. A win is not "we caught the recon". A win is "we shrank the exposed facts, we saw the clone domain on day one, and the help desk refused the fake reset call". Think in terms of exposure and friction, not blocking.

The faint artifacts that do exist

Even though the collection itself is invisible, five kinds of traces tend to appear. None of them proves an attack on its own. Together, they form a usable picture.

Web and CDN logs

When the attacker scrapes your public pages, the request still passes through your web servers or your CDN and WAF. Those layers log source address, path, time, and user agent. Bulk pulls of About, Leadership, and Locations pages within seconds are visible here.

Brand and digital-risk monitoring

Commercial services and open tools watch domain registrations, app stores, and social media for copies of your brand. They report look-alike domains, fake support accounts, and cloned pages. The feed contains many alerts that do not matter, but it is one of the earliest reliable signals.

CT log monitoring

Every publicly trusted TLS certificate is written to public Certificate Transparency logs. A new certificate for a domain that resembles yours, issued days before a phishing wave, is a strong early signal. Cheap watchers exist for this.

Support-channel records

Your own support portal, public forums, and contact forms record every question. Elicitation shows up as repeated, similar questions from one source, phrased carefully to avoid naming internal systems the asker should not know. These records usually stay with the support team and never reach the SIEM, which is exactly the gap to fix.

Employee reports

Staff on the receiving end of recruiter pretexts, odd connection requests, or strange phone calls are human sensors. One report is trivia. Several reports with the same theme in one week are a campaign. This only works if reporting is easy, thanked, and answered.

Six-step triage flow for a suspected recon signal

Use this flow when any T1591 signal arrives: a brand alert, a scraping pattern, or a staff report. It turns a vague hint into a decision.

1

Validate the signal. Confirm the source and the facts. Is the look-alike domain really new? Is the scraping address really external? Rule out your own scanners, partners, and marketing campaigns first, because those cause most false positives.

2

Scope what was collected. For scraping, list every path the source touched and compare it with your exposure inventory. For elicitation, collect the questions asked. Write down what the attacker now plausibly knows about your organization.

3

Check the mail gateway. Search the last 30 days for the look-alike domain in sender addresses, display names, and links, and for recruiter themes aimed at the roles involved. If mail already flowed, you are not in recon anymore; escalate to the phishing playbook.

4

Block and take down. Block the domain at mail gateway, proxy, and DNS filters. Open a takedown with your registrar contact or brand-protection vendor. A clone domain that cannot receive mail is a dead phishing page.

5

Hunt for related infrastructure. Pull CT log entries and passive DNS for the domain pattern, and check whether other domains in the same set exist. Map what you find to your exposure review list and notify the affected role owners.

6

Report and decide. Write a short summary: what was seen, what the attacker likely knows, what was blocked, and who was notified. Then make the decision that matters: treat it as background noise and close, or brief leadership and tighten exposure. Either answer is acceptable; an undocumented one is not.

5.2Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence (TI: analyzed knowledge about attackers and their methods) matters more than usual for a PRE technique. It can tell you what your industry is currently being profiled for. You cannot see the attacker reading your website. But vendor reporting may tell you that crews are targeting casino help desks this quarter, or that a recruiter-promise campaign is hitting aerospace staff. That knowledge turns your monitoring priorities from guesses into choices.

Note: what confidence means here

High means multiple independent, reputable sources agree, often with technical detail. Moderate means the claim is plausible and reported, but rests on fewer sources or on inference, for example "ransomware crews generally". Low means a single unverified report; treat it as a lead, not a fact. When you brief leadership, carry the confidence level with the claim. It changes the decision: a High-confidence targeting pattern justifies a control change this quarter. A Low-confidence rumor justifies watching.

From collected data to action in three steps

Threat intelligence only pays for itself when it moves through three stages: collection, analysis, and application. Each stage is small. Skipping one is where programs fail.

1

Collect. Pull the sources above into one weekly review: advisories, vendor blogs, your ISAC mailings, and your own logs. Set a fixed time slot so collection actually happens.

2

Analyze. Ask two questions per item: does it concern my industry and my roles, and how confident is the claim? Keep only what passes both, and record the confidence level with the finding.

3

Apply. Turn each kept finding into one of the four actions in the next list: a hunt, an alert note, a briefing line, or an exposure check. Intelligence that changes nothing is only a storage cost.

How to apply intelligence to this technique

  • Hunting. When reporting says crews profile your industry, pull your web logs, brand alerts, and support records for the matching patterns from the last 90 days. You are checking whether the campaign already passed your door.
  • Alert context. Attach what you know to the alerts you have. A scraping alert becomes more urgent if the tool and pacing match a reported campaign. A recruiter report becomes a campaign marker if two colleagues report the same fake agency in one week.
  • Executive briefings. Translate crews into risk language: "groups that hit our sector first study help desk verification; here is what we changed because of that". This is the sentence that gets funding for phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Exposure reviews. Use the collected-facts lists in vendor reports as a checklist against your own public pages. If a report says a crew used leadership photos and office layouts, check what yours expose today.

Categorization by signal platform

The same six threats sort into three groups by where you can act. This is the practical summary to remember.

Network and cloud signals you can log

Scraping (rank 2), document harvesting (rank 4), and support-channel probing (rank 5) all touch infrastructure you control or can observe: web servers, CDN, WAF, and your support portal. These are the only threats on the list that produce direct log evidence. Own them with the rules in section 5.5.

Identity and people signals you train and collect

Phishing and vishing kits (rank 1), role mapping (rank 3), and social monitoring (rank 6) happen on platforms you do not own. Your levers are training, easy reporting, help desk verification, and the mail gateway alerts that fire once the attack starts. Culture is a control here, not a soft extra.

The PRE gap you must accept and manage

Some of the attacker's work produces no signal you will ever see: private reading, note-taking, and joining facts into a map. You cannot detect that. You manage it by shrinking the source material with exposure reviews and by preparing for the stage where the profile gets used. Accept the gap; plan for the hand-off.

5.4Logs and Patterns

This subsection defines the six log sources that carry T1591 signals. For each source you get the key fields, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, a one-sentence detection logic, and a realistic sample event. The samples use the documentation address ranges (203.0.113.0/24, 198.51.100.0/24) and the fictional company Grandview Resorts, so nothing here points at a real victim.

a) CDN and WAF web logs (nginx access format)

Key fields
client_ip, time, method, uri_path, status, bytes, user_agent
Normal behavior
Human visitors spread across many addresses, request a handful of pages each, come from search engines and social sites, and carry browser user agents with cache headers. Bots from search companies identify themselves honestly and respect a crawl pace.
Suspicious behavior
One address fetches many organization-info pages in seconds, in path order, with a library user agent such as python-requests, no referer, and no asset requests like images or stylesheets. Machines read pages; humans load the pictures.
Detection logic
Alert when one client address requests more than 40 organization-info pages within 10 minutes, or pulls documents at more than 30 per hour.

The last line shows a normal visitor for contrast: browser user agent, a referer from a search engine, and one page. Note what is missing in the scraping lines: no referer, no image or stylesheet requests, and one-second gaps between pages.

b) Brand and digital-risk monitoring feed

Key fields
alert_type, lookalike_domain, similarity_score, first_seen, target_domain
Normal behavior
A low, steady background of alerts: domains that contain one common word from your brand, old registrations by partners, and marketing campaign domains you already know. Most organizations see a handful per week, and most close in minutes.
Suspicious behavior
High similarity score, very recent registration age, hyphen or digit substitutions of your exact brand, an MX record set up (which means the domain can receive mail), and a matching certificate issued within days.
Detection logic
Alert immediately on any look-alike domain above a similarity threshold whose registration age is under 30 days, and enrich it with mail gateway and CT log checks.

This single event deserves same-day action: a one-day-old domain, a 0.94 similarity, and a working MX record is the classic pre-phishing signature. A URL (uniform resource locator: a web address) on such a domain should be blocked at mail and proxy before the first message arrives.

c) Support portal and public forum records

Key fields
ts, source_ip, thread_id, subject, account
Normal behavior
Questions come from mixed addresses, name real order numbers or logged-in accounts, and vary in topic. Guests ask about bookings and pricing; signed-in users ask about their own tickets.
Suspicious behavior
One guest address posts several carefully worded questions about internal process in one session: badge vendors, VPN (virtual private network: a private tunnel into company systems) portals, approval steps. The questions never reference any order or account, and they avoid naming systems the asker should not know.
Detection logic
Alert when one source posts more than 10 form submissions to support or contact paths within 24 hours, and review the text for process and vendor topics.

Each question is polite and answerable on its own. Together they sketch contractor access at one site, which is exactly the fact set a caller would need to sound like a contractor. Route this feed to the SIEM so the pattern survives staff rotation.

d) Mail gateway and report mailbox

Key fields
ts, sender_domain, subject, report_type, recipients
Normal behavior
Real recruiters come from known agencies with full signatures and reply to the person they contacted. Volume of user reports stays low and spreads across many unrelated themes.
Suspicious behavior
Fresh sender domains with recruiter wording, thin signatures, urgency to move to another messaging app, and several staff reporting the same theme or agency name in one week.
Detection logic
Track user-reported suspicious contacts by theme and sender domain, and alert when the same theme repeats across reporters within days.

The four-day domain age is the strong clue here. Combine with the support and web log signals above and the picture is complete: someone is building a list of roles worth calling.

e) CT log monitor feed

Key fields
not_before, common_name, san, issuer
Normal behavior
Your own certificates renew on schedule, and unrelated certificates match your brand only loosely, for example common English words that appear in your company name.
Suspicious behavior
A new certificate whose full name contains your brand string, issued days before a campaign, from a certificate authority you never use, with a SAN (Subject Alternative Name: the list of hostnames a certificate covers) that repeats the same pattern.
Detection logic
Alert on any new publicly logged certificate whose common name contains the full brand string and that was issued by an authority outside your approved list.

A certificate for "hr-portal" plus your brand, issued by a public authority you have never used, is worth a same-day check: resolve the host, look for a login form, and open a takedown if one exists.

f) Mail gateway security events

Key fields
ts, recipient, sender_domain, sender_domain_age_days, subject, disposition, spf_result, dkim_result
Normal behavior
Recruiting mail arrives from domains with months or years of age, passes sender checks, and matches roles your recruiting team expects. Business mail is delivered; junk is quarantined at a steady, boring rate.
Suspicious behavior
A sender domain registered within the last 30 days, a subject that names your brand or a staff member, recruiter or support wording, and authentication results that fail or stay empty.
Detection logic
Alert when a message from a sender domain younger than 30 days is delivered and its subject contains your brand string, a staff surname, or a known pretext theme.

Authentication passed because the sender controls its own domain. That is normal for attacker-owned domains, and it is why the age and the subject carry the rule instead. Note that this is the gateway's own view of delivery. It pairs with the user report in source d: one campaign can reach one employee through the inbox and another through a phone call.

Note: baselines prevent false positives

Every source above has a "normal" profile, and your thresholds must come from your own baseline, not from this page. A careers site that is 80 percent job pages will trip a document rule written for a press-heavy site. Measure one quiet week per source: pages per visitor, documents per day, form posts per source. Then set thresholds at the level that would have caught the sample events but stays silent on your measured normal. Revisit the baseline when your site grows, and write the numbers down so the next analyst inherits them.

5.5Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

This subsection ships six working detections. Each detection comes in two forms: a Sigma rule and a matching Splunk SPL query. Sigma is a YAML text format that describes detection rules in a vendor-neutral way, so one rule can be translated into many SIEM products. Splunk SPL (Splunk Search Processing Language) is the search language used inside Splunk. Every Sigma rule here carries a unique id, a UUID (universally unique identifier: a random 128-bit ID string), so your rule repository never mixes up two rules.

The coverage map below links every ranked threat from section 5.3 to at least one rule, and names the two places where detection is indirect or impossible. Keep it next to the rules when you argue for funding.

R1 - Bulk Requests to Organization Information Pages

This rule catches the scraping step of the simulation: one address reading the whole organization story in minutes.

R1 notes

Mapping note
Sigma fields cs-method, cs-uri-stem, c-ip, and cs-user-agent map to the Splunk fields method, uri_path, src_ip, and user_agent after normalization at ingest. The dc() function counts distinct pages, so one address hitting /about forty times does not alert, but five organization pages do.
False positives
Approved scanners, marketing link checkers, and uptime monitors. Any of them can pull dozens of these pages in ten minutes.
Tuning guidance
Allow-list scanner user agents and their source ranges, exclude owned CIDR blocks (a CIDR block is a range of network addresses), and scale the threshold to your site: a small site with five org pages should lower the count, a large one should raise it. Add a repeat-visitor baseline for your top pages.
Severity
Medium

R2 - Look-alike Domain Alert from Digital Risk Monitor

This rule surfaces the brand-cloning step as soon as the monitoring feed reports it.

R2 notes

Mapping note
The Sigma rule runs on a custom product source (product digital_risk_monitor) because most brand feeds are software-as-a-service alerts, not raw logs. The SPL keeps the highest similarity score per domain so the sort works on one row per lookalike_domain.
False positives
Marketing's own campaign domains and legitimate regional partners. Keep a signed allow-list of known-good domains owned by your company.
Tuning guidance
Register common typos of your brand yourself so nobody else can take them. Raise the similarity floor if the source sends more than a handful of alerts per week, and always enrich with mail gateway checks before closing.

High

R3 - Employee Reports Suspicious Recruiter or Probe Contact

This rule is informational by design. A single report is not an incident, but a cluster of reports is the clearest human signature of role mapping. Note what it cannot do. The mapping itself happens on platforms you cannot log, so this rule is an echo signal. It detects the contact attempts that the finished staff list feeds, not the mapping itself.

R3 notes

Mapping note
Sigma reads from the ticketing product with a custom logsource, because reports live in ITSM systems (IT Service Management, the ticket platform) rather than in raw logs. The SPL groups by the role or person the contact was about, using reported_about, so a cluster across colleagues of one team stands out.
False positives
Genuine recruiters and sales outreach. The rule's value is the aggregate pattern, not the single event, so keep severity low.
Tuning guidance
Exclude test tickets from training exercises with a dedicated category value. Review weekly, and alert separately when three or more reports share a theme or sender domain within seven days. Always answer the reporter; unanswered reporters stop reporting.
Severity
Low (high value, informational)

R4 - Bulk Downloads of Public Documents

This rule catches the metadata harvest: a collector pulling every published PDF, DOCX, and PPTX to read file properties.

R4 notes

Mapping note
Same normalized fields as R1. The endswith modifier anchors on file extensions, and the regex in SPL anchors on the end of the path so query strings do not break the match.
False positives
Search indexers and archiving services, plus any quarterly release where you publish a full report set and news outlets fetch many files at once.
Tuning guidance
Exclude owned CIDR blocks and known indexer ranges. If you publish large sets regularly, raise the threshold for the publish window only, and note the exception in the rule comments so the exception does not quietly become permanent.
Severity
Medium

R5 - Repeated Inquiries to Public Support Channels

This rule watches for elicitation: the same source submitting question after question to your public support surfaces.

R5 notes

Mapping note
Same web log fields as R1 and R4, with cs-referrer added to the Sigma fields because referrer context helps separate a human support session from a scripted run. The threshold is deliberately gentle, since the point is to surface threads for reading, not to page anyone at night.
False positives
Uptime monitors, accessibility checkers, and one busy customer who uses the public form because login is broken. Read the subjects before judging.
Tuning guidance
Exclude owned CIDR blocks and your monitor vendor's ranges. Add the support portal's own submit paths to the allow-list if agents work from a shared address. Pair the alert with the text review step from section 5.4, because the volume alone means little.
Severity
Low

R6 - Fresh Sender Domain With Organization Pretext at the Mail Gateway

This rule closes the loop for threat 1: the phishing or vishing kit built from organization facts. It fires when a young sender domain names your organization or your people in a recruiter or support theme, and the message was delivered. This is the first moment the profile from T1591 becomes touchable on your side.

R6 notes

Mapping note
Sigma reads from a custom mail gateway source, because every mail security product logs delivery under its own schema. The two brand tokens ('Grandview', 'Alvarez') are the fictional company from the samples; swap them for your own brand string and the surnames of your most-targeted roles. The SPL mirrors the Sigma logic exactly: young domain AND pretext subject AND delivered disposition.
Field assumptions
The pipeline normalizes gateway events to sender_domain, subject, recipient, and disposition, and enriches sender_domain_age_days at ingest from domain registration records. If your gateway cannot track domain age, join it from a brand feed at search time and expect slower searches.
False positives
New but legitimate recruiting agencies, and your own marketing domains in their first weeks. Check the sender against your brand allow-list before escalating.
Tuning guidance
Keep the age threshold at 30 days and review the pretext tokens each quarter against reported pretexts. Add a second, quieter variant that also fires on quarantined deliveries if you want a hunting view instead of paging.
Severity
High

Good practice: test your rules

A rule that has never fired in a test is a hope, not a detection. Replay the sample events from section 5.4 against each rule, confirm it fires for the right event and stays silent for the normal ones, and record the result. Repeat the test after every threshold change. Two minutes of testing today prevents a quarter of silent failures.

Real-World Case Study

This case study shows T1591 in a real attack: the MGM Resorts compromise of September 2023. The facts below come from public reporting by Mandiant, Microsoft, and major news outlets. The framing stays defensive throughout: what the attackers did, which signals existed, and what other organizations should copy or avoid.

Context: who attacked, and against what

Scattered Spider (also tracked as UNC3944) is an English-speaking criminal group known for social engineering rather than exotic malware. In 2023 the group worked with the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware operation; ransomware is malware that encrypts a victim's files and demands payment for the key. Earlier that same month, the same crew's activity against Caesars Entertainment ended with a reported payment of roughly 15 million US dollars. That payment is context for what MGM was up against, not a separate event to analyze here.

MGM Resorts International runs hotels and casinos, which means three things attackers love: continuous guest-facing services, a huge and varied workforce, and a hard cost for downtime. The attack did not begin with a technical exploit. It began with the organization's public information and a phone call.

Tools used (as reported publicly)

Collection
Public professional network profiles, search engines, and job boards, studied before any contact
Initial access
A phone-based pretext (vishing) to the outsourced IT help desk, impersonating an employee with details from public profiles, ending in an MFA reset to a device the attacker controlled
Identity
Access to the Okta identity platform, then movement into the wider identity and management plane
Impact
ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware deployed against virtualization hosts (hypervisors), with server administration tools reportedly used to spread software across the estate

The recon phase, mapped to T1591

Public reporting describes the group studying MGM before making contact. The table ties the reported behaviors to the sub-techniques of T1591.

Reported recon behaviorT1591 mappingDefender lesson

Timeline of the incident

  1. Mid-2023 (before September)

    Scattered Spider builds its MGM profile from public sources: staff lists from professional networks, organization structure, and help desk practices. No intrusion has happened. This is T1591 exactly as this guide describes it.

  2. 2023-09-08

    A caller phones the IT help desk, impersonates a real MGM employee using details gathered from public profiles, and requests an account reset. The caller sounds informed: right names, right context, right confidence.

  3. 2023-09-08

    The help desk grants the request and resets the employee's MFA, enrolling the attacker's own device instead. Multi-factor authentication (MFA: a second check such as an app code that proves who you are) was working as designed; the identity verification behind it failed.

  4. 2023-09-08 to 2023-09-09 (weekend)

    Using the reset account, the attacker reaches MGM's Okta environment. Okta is an identity provider, the system that manages employee logins. From there the attacker escalates privileges, meaning they collect permissions beyond the original account.

  5. 2023-09-09 to 2023-09-10

    Lateral movement spreads access across the environment. Reporting highlights Microsoft Configuration Manager, a tool meant for administering thousands of company computers, being abused to push software at scale. The attack remains inside the identity and management plane, which most guest-facing monitoring does not watch closely on a weekend.

  6. 2023-09-10 to 2023-09-11

    The ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware payload is deployed against virtualization hosts. A hypervisor is the software that runs many virtual machines on one physical server; hitting hypervisors encrypts everything they host at once.

  7. 2023-09-11

    MGM takes large parts of its environment offline to contain the spread. This deliberate shutdown stops the encryption but also takes down hotel keys, payments, and casino systems that guests touch directly.

  8. 2023-09-11 to 2023-09-13, and the weeks after

    Operations run in manual mode: paper check-in, cash payments, long lines on casino floors. Core systems return within roughly 36 hours, while some services take days to weeks to fully recover. Reporting later puts the quarter's impact at about 100 million US dollars of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization: a measure of operating profit). One-time costs added about 10 million US dollars more.

The attack chain in six steps

1

Recon (T1591). Build the organization profile from public sources: roles, structure, help desk behavior. No signal reaches the victim.

2

Pretext. Phone the help desk as an employee, armed with enough real detail to sound legitimate. The call is the exploit.

3

MFA reset. Talk the help desk into resetting MFA and enrolling the attacker's device. Identity verification fails once, and that once is enough.

4

Access. Sign in through the identity provider with a legitimate credential and a device the attacker controls. Everything after this looks "normal" to the systems.

5

Escalation. Move across the identity and management plane over the weekend, collect permissions, and prepare software distribution through administration tools.

6

Ransomware. Deploy the encryptor to hypervisors to encrypt as many machines as possible at once, then pressure the victim while guests queue at desks. The recon from step 1 decided where this step would hurt most.

Warning: where detection could have happened

The recon phase produced no alerts, exactly as section 4 predicts. The first real chance to stop the attack was the help desk call. Verification should have failed, because no caller verification script can be satisfied by facts that are public anyway. The second chance came during escalation. Endpoint detection and response (EDR: software on servers and laptops that watches for malicious behavior) and firewall alerts eventually appeared, but on a weekend with a moving attacker. The lesson is not that detection was absent; it is that the cheapest kill was human, at the phone call, before the attacker ever touched a keyboard on your side.

Containment, eradication, recovery

Three response words appear in every major incident, so here they are in plain terms. Containment means stopping the attacker from spreading further. Eradication means removing every part of the attacker from your systems. Recovery means getting services back safely.

Containment

MGM took large parts of the environment offline on September 11, including guest-facing systems. Isolation stopped the encryption from spreading further and bought time, at the cost of visible service disruption. The decision was aggressive, and reporting suggests it was the right call: a partial outage recovered in days beats a full compromise of backups and every site.

Eradication

Removing an attacker who lives in your identity system means resets everywhere: credentials, MFA enrollments, tokens, and administrative accounts, plus rebuilds of systems where persistence cannot be ruled out. Credential hygiene became the project: fresh secrets, tightened verification for any future resets, and a review of who could touch hypervisors and management tools.

Recovery

Core systems came back within roughly 36 hours, and properties operated in degraded manual mode in the meantime. Full recovery stretched over days to weeks as services were validated and restored. The pace matched the scope that the recon had helped choose: identity, payments, keys, and casino floors.

Impact, in numbers

MGM reported the cost in a public 8-K filing (a report United States companies must file for material events). The filing put the damage at about 100 million US dollars of EBITDA for the third quarter of 2023. One-time expenses for legal, consulting, and remediation added roughly 10 million US dollars more. Guests saw queues, outages, and degraded services; employees ran manual processes for days. The final bill for a phone call that lasted minutes.

Team response and communication

Staff switched to manual check-in, cash handling, and paper processes, which worked only because people improvised well under pressure. MGM published statements acknowledging the issue and worked with law enforcement and external specialists. The case also triggered regulatory and notification duties that large companies owe after such events. For your own planning, the takeaway is simple: rehearse the manual mode, prepare the public statement template, and know who owes notifications before the day arrives.

Lessons learned

  • Strong help-desk identity verification. Caller ID, voice tone, and public facts are not identity. Use callbacks to numbers on file, video proof for high-risk resets, or supervisor approval paths that cannot be rushed.
  • Phishing-resistant MFA. Hardware-bound credentials and push-number matching resist both phishing and the reset abuse seen here. Weak MFA flows are part of the attack surface.
  • Role-exposure reviews. Run the exercise from section 3 on your own org: what do help desk staff, identity admins, and executives expose on public networks, and does it need to be there?
  • Employee reporting culture. Someone may have seen the reconnaissance touching their colleagues: odd recruiter messages, odd connection requests. Make reporting easy and thank the reporters.
  • Rehearse manual operations. Paper check-in and cash handling saved the guest experience here. Write the runbook before you need it, and test it like a fire drill.

Mitigation and Defense

You cannot block someone from reading public pages, so this section does not promise that. Instead it lists controls that shrink what attackers can learn, harden the places their profile will aim at, and speed up your response when the downstream attack starts. Effort and owner are stated for each control so the list can go straight into a plan.

Note: the honest framing

Reconnaissance is not stoppable at the source. What you control is the supply. That means: how much your organization publishes, how hard the help desk is to fool, how fast you see a clone domain, and how quickly the phishing wave dies. Read every control below through that lens. Effort ratings assume a mid-size organization: Low means days, Medium means weeks, High means a quarter or more of program work. ATT&CK lists one mitigation for this technique: M1056 Pre-compromise. Its advice matches this section. Since the technique runs outside enterprise defenses, focus on minimizing the amount and sensitivity of data available to external parties.

Do this first

If you only get six items funded this quarter, take these, in this order.

  • Fix help-desk verification. Callbacks to numbers on file, supervisor approval for MFA resets, and a rule that urgency is never a reason to skip steps. This is the cheapest control with the highest single-point payoff.
  • Ship web logs to the SIEM. Normalize fields, and keep 90 days in fast search storage. Without this, half of this guide is unreadable in your own environment.
  • Deploy the six rules from section 5.5 with tuned thresholds, and test each one with the sample events. Silent rules protect nobody.
  • Name an owner for public exposure. One person owns the exposure review, the brand feed, and the report mailbox. Unowned feeds decay into noise.
  • Answer every employee report. Publish the count of reports received and handled each month. The sensor only stays on if it is thanked.
  • Rehearse the pretext scenario. Ten minutes in a team meeting: someone reads a fake reset call aloud, the desk responds per script. Do it quarterly.

Home and small-office advice

If you are a small team or working from home

Enterprise tools are overkill, but the ideas shrink well. Lock down your own public profiles: remove job details, badge photos, and answers that describe how your employer verifies people. Verify unexpected calls by hanging up and dialing the official number yourself; never trust a number the caller gives you. Report odd recruiter messages to your employer's security contact even if you are not sure, because one message often is the first data point of a campaign. And treat "please read me your MFA code" as the end of the conversation, whatever the story is.

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

Most failures around this technique are organizational, not technical. The eleven cards below describe the mistakes teams actually make, each with the symptom you will notice, the root cause, the fix, and the prevention that keeps it from returning. The three habit cards at the end turn the fixes into routine.

1. Ignoring PRE techniques as "undetectable"

Symptom
The coverage matrix jumps from no coverage to full phishing coverage, with a blank where reconnaissance should be.
Root cause
The team treats "we cannot see it" as "there is nothing to do", and stops reading after the platform column says PRE.
Fix
Map the five observable signals from section 4 to owners this week, even if two of them are manual reviews.
Prevention
Add a standing rule in detection reviews: every PRE technique gets either a signal or a documented acceptance note.

2. Treating brand alerts as noise

Symptom
The look-alike domain alert queue shows dozens of auto-closed alerts, including the one that later phished the company.
Root cause
Feed volume was never tuned, and closing alerts became a chore with no feedback loop about which ones mattered.
Fix
Retune the feed, add enrichment (registration age, MX record, certificate), and require a 24-hour review for high-similarity hits.
Prevention
Track closed alerts against later incidents monthly, so the team sees when the feed produces real value.

3. No owner for public exposure

Symptom
Nobody can say who approved publishing the staff directory, and job ads describe the internal tools in detail.
Root cause
Public content is split across marketing, HR, and engineering, and security assumes someone else watches it.
Fix
Assign one accountable owner plus a page-level contact list, and run the first review with the section 3 exercise.
Prevention
Make exposure sign-off part of the publishing workflow, not an afterthought after launch.

4. Support and forum data never reaches the SIEM

Symptom
The elicitation pattern from section 5.4 existed for weeks, but only the support team ever saw it.
Root cause
The support portal is treated as a business system, not a sensor, so nobody built the pipe into central logging.
Fix
Export support form submissions and forum posts to the SIEM daily, and deploy rule R5.
Prevention
Include support channels in the logging inventory review, so new channels get a pipe by default.

5. Scraping alerts tuned too hot, causing fatigue

Symptom
R1 fires daily, analysts silence it, and a real crawler slips through during the silence.
Root cause
The threshold was copied from this page without measuring the site's normal traffic, so legitimate crawls trip it constantly.
Fix
Rebaseline over one quiet week, allow-list known scanners, and raise the threshold until alerts are rare and worth reading.
Prevention
Re-measure after every site change and require a test-fire before any threshold edit ships.

6. Scanner false positives never allow-listed

Symptom
The same approved scanner appears in alerts every week, and every week someone closes it by hand.
Root cause
There is no owned inventory of approved scanning sources, so no one writes the exclusion.
Fix
Collect the scanner user agents and address ranges from the owning team, then add matching exclusions to R1 and R4.
Prevention
Route scanner registration through change management so the list stays current as vendors rotate.

7. CDN logs kept 7 days or never normalized

Symptom
When a campaign is finally investigated, the scraping that preceded it happened three weeks ago and the logs are gone or unusable.
Root cause
Short default retention at the CDN, and no normalization layer, so field names differ per edge region.
Fix
Ship logs to the SIEM at ingest. Normalize the eight fields from the table in section 5.4. Keep 90 days in fast search storage, plus a year in cheaper storage.
Prevention
Put retention and normalization in the pipeline checklist for every new public property, including microsites.

8. Employee reports go to a mailbox nobody reads

Symptom
Staff stop reporting because nobody ever replies, and the mailbox is checked "when there is time".
Root cause
The mailbox has no queue, no owner, and no measurement, so reports pile up with no reply.
Fix
Route reports into the ticketing system with the R3 rule, set a same-day acknowledgement target, and thank reporters publicly.
Prevention
Publish monthly report counts and outcomes, so staff see that reporting does something.

9. No tuning per organization size or baseline

Symptom
Rules copied from a large-company repository either never fire (small org) or fire hourly (large org).
Root cause
Thresholds are absolute numbers that only make sense against a traffic profile nobody measured.
Fix
Derive every threshold from one week of your own measured baseline, then round to a defensible number.
Prevention
Store the baseline measurement next to the rule, with the date, so the next tuning has a reference point.

10. Rules never validated against test data

Symptom
A rule change silently breaks the logic, and nobody notices for months because the rule had zero fires anyway.
Root cause
No replay test exists, so deployments rely on luck instead of evidence.
Fix
Replay the section 5.4 samples after every change, and record the expected result per rule.
Prevention
Make the replay part of the deployment checklist, with a named approver per rule.

11. Ownership gap between PR, marketing, and security for look-alike domains

Symptom
Security sees a clone domain, marketing says "we handle brand", and the takedown starts four days late.
Root cause
Brand protection and security response are separate processes with no shared runbook.
Fix
Write one runbook with a 24-hour clock: security validates, brand team files the takedown, legal escalates if needed.
Prevention
Test the runbook twice a year with a simulated look-alike registration, and time it.

Habits that keep the defenses alive

Daily

Review new brand-monitor alerts and the report mailbox, both owned and both answered. High-similarity look-alike domains get a 24-hour decision, not a quiet auto-close. Two minutes of daily reading beats a quarterly fire drill.

Weekly

Review web-log anomalies against the baseline, check new CT log certificates that match the brand string, and scan the R3 report cluster for shared themes. Escalate anything that matches the pretexts playbook.

Monthly

Run a short exposure review on changed public pages, retune one threshold from fresh baseline data, and run a practice exercise of the pretext scenario with the service desk. Rotate a new analyst through the review so knowledge does not live in one head.

Response checklist when a T1591 signal fires

  • Validate. Confirm the signal is real and not your own scanner, partner, or marketing campaign.
  • Scope. Write down what the attacker plausibly collected: pages pulled, questions asked, domains registered.
  • Block. Look-alike domains go to mail gateway, proxy, and DNS filters; open the takedown the same day.
  • Hunt. Search mail gateway for the domains and themes, pull CT log matches, and check whether other domains in the set exist.
  • Report. Summarize what was seen, what was exposed, what was blocked, and who was notified, then decide whether leadership hears about it.
