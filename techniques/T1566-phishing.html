T1566 – Phishing

🎯Tactic: Initial Access 💻Platforms: Windows · macOS · Linux · Office Suite · SaaS · Identity Provider 🧩Complexity: Low 🔍Detection difficulty: Medium 📊Version: 2.7 (ATT&CK)

Phishing (T1566) is the most used way for attackers to get a first foothold inside an organization. The attacker sends a fake message — most often an email — and tricks a person into opening a file, clicking a link, or giving away a password. When the message is written for one specific person or company, experts call it spearphishing. When it goes to thousands of random addresses, it is mass phishing, also called malspam. The message can carry a malicious attachment, such as an Office file with a hidden macro. It can carry a link to a fake login page. It can arrive through a trusted third-party service, such as LinkedIn or WhatsApp. Or it can be a phone call that asks the victim to install remote software. MITRE ATT&CK splits this technique into four sub-techniques: attachment, link, service, and voice. Phishing matters because it attacks people, not firewalls. One convincing email can undo a large security budget. In September 2023, a short phone call to the MGM Resorts IT help desk started an attack that MGM later said cost about 100 million dollars. That is why every defender must understand how phishing works and what it looks like in logs.

📎T1566.001 Spearphishing Attachment

The email carries a file: an Office document with macros, a PDF, an ISO image, or an archive. The victim must open it, so the attacker needs one click.

🔗T1566.002 Spearphishing Link

The email carries a link, often to a fake login page. No file lands on disk, so many scanning tools never see a payload.

💬T1566.003 Spearphishing via Service

The attacker uses a trusted third-party service, like LinkedIn, WhatsApp, or a free cloud mail provider. The message comes from a real platform, so it passes simple sender checks.

📞T1566.004 Spearphishing Voice

The attacker calls the victim (vishing), or leaves a message that asks the victim to call a number (callback phishing). People trust voices more than emails.

🧵Tricks around the message

Attackers also spoof sender domains, hijack real email threads, and hide inside OAuth app requests. ATT&CK lists these evasions on the T1566 page itself.

☁️Where it lands

Per ATT&CK, phishing works on Windows, macOS, Linux, Office Suite, SaaS, and Identity Providers. In practice it hits cloud identity first: one stolen password opens SaaS and email.

Recon→ Phishing (T1566)→ User Execution→ Persistence→ Privilege Escalation→ Impact

Think like the attacker

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

3.1 When attackers use this

Phishing is almost always the first move. Attackers use it when they have no other way in: no exposed server, no zero-day (an unknown software flaw), no partner access. A single email costs almost nothing, so attackers can try many times at low risk. You will see phishing waves at predictable times: around invoice payment runs, tax season, holidays, and big world events. Attackers time spearphishing to moments when the target is busy, because busy people click faster. A help desk gets calls at shift change. Finance teams review invoices at month end. Those are the moments attackers choose.

Timing also depends on the goal. Ransomware groups such as GOLD SOUTHFIELD (REvil) send huge malspam waves and hope for any hit. State groups such as APT28 or Kimsuky research one person for weeks and send one perfect email. Both paths start with T1566; only the effort differs.

3.2 How attackers do it, step by step

  1. Choose and study the target. They collect names, roles, and email formats from LinkedIn, the company website, and leaked data. A finance clerk or a help desk agent is a better target than the CEO, because they handle documents and requests all day.
  2. Register a look-alike domain. They buy something like contoso-billing.net or set up a free mail account. They may also hijack a real email thread they bought or stole, so the message continues an existing conversation.
  3. Build the lure. They copy a real invoice, delivery note, or M365 login page. The Office file gets a macro or embedded object. The link points to a fake sign-in page that forwards typed credentials to the attacker.
  4. Deliver the message. They send it by email, through a trusted service like LinkedIn or WhatsApp (T1566.003), or by voice (T1566.004). A compromised partner mailbox makes the message look normal to filters.
  5. Trigger the payload. The victim enables macros, opens the ISO, or types credentials on the fake page. Some kits ask for a second factor to look real, then spam MFA pushes until the victim approves.
  6. Log in as the victim. With a password and an approved push, the attacker signs in from a new IP. Tools can steal session cookies, so sometimes no password is needed at all.
  7. Set up persistence. They create inbox rules to hide security mail, add a second MFA device, or approve a malicious OAuth app. Now the access survives even if the victim changes the password.
  8. Move deeper. From one mailbox they find more targets, VPN portals, and service accounts. Valid accounts (T1078) carry them from initial access to full compromise.

3.3 Where it works best

🎧IT help desks

Help desks are built to be helpful. Under time pressure, an agent may reset a password or MFA factor after one phone call. Scattered Spider used exactly this at MGM Resorts in 2023.

🧾Finance and AP teams

Invoice mailboxes expect attachments from strangers. A fake supplier invoice with a macro fits daily work, so the file gets opened and nobody asks questions.

👔Executives and HR

Business email compromise works on authority and urgency. Fake recruiter profiles (Operation Dream Job, linked to Lazarus) target staff with interesting job offers.

☁️Cloud-heavy companies

If one identity unlocks mail, files, VPN, and SaaS, one phish is enough. M365 and Okta tenants are prime targets, and consent phishing adds OAuth abuse on top.

📱Mobile users

QR codes and SMS links open on phones, where corporate web controls often do not run. QR-code phishing (quishing) and SMS phishing (smishing) skip past laptop defenses completely.

🏭Supply chains

Partners share documents every day. A phish sent from a real partner mailbox that the attacker already owns passes both human and machine trust checks.

3.4 Why attackers like this technique

  • It is cheap and easy to repeat. A failed email costs nothing, and spam infrastructure is rented by the hour.
  • It goes straight through the perimeter. The firewall is never touched; the victim opens the door from the inside.
  • It targets people, and people cannot be patched. Training reduces clicks, but never to zero.
  • The activity looks like normal user behavior. A valid sign-in from a browser is hard to call evil without extra context.
  • One hit is enough. From one mailbox, attackers reach password resets, tokens, and more mailboxes.

3.5 What attackers need first

  • A target list with names, roles, and email addresses, usually from public sources or breach dumps.
  • A sender: a look-alike domain, a rented spam service, or a real mailbox they already took over.
  • A lure: a copied invoice template, a cloned login page, or a short story that creates urgency.
  • Basic skills: simple OSINT (public-source research), some HTML, and a good phone manner for vishing (phishing by phone call). No exploit code needed.
  • Infrastructure: a host for the fake page, an HTTPS certificate, and a place to store stolen data.
  • Patience. Good spearphishing means waiting: research first, one email later.

Think like the defender

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

4.1 What defenders can see

Phishing leaves marks at every step, but they live in different systems. The mail gateway sees the message arrive. The endpoint sees the file open and the macro run. The proxy and DNS see the click and the fake page. The identity provider sees the new sign-in, the MFA reset, or the OAuth consent. No single log tells the whole story, so defenders win by joining signals across these systems within minutes of each other.

The strongest signal is a correlation: a suspicious message delivered to a user, then a new process on that user's machine, then a risky sign-in. Each event alone can be noise. Together they are an attack.

4.3 Where to look

  • Mail gateway and Defender for Office 365: delivery verdicts, spam confidence, quarantines, URL detonation results.
  • Endpoint (Sysmon/EDR): Office apps spawning shells, container files, remote tools, script block logging.
  • Proxy and DNS: clicks to new domains, mobile user agents on login pages, shortener chains.
  • Identity provider (Entra ID, Okta): sign-in risk, MFA changes, consent grants, service principal activity.
  • Microsoft 365 Unified Audit Log: inbox rules, mailbox exports, OAuth consents, URL clicks.
  • Help desk system: password and MFA reset tickets, caller phone numbers, agent notes.
  • Firewall: unexpected outbound sessions from workstations, especially to new ASNs.

4.4 Why this matters to the SOC

Phishing decides the size of the incident. Caught at the email stage, the cost is minutes of analyst time. Caught after the macro runs, you rebuild one machine. Caught after the identity is used, you face password resets across the company, data theft from mailboxes, and often ransomware days later. The blast radius — how far the damage can spread — grows by the hour because valid accounts work everywhere. Most ransomware cases that reach the news began as a phish nobody triaged in time. For the business, this means phishing detection is not a mail problem — it is a money problem.

4.5 Where it fits in the attack chain

Before phishing comes Reconnaissance: gathering employee identities (T1589) and phishing for information (T1598) to test how people respond. Phishing itself is T1566 under Initial Access. What follows is well known. User Execution (T1204) happens when the victim opens the file or link. Valid Accounts (T1078) come next, when the stolen credentials work. Persistence follows, such as Office Application Startup (T1137) or inbox rules. Later come Exfiltration (T1567) and Impact (T1486, ransomware). When you build detections, map each alert to its neighbor in the chain, so a phishing alert automatically raises the score of the next event.

Where threat intel comes from

🌐Open sources (OSINT)

Vendor blogs (Microsoft, Proofpoint, Talos, SentinelOne), CISA and NCSC advisories, abuse.ch URLhaus and ThreatFox for live phishing URLs, AlienVault OTX pulses, VirusTotal for file and domain pivots, and Shodan to find open phishing kits and login panels.

🎯ATT&CK-based intel

The MITRE ATT&CK page for T1566 lists the groups and software documented on this technique: AppleJeus (G1049), Axiom (G0001), GOLD SOUTHFIELD (G0115), INC Ransom (G1032), Kimsuky (G0094), MuddyWater (G0069), Sea Turtle (G1041), VOID MANTICORE (G1055), Hikit (S0009), and Royal (S1073). The sub-technique pages add the heavy users of specific variants, such as APT28 for attachments and APT29 for links.

💳Commercial and sector feeds

Mail security vendors ship campaign and indicator feeds with each block list. Financial-sector ISACs (FS-ISAC) and health ISACs share live phishing kits and sender domains the same morning they appear.

🏠Your own telemetry

Your gateway quarantine, URL click logs, and help desk tickets are the best intel you own. Trend them monthly: which lures reach your staff, which teams click, which domains recur.

The intel cycle for this technique

  1. Define priorities. Decide what matters most this quarter: macro attachments, credential harvesting, MFA abuse, or consent phishing. Base this on your platform mix and past incidents.
  2. Collect. Pull actor reports, indicator feeds, and your own logs. For phishing, collection must include live URL feeds, not only report PDFs.
  3. Process. Normalize indicators: domains, sender IPs, file hashes, kit fingerprints (HTML titles, page structure, GA/Telegram IDs in kit code).
  4. Analyze. Ask which behaviors (not just indicators) define the campaign: container attachments, thread hijacking, MFA fatigue. Behaviors age slower than hashes.
  5. Produce detections and hunts. Turn each behavior into a Sigma rule or a hunt query. Section 5.5 is exactly this step, already done.
  6. Feedback. Track which alerts fired, which were noise, and feed the miss reports back into collection next cycle.

Threat #1 — Macro/VBA-laden Office attachment runs a shell

The victim opens an invoice attachment and clicks "Enable content". The VBA macro inside then starts a command interpreter, usually to download and run a loader. This is the single most reliable endpoint sign of attachment phishing: Word, Excel, PowerPoint, or Outlook almost never launch PowerShell in normal business work. The pattern works because the macro runs with the same user rights as Office, so no exploit is needed — only trust in the document.

Threat #2 — Phishing link to credential harvest, then risky sign-in

The victim clicks a link, sees a convincing Microsoft 365 login page, and types corporate credentials. Minutes later those same credentials are used — often from a hosting provider or an anonymizing network. The identity provider cannot know who typed the password; it can only score the sign-in. That is why the high-risk + password-success combination is the pattern: it is exactly what credential replay looks like from the cloud's point of view.

The attacker calls the service desk, sounds stressed, and claims their phone was stolen. They know a real employee's name from LinkedIn. The help desk ticket is the fraud, so identity logs and the ticket system must be read together. Threat #9 — Thread hijacking / reply-chain phishing

The attacker steals (or buys) a real business conversation, then replies inside it from a look-alike domain, adding a link or a new "signed" attachment. Because the subject says RE: and the text quotes the old thread, filters and people both relax. Krebs on Security documented waves of exactly this in 2024. In message trace data the giveaway is structural. The message is inbound and delivered, and its subject looks like a reply. Threat #10 — High-volume malspam wave

Most phishing is not crafty. A spam engine blasts thousands of addresses with the same lure. Your mail relay sees the storm: many short SMTP connections from one IP or one /24 in a few minutes. Individually these are Low severity — the gateway deletes almost all of them. The wave still matters for two reasons: it hides the one targeted spearphish sent in the same hour, and a wave that suddenly passes filtering means your rules need attention. The sample is Postfix, the most common open-source relay, in standard syslog form.

5.5 Sigma Rules & Splunk SPL

Each threat from 5.3 gets exactly one Sigma rule and one matching Splunk SPL query. The Sigma selections use the same fields as the log samples in 5.4, so you can validate each rule against the sample. Learn from a real incident

Real-World Case Study

September 2023 — MGM Resorts: one phone call, one hundred million dollars

In September 2023, MGM Resorts International — one of the largest casino and hospitality operators in the world — was hit by a ransomware attack that began with a phone call. The crew behind it was Scattered Spider (also tracked as UNC3944), working at that time as an affiliate of the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware operation. The attack is famous for its simplicity: roughly ten minutes of vishing (T1566.004) against the outsourced IT help desk beat a multibillion-dollar security program. MGM disclosed in a US SEC Form 8-K that the incident was expected to reduce EBITDA (a reported profit measure) by about 100 million dollars. Later breach notifications stated that personal data of about 36 million customers was exposed.

The attackers first found the name of a real employee on LinkedIn. They then called the help desk, claimed to be that employee, and said their phone and password no longer worked. The agent reset the credentials and the MFA enrollment. From there, the intruders reached a privileged Okta identity role, created their own federation backdoor, mapped the Windows network, and deployed ransomware on the server estate. MGM's own response — shutting down large parts of its network — limited the damage but closed casinos, hotel systems, and reservations for days.

How the attack chain worked

  1. Recon: pick a visible employee from LinkedIn and learn the help desk's normal routine.
  2. Phishing — T1566.004: one phone call impersonating that employee; the help desk resets password and MFA.
  3. Valid Accounts — T1078: attacker signs in as the victim and reads mail and apps.
  4. Identity escalation: privileged Okta administrative access reached; a rogue federation trust is created as a backdoor.
  5. Discovery: Active Directory mapped with BloodHound-style tooling; service accounts collected.
  6. Lateral movement: access extends to virtualization infrastructure (ESXi).
  7. Impact: ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware deployed; MGM isolates its network; business interruption follows.

How it was detected

The uncomfortable truth is that the initial vishing call was not detected by a tool — it was detected by a human being lied to successfully. Public reporting indicates that monitoring later flagged unusual sign-ins and federation changes, but review and escalation were slow, and the help desk trusted the caller completely. The signals that should have ended the attack within hours were all present. An MFA reset was followed by new device enrollment. There was a sign-in from unfamiliar infrastructure, a new federation trust in the identity platform, and remote access tools installed on servers. Each one maps to a rule in Section 5.5 of this guide.

Containment

  1. Disable and reset the affected identity: passwords, MFA factors, and active sessions for the victim and any touched admin accounts.
  2. Remove the rogue identity federation trust so attacker-issued tokens stop working.
  3. Isolate and shut down affected systems, including virtualization hosts, to stop encryption spread.
  4. Block the remote access tools and the outside connections (egress) the crew used.
  5. Engage external incident response and law enforcement, and begin evidence preservation.

Recovery

  1. Rebuild trust in the identity platform: re-enroll devices, rotate secrets, and verify every federation and admin role.
  2. Restore server workloads from backups after scanning for dormant persistence.
  3. Bring guest-facing systems back in a planned order: casino operations, hotel access, reservations.
  4. Report to regulators and notify affected customers, followed by the SEC 8-K disclosure.

Lessons learned

  • Verification call-backs are mandatory: the help desk must call the employee back on the number stored in HR systems, never the number the caller gives.
  • MFA resets are security events: monitor, alert on, and require dual approval for factor resets, especially for privileged accounts.
  • Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 keys, number matching) removes the "approve the push" weak point that vishing targets.
  • Identity logs need real-time alerting: new federation trusts, new admin roles, and new MFA devices should page someone within minutes.
  • Privileged access should be tiered: a normal employee account must not be a bridge to Okta administration.
  • Practice the worst day: run tabletop exercises (a team walk-through of a fake incident) where a vished help desk starts a ransomware clock.
  • Outsourced help desks inherit your risk: contract for the same verification controls you enforce internally.
What this case teaches about T1566

The entry technique here is T1566.004 — Spearphishing Voice — and the follow-on is T1078 — Valid Accounts. ATT&CK treats voice-based social engineering as its own sub-technique under the phishing technique, so the mapping is exact, even though no email was involved. No malware email, no exploit, no attachment: only a human trust system abused by phone. That is why phishing detection must cover voice channels and identity logs, not just email gateways. The Sigma rules #2 and #4 in this guide are the direct answer to this case.

Layered defenses that work

Mitigation & Defense

Defense in depth works

One control will not stop phishing. Layers do. A layer that fails is caught by the next one. Build them all, then watch them work together.

7.1 Enterprise defenses

Enterprises should treat phishing as an identity problem first and a malware problem second. Modern attacks often carry no malware at all. They steal a login and walk through the front door. These controls cover both paths.

✉️Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC)

Publish all three DNS records and move DMARC to p=reject. This stops most cheap domain spoofing before the message ever reaches a user. Start at p=none with reports, watch the data for a month, then tighten. Monitor the DMARC reports weekly for new senders that abuse your brand.

📨Secure email gateway and sandboxing

Open every attachment in a sandbox before delivery. Rewrite URLs so clicks pass through a checker. Quarantine messages that fail authentication banners. Retest: send yourself a known-bad sample each month to confirm the gateway still catches it.

🔐Phishing-resistant MFA everywhere

Use FIDO2 keys or passkeys for admins and high-risk users. Number matching is the minimum for push approvals. Plain SMS codes are weak and should be retired. For the help desk, require call-back verification to a known number before any password or MFA reset.

🧑‍🏫Realistic user training and reporting

Run simulated phishing every month and keep the difficulty rising. Teach one habit above all: use the report-phishing button. Measure report rate and click rate together. Reward reporters publicly, because every report is an early warning sensor.

🚦Conditional access and device trust

Block sign-ins from unmanaged devices and impossible locations. Require compliant, enrolled devices for mail and cloud consoles. When a risky sign-in is detected, force a password reset and revoke refresh tokens automatically, not after a human review queue.

👀Monitor the identity layer

Alert on inbox rule creation, new MFA method registration, OAuth consent grants, and MFA fatigue patterns (many pushes in an hour). These are the signs after the click. Sections 5.4 and 5.5 give you the exact logs and rules.

🧯Fast revoke and recover playbooks

Write a one-page playbook: suspend the account, kill sessions and tokens, reset MFA, pull mailbox audit logs, search for exfil. Rehearse it twice a year with the help desk, identity team, and SOC in the same call.

🌐Domain and brand watch

Watch for look-alike domains registered against your brand (typosquats) and for your name in fresh phishing feeds. Ask your registrar for a defensive registration. Take down fake domains early, before the campaign lands.

7.2 Home user defenses

Home users are attacked exactly like enterprises, just with fewer safety nets. These simple habits remove most of the risk, and none of them need special tools.

  • Turn on MFA everywhere, and prefer an authenticator app or a passkey over SMS codes.
  • Let your browser and operating system update themselves. Old browsers are easier to trick.
  • Never log in from an email link. Open a new tab and type the address yourself, or use a bookmark.
  • Check the real sender address and the real link target by hovering, not by trusting the display text.
  • Be slow with anything urgent. "Act now or lose access" is the attacker's favorite trick.
  • Use your phone's built-in scanner settings that preview a QR link before opening it.
  • Keep one separate email for money and accounts, and one for newsletters, so noise never hides a real alert.
  • If something feels wrong, stop and verify by phone using a number you already know, never one from the message.

ATT&CK lists these official mitigation objects for phishing: M1017 User Training, M1049 Antivirus/Antimalware, and M1021 Restrict Web-Based Content. Two more are M1031 Network Intrusion Prevention (mail scanning and filtering) and M1047 Audit. M1054 Software Configuration covers email server hardening such as SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. If you can only fund three things: phishing-resistant MFA, DMARC enforcement, and help desk call-backs. Those three break every threat pattern in section 5.3 at its root.

8.2 Best practices for day-to-day SOC work

  1. Run the click playbook every time. User clicked, or you suspect it: pull their sign-ins, MFA registrations, inbox rules, and consent grants for the last 30 days. Ten minutes of checking closes the gap that payload-only triage leaves open.
  2. Build one campaign view. Group phishing alerts by sender infrastructure and subject pattern in your SIEM. Analysts then work one campaign instead of forty tickets, and containment hits every victim at once.
  3. Purple-team your rules monthly. Take the Sigma rules in section 5.5, replay their log events, and confirm each one fires. Fix the silent ones the same day. A rule that never fires is worse than no rule, because it creates false confidence.
  4. Feed intel back into the gateway. When you confirm a campaign, push its domains, senders, and attachment hashes to blocklists and your threat intel platform. Share through your ISAC if you can — the next victim's warning is someone else's log today.
  5. Measure what matters. Track time-to-detect and time-to-revoke for identity compromise, report-to-triage delay, and the share of incidents that started with a click. Show the trend to leadership in plain numbers.
  6. Drill the vishing scenario twice a year. Have a red teamer call your help desk role-playing the MGM pattern. Score the process, fix the gaps, and tell the story inside the SOC so the lesson sticks.
  7. Write it down while it is fresh. Every confirmed phishing incident should end with one updated rule, one updated playbook step, and one training note. Small edits, compounding value.
