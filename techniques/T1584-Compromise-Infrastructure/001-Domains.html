T1584.001Domains

A domain name is one of the strongest trust signals on the internet. This guide explains how attackers take over real domain names — through registrar account takeovers, expired-domain re-registration, or DNS hosting compromise — and turn that borrowed trust into phishing pages, stolen inboxes, and command-and-control traffic. It also shows every place where a defender can see the takeover and stop it.

Overview

This section explains domain hijacking in plain words, why a stolen name is so valuable to an attacker, and where this technique fits in an attack. It also shows the difference between taking over a domain and buying one.

What domain hijacking is

T1584.001 Domains means an attacker gains control of a domain name that already belongs to someone else, usually an organization with a clean history. Three routes are the most common:

  • Registrar account takeover. The attacker steals the login for the account that manages the domain, often through phishing or a reused password.
  • Expired-domain re-registration. The real owner forgets to renew. The attacker buys the name on the day it becomes free again.
  • DNS hosting compromise. The attacker breaks into the DNS provider panel and rewrites the records from inside.

With control of the name, the attacker decides where it points: the website, the mail servers, and every subdomain that trusts it.

Why it matters

A domain name is a trust signal. Your brand domain has years of clean history, so mail filters deliver its email, browsers show no warnings, and users click its links without thinking. An attacker who takes the name inherits all of that trust at once.

This is why domain reputation and mail deliverability are real security assets, not marketing details. One hijacked name can carry phishing into thousands of inboxes, host a perfect copy of your login page, or receive command-and-control (C2) traffic — the hidden channel between malware and its operator.

Where it fits in the attack chain

ATT&CK places this technique in Resource Development, the stage before any victim is touched. The hijacked name then serves several later steps:

  1. T1584.001 Domains
  2. T1566 Phishing (links and fake pages)
  3. T1568 Dynamic Resolution (fast-changing redirectors)
  4. T1071 Application Layer Protocol (C2 channels)
  5. T1041 Exfiltration Over C2 Channel

One hijacked domain can play several roles at the same time: mail sender, phishing host, and C2 redirector.

Compromise vs. acquire (T1583.001)

Do not confuse this technique with T1583.001 Domains under Acquire Infrastructure. There the attacker registers or buys a name — fresh, unknown, and cheap. Here the attacker seizes a name that already exists and belongs to someone else.

Why steal instead of buy? Three reasons. A stolen name costs nothing. It carries age and reputation that mail filters respect. And its real owner adds confusion: takedown requests and legal questions land on the victim first, which slows the response.

    The same story in words

    1. Watch for weak domains. The attacker studies expiring-domain lists and checks which organizations run weak registrar security. The goal is a trusted name, not a new one. SOC view: passive DNS feeds show unusual lookups on brand-like names.
    2. Take over the registrar account. The attacker phishes a domain admin, tricks registrar support, or buys the expired lookalike. Now they control the name. SOC view: a new registrar login appears from an unknown device.
    3. Change nameservers and DNS. The attacker moves the domain to their own nameservers and rewrites the records. SOC view: an NS change with no change ticket — the highest-value alert for this technique.
    4. Point the domain at attacker hosting. The name now resolves to the attacker server, and a fresh TLS certificate for the trusted name appears in Certificate Transparency logs. SOC view: a brand-name certificate your CA never issued.
    5. Send the phishing lure. Mail from the trusted domain reaches inboxes with high deliverability, and each link opens a copy of the real login page. SOC view: mail from your domain with a sending IP you have never seen.
    6. Victim logs in to the fake page. The employee signs in on the fake page, which stores the password and session cookie. SOC view: a login POST to a host with a days-old certificate.
    7. Relay stolen data to the attacker. Passwords and cookies move from the phishing host to the operator. SOC view: the same phishing kit appears on several lookalike domains.
    8. SOC responds and reverts. The SOC confirms the hijack, reverts DNS at the registrar, blocks the attacker addresses, and warns users. SOC view: NS records restored from the audit log and users re-authenticated safely.

    Red team / attacker perspective

    This section explains how attackers choose and take over domain names. Everything here is written for education and defense only. Practice these ideas only on names you own, inside an isolated lab.

    When and why attackers use it

    A hijacked domain is attractive for three concrete reasons:

    • Reputation. A name with years of clean history passes filters that instantly block a domain registered yesterday.
    • Deliverability. Mail from an aged name with proper SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records lands in the inbox. These records prove that a sender is allowed to send mail for the domain, and a hijack hands them to the attacker for free.
    • Takedown resistance. Reporting a fake brand-login.com takes one abuse form. Unwinding a hijack of the real brand name involves registrars, legal teams, and days of waiting — while the attacker keeps sending.

    Attackers also pick names on purpose: expired names of closed partners, rebranded companies, or parked names with old backlinks that still bring visitors.

    How the takeover happens (conceptual)

    • Registrar account takeover: phishing the domain admin, reusing leaked passwords, or abusing weak email-based account recovery at the registrar.
    • Support-desk social engineering: calling the registrar with stolen company details and requesting a "password reset" or a transfer authorization code.
    • Expired-domain re-registration: watching drop-lists for names that expire, then buying them the moment they are released.
    • Subdomain takeover: claiming a dangling CNAME — a subdomain record that still points to a deleted cloud service — and serving content under the trusted parent name.
    • DNS hosting compromise: a stolen DNS provider account or an exposed DNS admin panel gives the same power as the registrar account.

    What each route needs first

    RoutePreconditionsTypical access path
    Registrar account takeoverWeak or absent MFA; password reuse; guessable recovery answersPhishing mail, credential-stuffing with leaked passwords, recovery-mail takeover
    Support social engineeringPublic company details; support desk without strong caller verificationPhone or ticket posing as an employee with "lost access"
    Expired-domain re-registrationOwner forgets renewal; auto-renew off; payment card expiresDrop-list monitoring, then a normal purchase the day the name is released
    Subdomain takeoverDangling CNAME to a removed service (hosting, cloud storage, helpdesk)Scan for dangling records, then claim the name on the target service

    Lab practice that stays legal

    You can experience both sides of this technique safely. Run your own DNS server — for example BIND or dnsmasq — in an isolated lab network. Create a fake "corporate" zone, then play both roles: change its records, move its nameservers, and read every log while you do it. Useful commands against your own lab names only:

    Repeat the exercise and compare the logs of the DNS server, the web server, and the client resolver. The differences you see are exactly the evidence described in section 4.

    Danger. Never touch domains you do not own. Never run dig, whois, takeover tests, or record changes against real domains, registrars, or DNS providers that are not yours. Even a "harmless check" against a name you do not control can be illegal and will not teach you more than your lab does.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    • Variation — quiet mail hijack: leave the website unchanged for weeks and touch only the MX records, so mail flows through the attacker unnoticed.
    • Variation — subdomain-only hijack: take over one forgotten subdomain and leave the parent name untouched to stay under the radar.
    • Mistake — IP reuse: pointing several hijacked names at the same attacker server lets defenders link whole campaigns with one lookup.
    • Mistake — no certificate: a trusted name without a valid TLS certificate scares users, triggers browser warnings, and raises filter scores.
    • Mistake — ignoring mail warmup: sudden bulk mail from a "revived" name burns its reputation within days and gets the name blocklisted.

    Blue team / SOC perspective

    Defenders cannot stop every hijack attempt on the internet, but they can see most successful ones early. This section shows the four visibility sources that matter, the artifacts a hijack leaves behind, and a short triage flow for analysts.

    How defenders can see it

    Domain hijacking is unusual: the victim network often shows nothing. The action happens in four places outside your firewall, so visibility has to be bought deliberately:

    • Registrar audit logs. The registrar records every login and every record change. Ship this feed to your SIEM — it is the single best source for this technique.
    • Passive DNS. Services that record which IP addresses a name resolved to over time. A sudden jump in nameservers or IPs is visible within hours, even years later.
    • Certificate Transparency (CT) logs. Public, append-only records of every TLS certificate issued. A new certificate for your brand, issued by a CA you never use, appears here within minutes.
    • Mail gateway. The first place a mail hijack becomes visible: your brand sending from unknown servers, or DMARC results turning from pass to fail.

    If you only watch inside your perimeter, you will meet this attack when a user clicks. The four sources above let you meet it at the registrar instead.

    Artifacts it leaves and where to look

    ArtifactWhere to lookExample signal
    Nameserver (NS) change on a domain you ownRegistrar audit log, DNS console, passive DNS historyNameservers replaced overnight, no change ticket, actor never seen before
    Fresh certificate for a brand-like nameCT logs (crt.sh, Censys), your own CA dashboardLet's Encrypt certificate for portal-yourcompany.com issued today
    Subdomain takeover of a dangling CNAMEPeriodic dangling-record scans, vendor response pagesSubdomain answers with a "service not found" page from a cloud provider
    MX record change on a domain you ownRegistrar audit log, mail flow dashboard, DMARC reportsMX points to an unknown server; mail bounces or DMARC failures spike

    Why this matters for your organization

    Your brand can be hijacked even if your network is perfectly defended. The attacker does not need a foothold in your company — only in your registrar account. From there they can receive your mail, clone your sites, and speak with your name to customers, partners, and employees.

    The reverse is also true: hijacked other people's domains are used against you every day. Phishing that arrives on a real university or government domain defeats both user training ("check the sender!") and simple filters. Watching external signals protects you in both directions.

    Short triage flow

    1. Alert received — registrar change, CT hit, mail report, or dangling record.
    2. Validate: is the change approved? Check the change ticket and the domain owner first.
    3. Enrich: WHOIS history, passive DNS, CT records, and threat intel for the name and its new IP.
    4. Scope: who received mail from it or visited it in the last 30 days?
    5. Contain: revert records at the registrar, block attacker IPs, sinkhole the name if needed.
    6. Recover and report: re-issue certificates, notify partners, hunt for older abuse of the same name.
    Good practice. Build a small "domain trust" alert list and keep it in the SOC runbook: nameserver or MX changes on owned domains, new CT certificates containing your brand, and DMARC reports from unknown senders. These three cheap alerts catch most hijacks before the first user clicks.

    5.2 · Threat intelligence

    Because domain hijacking lives mostly outside your network, external intelligence does the heavy lifting. A simple pipeline works: collect raw signals, analyze them against your own assets and brand, apply the result to detections and blocks, then review what worked and retire noise.

    Sources worth wiring up

    • MITRE ATT&CK: the T1584.001 page and its citations describe real actor use.
    • Vendor reporting: Cisco Talos, Microsoft, ESET, and Mandiant blogs publish hijack and lookalike campaigns.
    • Passive DNS and WHOIS history: SecurityTrails, RiskIQ, WhoisXML — shows how names moved between owners and servers.
    • Certificate Transparency: crt.sh, Censys, certstream — new certificates for your brand appear here first.
    • Newly-registered-domain (NRD) feeds: daily lists of fresh registrations and drops, ideal for lookalike hunting.
    • ISACs: sector sharing groups (FS-ISAC, H-ISAC, MS-ISAC) push fast, relevant hijack alerts.
    • Internal telemetry: your registrar audit logs, mail gateway reports, and proxy logs — the highest-signal source you own.

    Confidence levels that work

    Tag every indicator with a confidence level and keep the meaning strict:

    • High: confirmed by two independent sources or direct observation — for example your registrar log plus a vendor report.
    • Medium: one reliable source, no second confirmation yet — good for detection and hunting, not for blocking.
    • Low: rumor, paste-site claim, or machine-only correlation — good for watchlists only.

    Write the level into the indicator record, so the analyst who meets it at 03:00 knows how much to trust it and what action is allowed.

    5.5 · Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    The four rules below cover the highest-value threats (TH-01, TH-02, TH-05, TH-06). Each Sigma rule is valid YAML and is followed by a matching Splunk query, a mapping note, false positives, and tuning advice. Replace index and sourcetype names with your local naming — the field assumptions are stated under each query.

    SIG-001 · Registrar audit — unauthorized nameserver or record change (TH-01)

    Mapping note: registrar:audit is the JSON audit feed offered by registrars and DNS providers (Route 53, Cloudflare, GoDaddy all provide one). Field names assume the JSON keys are extracted as search-time fields; use spath or a props.conf stanza if they arrive nested. False positives: unticketed emergency changes. Tuning: allowlist the provider automation actors exactly, keep the change-ticket field mandatory in your process, and pair this rule with a CT watch so a confirmed NS change and a fresh brand certificate always correlate into one high-priority case.

    SIG-002 · Client DNS query to a lookalike domain (TH-02)

    Mapping note: assumes Sysmon Event ID 22 ingested via the Splunk Windows add-on, where the DNS query name lands in QueryName. Replace yourcompany with your brand token and extend filter_real with every TLD you actually own. False positives: legitimate domains that contain the brand word. Tuning: keep a reviewed allowlist of partner names, add known campaign lookalikes from threat intel as QueryName matches for faster, more precise hits, and correlate a hit with the next browser POST from the same host.

    SIG-003 · Web server hit by a lookalike Host header or unexpected path (TH-03, TH-05)

    Mapping note: assumes Splunk IIS field extractions (cs_host, cs_uri_stem, sc_status, c_ip). The lookalike-host half catches traffic aimed at imitator names that still resolves to your IP; the unexpected-path half catches phishing-kit probing before a hijack completes. False positives: scanners and legacy apps, as listed. Tuning: build a baseline of rare paths over 30 days and replace the fixed path list with it; add every lookalike name from your CT and NRD watchlists to the host match so the rule grows with the campaign.

    SIG-004 · First interaction with a newly registered domain (TH-04, TH-06)

    Mapping note: the rule expects an enrichment pipeline that joins proxy and mail logs with an NRD feed and writes the age into nrd_days; in Splunk this is one lookup against a daily-updated KV store. For mail gateways, run the same logic with the sender domain as url_domain. False positives: fresh legitimate marketing and vendor domains, as listed. Tuning: keep a reviewed allowlist, adjust the age window (7 days for mail, 30 for browsing), and raise the priority when the fresh domain also contains a brand token — that combination is the classic lookalike launch.

    Real-world case study — Operation Dream Job (Lazarus Group)

    Operation Dream Job is the name researchers gave a long Lazarus Group campaign built on fake recruiters and, crucially for this page, compromised real domains used as C2 infrastructure. ATT&CK records it as campaign C0022 and lists it as a procedure under T1584.001: Lazarus "compromised domains in Italy and other countries for their C2 infrastructure."

    What happened, step by step

    Lazarus operators posed as headhunters on professional networks. They talked to employees in aerospace, defense, and media, built trust over days, and then sent a "job description" file. Opening it installed a backdoor. What made this campaign special was the next hop: instead of only fresh attacker domains, the implants reached back to command-and-control servers riding on legitimate domains that Lazarus had taken over — trusted names with years of clean history.

    • 2020 — approach

      Fake recruiters contact targeted employees. The persona is professional and patient; conversations move to private messaging or personal email, away from corporate monitoring.

    • Days later — the lure

      The victim receives a job description — often a stylized HTML file. Opening it runs a small installer, and a backdoor lands on the workstation.

    • Same week — trusted C2

      The implant beacons over HTTPS to attacker infrastructure that includes compromised legitimate domains in Italy and other countries (per ATT&CK's T1584.001 procedure for C0022). To mail filters and proxies, the traffic points at names with clean reputations.

    • Following weeks — tasking

      Operators issue commands, expand access, and collect documents of defense and policy interest. The hijacked domains rotate slowly, which keeps the beacon pattern stable.

    • 2021–2022 — exposure

      Researchers publish analyses of the recruiter lures and the C2 chains. ATT&CK records the activity as campaign C0022 with the domain-compromise procedure attached.

    The attack chain

    1. Fake recruiter builds trust
    2. HTML "job description" runs an installer
    3. Backdoor installed on the workstation
    4. Beacon to a compromised legitimate domain (T1584.001)
    5. Commands and stolen files blend into trusted-looking traffic

    The technique placement matters: the domain hijack happened in Resource Development, before any single victim was touched. One hijacked name then served many intrusions, which is exactly why the technique is worth watching as its own problem.

    Detection point

    The strongest general signal was reputation decay: an aged domain that suddenly changed content, name servers, or certificate. Inside a network, the beacons were ordinary HTTPS — so egress monitoring that compares the destination's age and history against its recent behavior is what catches this class. New certificates on old domains (visible in Certificate Transparency) and newly registered-neighbor domains for your brand are the early-warning cousins of that check.

    Response arc

    • Containment: block the C2 indicators at the proxy and firewall; report the hijacked domains to the registrar for takedown or sinkholing; isolate hosts that beaconed.
    • Eradication: remove implants, reset credentials exposed on those hosts, and sweep for other beacons using the same infrastructure.
    • Recovery: return hosts from clean images, add the indicators to long-term watchlists, and tighten egress rules so a repeat beacon cannot phone home quietly.

    Impact and communication

    The campaign stole sensitive defense- and policy-related material from targeted people, and it worked because the C2 rode on names nobody wanted to block. For the domain owners, the impact was indirect but real: their names appeared in threat reports, and their mail and web traffic shared infrastructure with espionage activity. Researchers coordinated disclosure through vendor reports rather than public shaming of the domain owners, which kept the focus on tradecraft.

    Lessons learned

    • Reputation is an asset attackers steal. An aged, clean domain is worth more to an attacker than a fresh one — watch old domains the way you watch new ones.
    • Watch changes, not just names. New certificates, new name servers, or new content on an old domain is a hijack signature.
    • Recruiter lures target people, not software. Train staff on job-offer lures that arrive outside corporate channels.
    • Egress context beats blocklists. Knowing a destination's age, owner, and history turns a silent beacon into a loud alert.

    Mitigation

    ATT&CK lists M1056 Pre-compromise for this technique: you cannot stop a hijack attempt with a firewall, because it happens in someone else's account. But you can make your domains extremely hard to take, and make any takeover loud and short.

    Lock down the registrar

    Hardware-key MFA for every registrar account, one account per admin (no shared logins), and two locks on each domain: a registry lock, which freezes changes at the registry level until you confirm out-of-band, and a transfer lock, which blocks moving the name to another registrar.

    Control DNS changes

    Every record change needs a ticket and a second approval for NS and MX records. Restrict registrar logins to known devices and networks, and turn on registrar notifications so every change sends an alert to a group mailbox, not one person.

    Monitor Certificate Transparency

    Stream CT logs and alert on any new certificate that contains your brand or a lookalike pattern. Free tooling makes this a weekend project, and it catches phishing infrastructure before the first mail is sent.

    Keep passive DNS history

    Record where your domains pointed over time, either with a service or by logging resolver data. Sudden nameserver or IP changes surface fast, and historic answers let you scope a hijack backwards for months.

    Watch egress and client DNS

    Log client DNS queries and egress destinations, and alert on lookalike names and very young domains. Keep server destination allowlists small — servers that talk to only five names make a sixth one jump out.

    Prepare the response runbook

    Write down the registrar escalation contact, the revert-DNS steps, certificate re-issuance, blocking actions, and communication templates. Rehearse the hijack scenario twice a year with the real people who would answer the phone.

    Home and small-office protection

    Small organizations and individuals are hijack targets too, because they rarely watch anything. A short list covers most of the risk: turn on auto-renew for every domain, enable the strongest MFA the registrar offers, add the transfer lock if available, and keep the account recovery email on a mailbox you also protect with MFA. Check after any provider change that the website and mail still work as expected — a broken MX record is how many real hijacks are found by accident.

    Cheat sheet

    The one-screen summary: key facts, the four detections that carry the most weight, the response checklist, and the links to read next. Print this section for the SOC wall.

    ATT&CK ID
    T1584.001 — Domains · Resource Development · Platform PRE · Created 1 Oct 2020 (ATT&CK v8) · Mitigation M1056

    Response checklist

    1. Confirm change tickets and domain owners before touching anything.
    2. Revert NS and DNS records at the registrar from a known-good device.
    3. Block attacker nameservers and hosting IPs at the proxy and firewall.
    4. Re-issue TLS certificates and watch CT for duplicates.
    5. Force password resets for accounts exposed during the hijack window.
    6. Hunt 90 days back for mail and traffic tied to the name.
    7. Notify partners and update internal and shared blocklists.
