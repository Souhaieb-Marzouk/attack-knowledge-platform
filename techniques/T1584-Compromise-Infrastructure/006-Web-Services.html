T1584.006Web Services

Attackers do not always rent their own servers. They also take over accounts and sites that real people already own on popular web services such as GitHub, Dropbox, Google, SendGrid, or a WordPress blog. This guide shows how that hijack works, why the traffic is so hard to block, and what defenders can see at every step.

Overview

This section explains the technique in plain words, introduces the key term dead-drop resolver, and shows where it sits in an attack. It also contrasts this technique with its sibling T1583.006, where the attacker registers a fresh account instead of stealing one.

What the technique is

T1584.006 Web Services means an attacker takes over a legitimate user's account — or a vulnerable website — on a popular web service, and then abuses that trusted spot as attack infrastructure. Think of a developer's account on a code host like GitHub, a cloud drive such as Dropbox or Google Drive, a bulk mail sender like SendGrid, or a small company website built on a content management system (CMS).

A CMS is software like WordPress that runs millions of websites. When attackers break into one, they get hosting space on a domain that has been trusted for years. The service itself is never broken — the attacker simply misuses the trust that everyone places in it.

Why it matters to defenders

The domains of big providers sit on almost every allowlist, their traffic is TLS-encrypted, and their volume is enormous. A malware beacon that checks a paste site every 60 seconds hides inside that noise. If you block the provider's domain, you break normal business tools at the same time.

There is one strong counter-move: reporting abuse to the provider, which can suspend the hijacked account. But that takes time, and the attacker can open the next account. Detection must therefore watch behavior — who talks to the service, how often, and from which process — not just domain names.

The key term: dead-drop resolver

A dead-drop resolver borrows a trick from spy craft: two agents never meet, they leave messages in a secret spot. Here the spot is a file on a trusted web service. The attacker posts an encoded "task" file, for example as a gist or paste. Malware on the victim's machine reads that file over HTTPS, runs the commands inside, and posts the encoded results back.

Neither side ever connects directly to the other, and every message travels to and from a domain nobody wants to block. The same hijacked account can also host malware downloads (file staging), send phishing links, or receive stolen data.

Where it fits in the attack chain

ATT&CK places this technique in Resource Development, before the victim is touched. The hijacked account then supports many later techniques:

  1. T1584.006 Compromise Web Service
  2. T1566 Phishing (links from a trusted account)
  3. T1102 Web Service (C2 and tasking)
  4. T1102.002 Bidirectional Communication
  5. T1567.002 Exfiltration to Cloud Storage

One hijacked account can play several roles at once: tasking board, malware shelf, phishing springboard, and data mailbox.

Compromise access (T1584.006)

In this technique the attacker steals access: session cookies, passwords, API tokens of a real user, or full control of a vulnerable CMS site. The account has history, followers, and reputation from day one.

Detection angle: the real owner is your ally. Odd logins, strange file activity, and abuse reports stand out because the account's normal pattern is known. The provider can suspend the account once alerted.

Register own account (T1583.006)

In the sibling technique T1583.006 Acquire Infrastructure: Web Services, the attacker simply registers a fresh account — often with a burner email — and uses it the same way. No victim is robbed, so no owner will complain.

Detection angle: the account is young. Age, thin history, and odd registration details are the signals. In both cases your egress-side detections look the same, which is why Section 5 covers both.

    The same story in words

    1. Steal an account. The attacker takes the session cookie or password of a developer account on a popular code-hosting service. SOC view: the SaaS audit log shows a new session from an unknown device or country.
    2. Post the task file. The attacker uploads an encoded "task" file as a gist or paste — the dead drop. SOC view: a normally quiet account creates a public file at an odd hour.
    3. Malware polls the service. Malware on the infected workstation checks the service over HTTPS every 60 seconds. SOC view: egress logs show one identity touching one popular domain at a metronome rate.
    4. Service returns tasking. The service answers with the task file inside normal TLS traffic. SOC view: small downloads on a fixed cadence from a trusted provider.
    5. Malware executes. The malware decodes the file, runs the commands, and encodes the results. SOC view: a download is followed at once by a script or download utility process.
    6. Results uploaded. The encoded results go back to the service through the same account. SOC view: an encoded blob is pushed to a paste or file path on a regular interval.
    7. Attacker reads results. The attacker logs into the hijacked account and reads the answers. SOC view: the account logs in again and downloads the files the malware touched.
    8. SOC responds. The SOC links the odd cadence and TLS pattern into one case, blocks the account endpoints for that identity, and files an abuse report so the provider suspends the hijacked account. SOC view: the C2 path is cut without blocking the whole provider.

    Red team / hacker perspective

    This section explains how attackers think when they abuse popular web services. Everything here is described for education and defense only. Practice these ideas exclusively in your own isolated lab, against your own test endpoints.

    Why attackers pick web services

    • Trusted: the domain is real, old, and on every allowlist. Filters that stop fresh attacker domains wave this traffic through.
    • Encrypted: TLS hides the content from most inspection points, so commands and stolen data travel unseen.
    • Free: a stolen session cookie costs nothing, and a hijacked CMS site is a free hosting slot.
    • Resilient to blocking: blocking the provider's domain breaks normal business, so defenders hesitate — exactly what the attacker wants.

    How the takeover happens (conceptual)

    • Session theft: malware or a phishing page copies the session cookie — the small file that keeps a user logged in — so the attacker reuses the login without the password.
    • Credential stuffing: replaying passwords stolen in old data breaches, which works wherever people reused passwords.
    • Token and MFA fatigue: pushing approval requests until a tired user accepts, then grabbing the session token.
    • CMS takeover: an outdated plugin or a weak admin password on a WordPress site turns an innocent domain into a malware shelf.

    Where it applies and what it needs

    Attacker goalTypical servicePreconditions
    C2 dead-drop resolver (tasking by file)Code host gists, paste sitesAccount access; malware knows the file URL
    Payload hosting (malware downloads)Compromised CMS sites, cloud drivesCMS foothold or storage account with public links
    Exfil drop (stolen data collection)Cloud storage, file-sharing servicesWrite access to a folder the attacker can read later
    Phishing senderHijacked SaaS or mail accountWorking session; the mail passes SPF because it is genuinely from the provider

    Lab practice that stays legal

    You can safely feel both sides of this technique offline. Simulate the polling channel with curl against your own test endpoint in a lab — for example a local file server or a private paste clone — never against a real provider:

    1. Start a local web server and place an encoded "task" file on it.
    2. From a lab VM, poll it on a fixed cadence: curl -s http://lab.local/task.txt every 60 seconds.
    3. Log both sides: server access log plus a proxy in front of the VM.
    4. Now play SOC: find the cadence, the odd user-agent, and the fetch-then-run pattern in your own logs.
    Danger. Do not create real malicious accounts on real services, and do not task real malware. Testing against real providers without written approval violates their terms and the law in many countries.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    • Variation — many drops: a new file URL per campaign, so blocking one link catches nothing else.
    • Variation — mixed traffic: the malware reads the same account the owner really uses, hiding in genuine activity.
    • Mistake — predictable file names: names like tasks.txt or fixed date patterns are easy hunt targets.
    • Mistake — fixed polling cadence: a metronome every 60 seconds is trivially visible in timing analysis.
    • Mistake — reusing accounts: one hijacked account across many campaigns links them all together for investigators.

    Blue team / SOC perspective

    You cannot stop the hijack happening to someone else's account, but you can see your side of the channel very clearly. This section shows the telemetry that makes web-service abuse visible, the artifacts it leaves, and a short triage flow for analysts.

    How defenders can see it

    • Proxy and egress logs with TLS metadata: who in your network talks to which provider, how often, and how much data moves. TLS metadata describes the handshake without reading content.
    • JA3 and JA3S fingerprints: JA3 is a short hash of how a client says hello during TLS setup; JA3S is the same for the server's reply. Malware libraries often use rare TLS settings, so their fingerprint stands out even when the domain is trusted.
    • User-agent analytics: the user-agent is the text string a client sends to say which program it is, like a browser name and version. A server process claiming to be "Mozilla/5.0" every 60 seconds is a classic tell.
    • EDR network events: endpoint agents (for example Sysmon Event ID 3 for connections and Event ID 22 for DNS queries) show which process made the request — a browser is normal, certutil.exe is not.
    • SaaS audit logs: if you run GitHub Enterprise, Google Workspace, or similar, their audit logs show new logins, new files, and downloads on your tenants.
    • Vendor abuse reporting: reporting a hijacked account or malicious file to the provider gets it suspended — a real containment action that costs nothing.

    Artifacts it leaves and where to look

    ArtifactWhere to lookExample signal
    Regular polling to a popular domain with an odd user-agentProxy / egress logsWorkstation or server identity GETs a gist every 60 s with a static, non-browser UA
    Large encoded blobs in URLs or uploadsProxy logs, DLPBase64-looking path or body sent to a paste or file-hosting path
    New gist or paste access patterns from serversProxy logs, SaaS audit logsServer identity downloads a public file that no human ever opened
    New logins and file activity on a SaaS accountSaaS audit log (e.g., GitHub audit log)New device and country, new public file created at 03:00 local time

    Why this matters for your organization

    Web-service abuse defeats your two favorite controls. Domain blocklists cannot touch github.com or dropbox.com, and user awareness cannot help when the phishing mail really comes from a colleague's real mailbox. That means detection must move to behavior: timing, fingerprints, processes, and account history. The upside: this telemetry is cheap, and the provider is a willing partner once you report abuse.

    Short triage flow

    1. Alert received (cadence to a provider, odd UA, JA3 hit, SaaS audit anomaly).
    2. Validate: is this identity allowed to use that service? Check the asset and account owner.
    3. Enrich: JA3, user-agent, URL path, threat intel for the exact file or account.
    4. Scope: which other hosts show the same fingerprint, path, or cadence?
    5. Contain: block by process and identity, isolate the host, report the account to the provider.
    6. Hunt & report: search 90 days for the same indicators and file the case.
    Good practice. Keep two lists apart: an allowlist of which identities may use which provider, and behavior analytics for everyone else. "First-seen process downloads from provider X" is a simple alert that catches most of this technique.

    5.2 · Threat intelligence

    Web-service abuse is a favorite of many groups, so external intelligence finds campaigns you cannot see from inside. A simple pipeline works: collect signals, analyze them against your assets, apply the result to detections and reports, then review what worked.

    Sources worth wiring up

    • Vendor reporting: Microsoft MSTIC and Mandiant blogs describe campaigns with exact service paths and indicators.
    • urlscan.io: scan and look up URLs to see screenshots, hosting details, and who else fetched the same file.
    • abuse.ch: URLhaus and MalwareBazaar track malware download URLs and samples, many hosted on web services.
    • Provider security reports: GitHub Security Lab posts and provider transparency reports describe abuse patterns and takedowns.
    • ISACs: sector sharing groups push fast, relevant alerts about hijacked accounts and malicious files in your sector.
    • Internal telemetry: your own SaaS audit logs and proxy logs — the highest-signal source you own.

    Confidence levels that work

    Tag every indicator with a confidence level and keep the meaning strict:

    • High: confirmed by two independent sources or direct observation (your logs plus a vendor report).
    • Medium: one reliable source, no second confirmation — good for detection, not for blocking.
    • Low: rumor, paste-site claim, or machine-only correlation — good for hunting lists only.

    Write the level into the indicator record so the analyst who meets it at 03:00 knows how much to trust it — and whether to block or only watch.

    5.5 · Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    The four rules below cover the highest-value threats (TH-01, TH-02, TH-03, TH-04). Each Sigma rule is valid YAML and is followed by a matching Splunk query, a mapping note, and tuning advice. Replace index and sourcetype names with your local naming — the field assumptions are stated under each query.

    SIG-001 · Server identity beaconing to a code or paste host (TH-01)

    Mapping note: assumes a Squid-style proxy log with parsed url_domain and user fields (use cs_username for Blue Coat style logs). The 5-minute bucket approximates "fixed cadence". False positives: build servers fetching from GitHub. Tuning: keep an allowlist of CI identities and their expected domains; treat any other server identity as a finding and check byte patterns — C2 is small and regular, downloads are larger and varied.

    SIG-002 · Unusual process connects to a paste or code host (TH-01/TH-03)

    Mapping note: uses Sysmon Event ID 3 (network connection) with host-name enrichment from Sysmon DNS events (EID 22). False positives: approved admin fetch scripts. Tuning: allowlist known script paths and their exact destination files; alert on the pair (process, host) rather than on the domain alone, so a browser-like UA on raw.githubusercontent.com from powershell.exe still fires.

    SIG-003 · Download utility fetches a web-service payload path (TH-02)

    Mapping note: uses Sysmon Event ID 1 (process creation). False positives: documented installers. Tuning: capture the full command line and hash the fetched file later; if a hit repeats from many hosts, pivot to the exact URL in your proxy logs and report the account to the provider.

    SIG-004 · DNS query with random label to a hosting service (TH-03/TH-06)

    Mapping note: uses Sysmon Event ID 22 (DNS query) with Image to name the asking process. False positives: content-addressed developer subdomains. Tuning: raise the label length if your developers use hash-style names, and require at least two hits per host to cut one-off noise. Keep this rule in test status until you know your baseline.

    Warning. Validate every rule in a test index before production, and record the validation result in the rule's comments. A rule that has never fired in a test is a guess, not a detection.

    Real-world case study — Nobelium and the GitHub C2 channel (2021)

    After the SolarWinds supply-chain attack made its server infrastructure famous, the group that Microsoft tracks as Nobelium (also known as APT29 or Cozy Bear) changed its habits. Microsoft's Microsoft Threat Intelligence Center (MSTIC) reported in 2021 that Nobelium moved parts of its operations onto legitimate cloud services — including GitHub, which carried tasking for the backdoor Microsoft calls NativeZone. It is a perfect teaching case for T1584.006.

    Context

    Nobelium is a well-resourced espionage group. In December 2020 the SolarWinds campaign exposed many of its servers, and defenders worldwide published blocklists of them. Instead of renting new look-alike servers, the group pivoted: commands and tools moved to accounts and files on popular providers, where a domain blocklist is useless. The malware — including the NativeZone backdoor — fetched its instructions from a trusted code host over ordinary HTTPS.

    Attack chain in short

    1. Steal or take over developer and staff accounts on cloud services.
    2. Stage encoded task files and tools on the trusted provider.
    3. NativeZone malware polls the files on a fixed cadence over HTTPS.
    4. Execute the commands, encode the results, upload them back.
    5. Attacker reads the results through the same account — no direct connection ever exists.

    Timeline

    1. December 2020
      SolarWinds disclosed. The supply-chain campaign is named, and Microsoft begins tracking the actor as Nobelium. Defenders publish its known servers on blocklists within days.
    2. Early 2021
      Pivot to legitimate services. Nobelium shifts activity onto accounts and files at popular providers. Tasking for the NativeZone backdoor moves to GitHub, riding on the provider's trust and encryption.
    3. May 2021
      Microsoft MSTIC reporting. Microsoft describes Nobelium's continued campaigns and the use of legitimate services — with the NativeZone backdoor reaching GitHub for C2 — and publishes hunting guidance for defenders.
    4. Mid 2021 onward
      Mitigations spread. SOCs add behavior analytics instead of domain blocks, providers suspend abused accounts after reports, and phishing-resistant MFA rolls out for the targeted identity types.

    Detection point

    The channel became visible through odd TLS and user-agent patterns plus process-network correlation. Malware fetching task files used TLS settings and user-agent strings that real browsers and sync clients do not use, and it polled at a fixed cadence. On the endpoint, the process that fetched the file was a scripting or download utility — not a browser — and it was followed within seconds by execution activity. Each signal alone was weak; together they named the channel.

    Response arc

    • Containment: block by process and identity rather than by domain, and report the abused accounts to the providers so they suspend them.
    • Eradication: hunt for NativeZone implants and stealers, revoke stolen sessions and tokens, and force re-authentication.
    • Recovery: restore normal egress with the new analytics in place, and keep monitoring the same providers for the next account.

    Impact and team response

    The campaign showed how far a patient group can go with borrowed trust: tasking survived inside traffic that looked like ordinary developer activity, and no blocklist could stop it without breaking the business. Response demanded cooperation across teams — identity owners, network engineers, detection engineers, and the providers themselves — because the fix lived in four places at once: accounts, endpoints, egress, and the vendor's abuse desk.

    Lessons learned

    • Blocklists by domain fail against trusted services; behavior is the signal that matters.
    • Fixed cadence and odd fingerprints are cheap, high-value detections.
    • SaaS audit logs are detection gold — ingest them before you need them.
    • Abuse reporting is a real containment tool; build the playbook before the incident.
    • Assume the attacker's key is a credential, not malware — protect sessions like assets.

    Mitigation and defense

    ATT&CK lists only M1056 Pre-compromise for this technique, which honestly says: you cannot fully prevent the hijack of someone else's account from inside your network. What you can do is make your own accounts hard to steal, see the abused channel quickly, and limit what it can do to you.

    Enterprise controls by layer

    Network & egress

    • Egress policy for servers: server identities get small allowlists; a build server rarely needs a paste site.
    • TLS inspection where lawful: check local law and policy first; inspect only the zones where it is allowed and disclosed, and log the decision.
    • JA3/JA3S baselining: learn the normal TLS fingerprints per identity and subnet, so a malware library stands out.

    Identity & SaaS

    • MFA on SaaS accounts: prefer phishing-resistant methods (security keys) for developer and admin accounts.
    • Fast session revocation: a tested runbook to kill sessions and tokens in minutes, not days.
    • Monitor SaaS audit logs: ship GitHub, Google Workspace, and similar audit logs into the SIEM and alert on new devices, new files, and bulk downloads.

    Endpoint

    • Keep EDR network events on (Sysmon EID 3 and 22 or vendor equivalents) so every connection has a process name.
    • Watch fetch-then-run patterns: a download followed at once by a script or download utility.
    • Restrict living-off-the-land tools like certutil.exe with application control where possible.

    Detection & response

    • Abuse-report playbooks: know in advance who reports a malicious file or account to GitHub, Dropbox, or Google, and how.
    • Browser isolation for risky fetches so suspicious links open in a disposable environment.
    • Run the Section 5.5 detections and review them quarterly.

    Home and small office

    • Turn on 2FA for every account you own on GitHub, Dropbox, Google, or your mail provider — an authenticator app is better than SMS.
    • Use unique passwords per service, stored in a password manager, so one breach cannot be replayed elsewhere.
    • Review active sessions monthly and sign out devices you do not recognize.
    • If your WordPress site is compromised: change passwords, update the CMS and plugins, restore a clean backup, and report it to your host.

    Response checklist — suspected web-service abuse

    • Identify the process, identity, and account behind the traffic (proxy + EDR + SaaS audit log).
    • Pull the exact URL or file and enrich it (urlscan.io, abuse.ch, vendor reports).
    • Block narrowly: process and identity first, never the whole provider domain.
    • Isolate affected hosts and preserve the fetched files and command lines.
    • Report the file or account to the provider's abuse desk and track the suspension.
    • If a SaaS account was hijacked: revoke sessions and tokens, force re-auth, review what it read.
    • Scope: search 90 days of logs for the same path, fingerprint, and cadence.
    • File the incident record with timeline, evidence links, and indicator list.
