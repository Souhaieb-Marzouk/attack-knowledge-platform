T1584.005Botnet

A botnet is a network of infected devices that one attacker controls from afar. This technique covers the moment the attacker builds that network: they hack thousands of cameras, routers, and computers, or take over a botnet somebody else built. Those stolen devices then send spam, flood websites, and hide the attacker's real location.

Overview

This section explains what a botnet is in plain words, what T1584.005 adds to the picture, and how building a botnet differs from renting one. It also shows where the botnet sits in an attack.

What the technique is

T1584.005 Botnet means the attacker creates their own botnet. A botnet ("robot network") is a large group of devices infected with malware. Each infected device is called a bot or zombie. All bots listen to one attacker, who sends them commands through a C2 channel (command and control, the attacker's communication line to the malware).

The attacker gets the bots by hacking devices that belong to other people: home routers, IP cameras, digital video recorders, servers, and ordinary computers. Another way is to take over an existing botnet, for example by registering its expired C2 domain. Either way, the attacker now commands a private device army they got for almost free.

Why it matters to defenders

A botnet gives the attacker three things at once: volume (thousands of machines act together), spread (traffic comes from many homes in many countries, so one blocklist entry does not stop it), and anonymity (victims see home IP addresses, not the attacker's server).

This matters twice for you. First, your organization can be the target of a flood or a spam wave. Second, your own printers, cameras, and laptops can quietly join somebody else's botnet. Both directions need monitoring.

What botnets are used for

Botnets are general-purpose attack capacity. The same network of infected devices can switch jobs in minutes:

JobHow it worksWho feels it
DDoSAll bots send traffic to one target at the same time until the service falls over. DDoS means distributed denial of service: too much traffic, from too many places, for one server to answer.The flooded website or service
SpamBots send millions of phishing and advertising emails. Mail from many home IP addresses passes filters more easily than mail from one known spam server.Mail recipients and mail providers
ProxyingBots forward the attacker's web traffic. Attacks then seem to come from normal homes, and the attacker hides behind innocent devices.Everyone the attacker attacks next
Credential stuffingBots try huge lists of stolen username and password pairs on login pages. Spreading the tries over many IPs avoids rate limits. Rate limit = a cap on how many tries one address may make.Login pages and their users
Click fraudBots quietly click paid ads to earn the attacker advertising money.Advertisers

Where it fits in the attack chain

ATT&CK calls this stage Resource Development. It happens before a chosen victim is involved. The finished botnet then supports many later techniques:

  1. T1584.005 Botnet
  2. T1566 Phishing (spam waves)
  3. T1498 Network DoS (floods)
  4. T1110 Brute Force (stuffing)
  5. T1090 Proxy (hiding origin)

One botnet can do several of these jobs in the same week, and the bots themselves are the evidence — spread over thousands of networks.

Build (T1584.005) vs. rent (T1583.005)

The same attack capacity can be built or bought. ATT&CK splits these into two techniques:

  • T1584.005 (this page): the attacker infects devices themselves, or hijacks an existing botnet. Higher effort, but full control and no fees.
  • T1583.005: the attacker rents ready-made capacity — a booter or stresser service. A booter is a paid DDoS-for-hire website: enter a target, click a button, pay a few dollars.

Renting is fast but shared: the same booter is used by many customers, its capacity is known, and its operators can be arrested or scam their own customers. Building is slower but private and reusable. Skilled groups often do both.

    The same story in words

    1. Scan for weak devices. The attacker sweeps the internet for cameras and routers that still use factory default passwords. SOC view: a wide, slow scan wave across Telnet and SSH ports shows up on perimeter firewalls.
    2. Infect the devices. Bot malware is pushed onto every device that answers with a default login. SOC view: an unknown binary appears on a managed device and starts new processes.
    3. Bots register with C2. Each new bot "checks in" to the C2 channel so the attacker knows the army size. SOC view: many devices reach one fresh domain at the same interval.
    4. Attacker issues a command. A target list and an order travel through the C2 channel to every bot. SOC view: unusual repeated downloads from one host on infected machines.
    5. The flood starts. All bots send traffic to the target service at once — a DDoS attack. SOC view: traffic graphs go vertical; thousands of unique source IPs hit one port.
    6. Spam and stuffing batches. Between floods, the same bots send spam and try stolen passwords on login pages. SOC view: login failures spike from many residential IPs.
    7. The SOC responds. The victim SOC sees the volumetric anomaly and engages a scrubbing service that filters the flood upstream. SOC view: clean traffic returns while the scrubbing center absorbs the junk.
    8. Takedown and cleanup. The C2 domain is sinkholed and ISPs start cleaning the infected devices. SOC view: beacons now hit a sinkhole the defender controls; the botnet shrinks.

    Red team / hacker perspective

    This section explains how attackers think when they build a botnet. Everything here is described for education and defense only. Practice these ideas exclusively in your own isolated lab.

    When and why attackers build their own

    A botnet is an investment. Scanning and infecting thousands of devices takes days or weeks, so attackers build one when they plan to reuse it. Four reasons come up again and again:

    • Control: the attacker decides when to flood, when to spam, and when to rest the army. No rental schedule, no shared capacity.
    • Persistence: a private botnet keeps working between campaigns. It can grow quietly for months before it is used.
    • No rental fees: booter subscriptions cost money and leave payment records. Infected devices cost nothing but scan time.
    • Resale: some operators build a botnet, run their own attacks, and then rent or sell parts of it to others — which turns T1584.005 into a business.

    How it is done (conceptually)

    Botnet building follows a repeatable pattern. Knowing the pattern is what makes it detectable:

    1. Scan: sweep the internet for devices with default passwords (Telnet on port 23, Telnet on 2323, SSH on 22) or old, unpatched firmware.
    2. Infect: log in with the default credentials or exploit the firmware bug, then download the bot malware. Many families also copy themselves to the devices the first bot can reach — worm propagation, spreading without the attacker's help.
    3. Connect: each bot registers with the C2 channel so the operator can count and command the bots.
    4. Take over (variant): instead of infecting devices, an attacker hijacks an existing botnet — for example by registering the C2 domain after it expires, or by using a flaw in the bot protocol to seize the channel.

    Where this shows up

    Built botnets power several criminal service areas:

    • DDoS-for-hire backends: some booter sites run on botnets the operators built themselves, which gives them capacity no hosting provider can seize.
    • Spam infrastructure: spam campaigns need many sending addresses with clean reputations — exactly what residential bots provide.
    • Residential proxy networks: some services route buyer traffic through infected home devices. The user of the proxy may never know the devices are infected.
    • Click fraud farms: bots click ads in ways that mimic human visitors.

    What an attacker needs first

    Prerequisites tell you where to look for early signals:

    • A scan tool or masscan-style sweeper and lists of device types to target.
    • A bot malware build with a working C2 address baked in (or a DGA to find it — see below).
    • A C2 channel: a domain, a set of servers, or a peer-to-peer protocol.
    • For takeover: knowledge of the victim botnet's protocol, or access to its expired or leaked C2 domain.

    Every one of these leaves traces: scan waves, fresh domains, beacon traffic, and protocol fingerprints.

    LAB ONLY. You may study botnets safely only inside an isolated lab. A safe setup: three virtual machines with a deliberately weak service acting as "bots", one VM as the local C2, and a monitoring VM capturing all netflow. Infect nothing outside the lab segment, use only your own test devices, and keep the lab network unplugged from production. The learning goal is defensive: observe what the beacon and command traffic looks like in your own netflow and PCAP captures, then write detections from it. Attacking real devices you do not own is a crime in most countries.

    Variations red teamers discuss

    • Peer-to-peer (P2P) C2: bots talk to each other instead of one server, so there is no single C2 to sinkhole. Detection shifts to protocol fingerprinting and peer-list exchange traffic.
    • DGA domains: a DGA (domain generation algorithm) is an algorithm that creates long lists of random-looking domain names, for example qwezxvbn-nightfall.com. The bot computes the same list, tries the names until one answers, and the attacker registers one name per day. This defeats static blocklists.
    • Living-off-the-land bots: instead of new malware, the bot uses tools already on the device, which makes endpoint detection much harder.

    Mistakes bot builders make

    • Loud scanning: sweeping the whole internet is visible to every network telescope and darknet sensor.
    • Hardcoded C2: one domain or IP baked into the malware — sinkhole it once and the whole botnet dies.
    • No firmware diversity: infecting only one device family means one vendor patch removes a large share of the bots at once.

    Each mistake is a defender opportunity. Section 5 turns them into detections.

    Blue team / defender perspective

    Botnets give defenders two jobs at once: watch for a botnet coming at you (floods, stuffing, spam) and watch for your own devices joining one. This section covers visibility, artifacts, and a triage checklist for both.

    Your visibility checklist

    • NetFlow volume analytics: NetFlow is a summary of conversations (who talked to whom, how much, when). Baseline normal traffic per service so a flood is a visible jump, not a surprise.
    • Firewall and perimeter logs: connections per source, per destination port, and unique-source counts.
    • Zeek conn.log and dns.log: Zeek is a free network sensor. Its conn.log shows conversation volumes; dns.log shows DGA-like query bursts.
    • DDoS mitigation dashboards: your ISP or scrubbing provider's console shows volumetric attacks aimed at your addresses, often before they reach you.
    • Threat intel botnet feeds: lists of known botnet C2 domains, IPs, and infected-device ranges (see 5.2).

    Artifacts botnets leave

    ArtifactWhereLooks like
    Volumetric floodNetFlow, ISP mitigationGigabits per second toward one service, from thousands of sources
    Mass unique source IPsFirewall, web logsOne destination port, one URL pattern, thousands of never-seen IPs
    DGA query burstZeek dns.log, resolver logsLong random labels, many NXDOMAIN answers (domain does not exist)
    Known C2 contactFirewall, proxy, EDRConnection to a listed botnet C2 IP, domain, or port
    Infected device calling outNetFlow egress, NACA printer, camera, or TV on your network sending Telnet scans or beacon traffic

    Where to look, in order

    1. ISP / scrubbing console: are you under attack right now? What volume, what ports?
    2. Perimeter flow: unique sources per minute toward each public service, compared with baseline.
    3. Egress flow: which internal devices talk to known C2 lists or behave like bots (scanning, beaconing)?
    4. DNS: NXDOMAIN bursts and random-label queries from single hosts.
    5. Asset inventory: what is the affected device, who owns it, what firmware?

    Why this matters even if you are "small"

    Two directions, one program. Inbound: any service you publish can be flooded or hammered with stuffed logins; the fix is baseline, rate limits, and a scrubbing contract signed before the attack. Outbound: every device you own is a potential bot. An infected camera does not steal your data — it attacks strangers, burns your bandwidth, damages your brand, and can get your address block blacklisted for spam.

    Finding your own bots early is one of the highest-value network detections a team can build, and it is what the next section turns into rules.

    Triage checklist — suspected botnet event

    • Classify direction: are we the target (inbound flood) or the source (internal bot)?
    • If inbound: engage the scrubbing service, apply rate limits, confirm which service and port.
    • If internal: isolate the device at the switch or NAC level, keep its VLAN up for logging.
    • Pull the device's last 7 days of flow and DNS; look for beacons, scans, and DGA queries.
    • Check the C2 indicators against threat intel feeds; note confidence level.
    • Notify the ISP: they can filter upstream and may have seen the same sources hitting others.
    • Re-image or replace the device; block its firmware from calling home again (see section 7).
    • Document timeline, indicators, and cost; feed indicators back to your intel process.

    5.2 · Threat intelligence

    Botnets are shared problems: the same C2 domains and infected ranges hit thousands of organizations. External intel tells you who is active and what to block; your own telemetry confirms what is actually talking to you.

    Where to get botnet intel

    • abuse.ch feeds: community-maintained lists of malware C2 servers and botnet-related URLs, updated continuously.
    • Spamhaus: blocklists and research on spam infrastructure, botnet C2, and hijacked networks.
    • Shadowserver Foundation: scans the internet and emails network owners daily about infected devices in their ranges — free and worth registering for.
    • CISA and national CERT advisories: free, authoritative guidance — the Volt Typhoon advisories (AA23-144A, AA24-038A) name the exact router and VPS relay patterns to hunt.
    • Vendor DDoS reports: Cloudflare, Akamai, and similar providers publish quarterly attack trends, sizes, and exploited vectors.
    • ISP notices: your provider may tell you that your address block is scanning or spamming.
    • Internal telemetry: your own flow records, darknet responses, and honeypots — the only feed that cannot lie about your network.

    Working with confidence levels

    Every indicator should carry a confidence level, and your response should match it:

    • High — seen by several independent feeds and in your logs. Block at the firewall, hunt for it everywhere.
    • Medium — one reputable feed or partial matches. Alert and enrich; block at the proxy for users, not critical paths.
    • Low — unverified or stale. Keep in a watchlist; do not block business traffic on it.

    Always record where an indicator came from and when. Botnet C2 lists age fast: a domain sinkholed yesterday is harmless today, and blocking it wastes attention.

    5.5 · Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    The four rules below cover the highest-value threats (TH-1, TH-2, TH-3, TH-6). Each Sigma rule is valid YAML and is followed by a matching Splunk query, a mapping note, and tuning advice. Replace index and sourcetype names with your local naming — the field assumptions are stated under each query. The IP ranges shown are documentation ranges; replace them with the current ranges from your botnet intel feed.

    SIG-001 · Bot malware runs network scanning commands (TH-2 / TH-6)

    Mapping note: assumes Sysmon Event ID 1 shipped as process_creation; Image and CommandLine are CIM-normalized. False positives: the admin scanner hosts listed above. Tuning: build an allowlist of scanning hosts by Computer name and exclude them; alert harder when the parent process is a browser or mail client, which means a user downloaded the scanner.

    SIG-002 · DGA-like domain query burst (TH-2)

    Mapping note: the aggregation in the Sigma condition (count per host) matches the SPL 60-second window. host here is the querying client IP. False positives: misconfigured clients with a stale search domain. Tuning: raise the threshold for mail servers and CDN nodes that resolve many names; add a second check that the failed labels look random (high consonant runs) to cut retries of real but missing names.

    SIG-003 · Connection to known botnet C2 IP or bot protocol port (TH-2)

    Mapping note: assumes Sysmon Event ID 3 (network connection). The botnet_c2_intel lookup is your feed table with columns dest_ip, feed, confidence; update it on schedule. False positives: any real TR-069 device management on your network. Tuning: route the intel ranges through the lookup rather than the rule text so updates need no rule edit; keep Telnet egress alerts at critical, because almost no enterprise host should ever do it.

    SIG-004 · IoT-style user agent or bot protocol path in proxy logs (TH-6)

    Mapping note: field names assume a standard access log normalized to user_agent and uri; rename to cs_user_agent/cs_uri_stem if you use IIS-style fields. False positives: the vendor agents above. Tuning: join src_ip with the asset inventory so the alert names the device type — an alert that says "printer G0102" is actionable; an IP alone is not. Add your region's known bot UAs from feed reports as they appear.

    Real-world case study — Mirai (2016)

    Mirai is the textbook example of building a botnet (T1584.005). Three young operators infected hundreds of thousands of cameras, DVRs, and routers by trying factory default passwords — then used the army to launch the largest denial-of-service attacks the internet had seen.

    Context

    In 2016, cheap internet-of-things devices — IP cameras, digital video recorders, home routers — were shipped with default usernames and passwords like admin/admin and rarely updated. The Mirai operators wrote malware that swept the internet for these devices, logged in with the default credentials, and installed the bot. No exploit was even needed for most victims: the front door was open.

    The result was a botnet estimated at hundreds of thousands of bots, powerful enough to generate record-setting floods.

    Attack chain in short

    1. Scan the internet on Telnet ports 23 and 2323 for devices with default passwords.
    2. Infect each answering device with the Mirai bot and hide the malware in memory.
    3. Bots report to hardcoded C2 servers and wait for orders.
    4. Operator sends a target list; bots flood it with UDP, SYN, or GRE traffic.
    5. Attack-for-hire: others could pay to point the botnet at targets.

    Timeline

    1. Aug 2016
      Building the army. The operators grow the botnet by default-credential scanning across whole address blocks.
    2. Sep 20, 2016
      620 Gbps at Krebs on Security. A flood of roughly 620 Gbps hits the security journalist site; its host drops the site, and the attack becomes public proof of IoT botnet power.
    3. Sep 2016
      ~1 Tbps at OVH. French hosting provider OVH reports a flood near one terabit per second, launched from about 150,000 IoT devices.
    4. Sep 30, 2016
      Source code leaks. One operator publishes the Mirai source code online. Dozens of copycat variants appear within months — the technique spreads faster than the cleanup.
    5. Oct 21, 2016
      Dyn DNS attack. Mirai bots flood the DNS provider Dyn, which resolves names for Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, Spotify, and many others. Major sites become unreachable across North America and Europe — without the botnet ever touching them.
    6. Dec 2016 onward
      Enforcement and cleanup. Researchers and providers sinkhole C2 infrastructure; ISPs contact customers and replace infected devices. The three operators are later identified and plead guilty in the United States.

    Detection point

    The strongest early signals were volumetric anomalies at network providers: scan waves on Telnet ports, and floods visible in flow records long before targets failed. On the defender side, infected devices showed a clear pattern — a camera or DVR making outbound Telnet connections across many external IPs, and DNS lookups for freshly created domains.

    The Dyn attack also showed a blind spot: the victims did nothing wrong. Their provider was flooded. This pushed many organizations to buy upstream mitigation — detection capacity they do not own.

    Response arc

    • Containment: targets engaged scrubbing services that filtered the floods upstream; providers blackholed attack traffic toward the biggest targets.
    • Takedown: researchers sinkholed Mirai's C2 servers, so new bots could not receive commands; known C2 addresses were blocked across the industry.
    • Eradication: the slow part. Each infected device needed a firmware update or replacement, across hundreds of thousands of homes. ISPs ran mail campaigns and forced modem swaps — it took months.
    • Recovery: services returned quickly after each flood, but confidence took longer: after Dyn, many companies added second DNS providers.

    Impact and team response

    Mirai rewrote expectations for attack size: from "hundreds of gigabits" to "terabits, launched from doorbells and DVRs". Krebs on Security lost its hosting; OVH fought a record flood; the Dyn outage affected a long list of household-name services at once. Response teams learned to escalate through their ISPs within minutes, because a volumetric attack cannot be fought at the target's own firewall — the pipes fill first.

    The leak of the source code changed the team-response picture too: every new variant had new C2 infrastructure, so indicator lists needed constant refresh instead of one-time cleanup.

    Lessons learned

    • Default credentials are a botnet delivery system. Any device with factory passwords is a future bot.
    • Know your devices. Mirai bots hid in plain sight; organizations that had an IoT inventory found their infected devices in days, not months.
    • Provider cooperation is a control. Scrubbing contracts and ISP relationships decided how long outages lasted.
    • Detection must not wait for the flood. Telnet scans and beacon traffic were visible for weeks before the headline attacks.
    • Publicity scales the problem. The code leak multiplied variants faster than cleanup could run.

    Mitigation and defense

    ATT&CK lists only M1056 Pre-compromise for this technique — you cannot stop a botnet from being built somewhere else. What you can do is avoid feeding botnets, survive being targeted by one, and catch devices that join one.

    Enterprise controls by layer

    Network & volume

    • Sign a DDoS scrubbing contract before any attack; a scrubbing service filters flood traffic upstream so your pipes stay open.
    • Build traffic baselines and anomaly monitoring on flow data; consider a network telescope view of unused address space to see scan waves.
    • Rate-limit login endpoints so stuffing waves hit math, not accounts.

    Egress & segmentation

    • Block outbound Telnet (23, 2323) and TR-069 (7547) from user and device VLANs — normal devices never need them.
    • Put cameras, printers, and building systems on an IoT VLAN that can only reach vendor update endpoints.
    • Integrate botnet C2 feeds into firewall and proxy blocking with automatic refresh.

    Devices & credentials

    • Keep an IoT/device inventory with firmware versions and owners; retire anything that no longer gets updates.
    • Enforce credential hygiene on every device: no factory passwords, unique credentials per device class.
    • Patch internet-facing appliances on a fixed clock; check vendor advisories for your camera and DVR models monthly.

    Community & response

    • Join sinkhole participation programs: registering for groups like the Shadowserver Foundation means you get told when your addresses appear in botnet data.
    • Integrate botnet feeds into the SIEM and hunt for your own ranges in external datasets.
    • Practice the flood runbook: who calls the ISP, who engages scrubbing, who talks to users.

    Home and small office

    Built botnets feed on home devices. If you run a small business or work from home, four steps remove most of the risk:

    • Change default passwords on every router, camera, and smart device — this single step defeats most Mirai-style recruitment.
    • Update firmware or enable automatic updates where offered.
    • Disable UPnP on the router so devices cannot open ports to the internet by themselves. UPnP is a feature that lets devices forward ports automatically — botnets love it.
    • Replace devices that no longer receive vendor updates; buy from makers with a patch record.

    Common mistakes

    These are the mistakes we see repeatedly in botnet-related incidents — twelve of them, each with a cheaper fix. Read them as a pre-mortem for your monitoring program.

    #MistakeWhat it costsWhat to do instead
    1No traffic baseline, so floods go unnoticed until users callResponse starts late; outage is longer and louderRecord per-service volume and unique-source counts for 30 days; alert on deviation
    2No DDoS response planTeams improvise during an attack; the ISP call alone can take an hourWrite a one-page flood runbook with phone numbers, decision points, and templates
    3Trusting volume thresholds aloneLow-rate stuffing and beaconing pass under a flood-focused alert setPair volume rules with behavior rules (per-IP spread, beacon intervals, DGA bursts)
    4Alert fatigue: everything is criticalAnalysts mute the channel; the real flood alert is missedUse the severity ladder honestly; review noisy rules monthly
    5Ignoring infected internal devicesYour network attacks others; you get blacklisted and named in reportsRun egress monitoring and treat "our device is a bot" as a first-class alert
    6No IoT/device inventoryNobody knows what the scanning camera is or who owns itInventory every device with type, firmware, owner, and last-seen update
    7Blocking entire residential ranges blindlyLegitimate customers and remote staff break; the flood just shifts sourcesBlock or challenge per destination service and rate; keep residential traffic on a challenge path
    8Not sharing indicatorsPeers re-detect the same botnet from zero; you lose early warningsContribute to ISACs and feed maintainers; share C2 domains with context
    9Ignoring ISP noticesA warning email about scanning becomes a formal abuse complaint and a blocked rangeRoute abuse@ and provider notices into the SOC queue with an SLA
    10Runbooks never testedScrubbing engagement fails at 03:00 because nobody knows the account or portalTabletop the flood scenario twice a year, including the provider call
    11Treating DDoS as only an availability issueFlooding hides other activity — extortions pair with intrusionsDuring any flood, review intrusion alerts; watch for follow-on compromise
    12Missing egress monitoringBots live inside for months, invisible because nobody watches outboundShip firewall, DNS, and proxy logs to the SIEM; run the section 5 rules

    Habit cadence for the team

    Daily

    • Scan egress reports and ask: which internal devices talked to the internet that should not talk at all?
    • Triage new botnet C2 feed hits against your own logs.

    Weekly

    • Botnet C2 data ages fast — re-validate blocked lists and remove sinkholed or expired indicators.
    • Review Shadowserver and ISP notices for devices in your ranges.

    Monthly

    • Rehearse upstream: verify the provider scrubbing contact and portal access before any attack.
    • Test one flood-scenario runbook step and review office and SOHO device egress.

    Response checklist — suspected botnet event

    • Engage the scrubbing service if any service is flooded; record start time and vector.
    • Identify internal infected hosts: check egress flow, DNS, and SIG-003 hits from the last 24 hours.
    • Sinkhole or block the C2 domain and IPs at the resolver and firewall; keep logs.
    • Notify the ISP — ask what they see on their side and whether peers report your ranges.
    • Isolate and re-image or replace infected devices; fix the default password or firmware flaw.
    • Search 90 days of logs for the C2 indicators to scope the event.
    • Document the timeline, indicators, and cost; feed indicators back to your intel process.
