T1030Data Transfer Size Limits

Many companies run a simple defense: raise an alert when one upload is too big, for example anything over 10 MB. So attackers stop sending big uploads. They split stolen data into many small chunks (small pieces of the data), and every piece stays under the alarm line. Instead of one loud 60 GB transfer, the attacker sends 7,000 quiet ones, and each of them looks harmless on its own.

Overview

Before you can detect a technique, you need a clear picture of it. This section explains data transfer size limits in plain words. It shows where the idea sits in an attack, and it lists the facts you will use again later in this guide.

What is it?

Data transfer size limits is an attacker behavior, not a tool. Many data-loss-prevention (DLP) tools work the same way. So do egress rules, which watch traffic that leaves your network. They raise an alert when one transfer crosses a size threshold — a limit line such as one upload larger than 10 MB. An attacker who knows (or guesses) that line simply never crosses it. The data still leaves — it just leaves in slices.

Chunking is the simplest form. The attacker takes a 60 MB file, cuts it into pieces of 9 MB, and uploads the pieces one by one. Each upload passes the check, because each upload is small. Tools help here: archivers can create multi-volume files (7z -v9m), the Linux split command cuts any file into fixed parts, and some malware does the cutting inside its own protocol. MITRE describes the same idea with packet sizes: an adversary may also keep each network packet below a threshold, so even packet-level counters stay quiet.

The key insight for defenders is this: one chunk tells you nothing; the pattern tells you everything. Seven uploads of almost the same size, from one host, to one new destination, inside a few hours, are not seven coincidences. They are one theft in disguise — and that shape is exactly what your analytics should catch.

Why it matters

  • Size thresholds alone are bypassable. If your only egress alert is "over 10 MB", a 30 GB theft in 3 MB parts never fires it.
  • Slow theft means a long exposure window. A drip that takes five weeks is five weeks of compliance duty, not one bad night.
  • It hides inside normal traffic. Small uploads look like sync clients, backups, and browsing, so per-connection review passes them.
  • It is documented everywhere. From Helminth's 23-byte DNS chunks to AppleSeed's 16 MB splits, real families use size limits as standard practice.
  • Ransomware crews use it too. Splitting and throttling uploads (as campaign C0015 did with rclone) keeps bulk theft under bandwidth alarms.

Where it fits in the attack chain

T1030 is not a channel of its own. It is a sizing rule that an attacker applies on top of whichever channel carries the data. A typical chain looks like this:

  1. Initial access (for example phishing, T1566, or a phished vendor VPN account).
  2. Collection (TA0009) — data is gathered and staged, often packed into an encrypted archive.
  3. Channel choice: the C2 channel (T1041), an alternative protocol such as DNS (T1048.003), or a web service such as cloud storage (T1567.002).
  4. T1030 — every transfer is sized to stay under the alarm line.
  5. The attacker reassembles the parts far away from your logging.

This ordering matters for defense: collection must exist before anything can be chunked, so the archive parts on disk (section 4) are your earliest local evidence.

Written version of the simulation

This is the same story in text form. It covers every step the animation shows, so nothing is lost if you cannot see or play the canvas.

  1. The export is collected. The implant on the database host DB-APP-07 receives a task and runs a full dump of the customers table. The result is a single 60 MB export file in a temp folder. SOC view: EDR shows a large mysqldump child process on DB-APP-07 writing to a temp path.
  2. The alarm line is measured. The operator checks the victim's controls and finds the line: the egress DLP gateway raises an alert for uploads over 10 MB. A public policy page and a misconfigured console had already exposed the limit. SOC view: one probe upload of 11 MB fired an alert that nobody joined to the recon session.
  3. The archive is split. The implant packs the export into an encrypted archive and splits it into 9 MB volumes. One smaller final part completes the set, and every part sits under the line. SOC view: Sysmon records 7z started with a -v9m volume flag, then part files appearing in temp.
  4. Part 1 slips through. The first 9 MB part uploads to the attacker's cloud bucket. It is under the line, so the gateway stays silent. SOC view: the proxy records one 9 MB POST to a brand-new external host — below every threshold, no rule fires.
  5. The streak builds. Parts 2 to 6 follow over the next hours. Every part is the same size, from the same host, to the same bucket. SOC view: each upload is still under the line. Only the counters note the repetition: same size, same host, six times.
  6. The last chunk leaves. Part 7 carries the final 6 MB. It uploads next to a small README that describes how the parts fit together. The streak is complete: 60 MB total. SOC view: the upload streak counter reaches seven parts — 60 MB in six hours from one host to one destination.
  7. Reassembly on the far side. The attacker joins the parts back into the original archive and deletes them from the bucket. No stolen file ever moved in one piece. SOC view: bucket activity shows a rapid download of all parts, then deletion — the theft is already finished.
  8. The aggregate screams. A streak alert fires: six uploads of near-identical size plus one smaller final part, one new destination, within six hours. The SOC blocks the bucket and isolates DB-APP-07. SOC view: aggregate analytics did what per-upload rules could not — they turned seven silent uploads into one loud pattern.

Red team / hacker perspective

This section explains why attackers keep transfers small and how the splitting works. Everything here is described for defense, education, and safe lab practice. Do not run these ideas against systems you do not own.

When and why an attacker splits data

Splitting is a reaction to a control. If a network alerts on large uploads, every big transfer is a risk, so the attacker sizes each transfer to stay under the line. The goal is threefold. Defeat size-based DLP: no single transfer looks worth an alert. Stay under egress thresholds: bandwidth and volume counters never spike. Keep bandwidth discipline: campaign C0015 throttled rclone during exfiltration for exactly this reason. Ransomware crews copy data slowly on purpose before encryption day.

Sizing also buys patience. A drip of 40 parts per night takes weeks, but it survives environments where a one-hour 30 GB upload would be blocked or flagged within minutes. Espionage teams accept the slow pace; ransomware crews accept it too, because stolen data is their leverage and silence is worth more than speed.

Mechanism 1: archive volume splits

The cheapest method needs no malware at all. Standard archivers create multi-volume files: 7z a -v9m data.7z produces data.7z.001, .002 and so on; RAR uses rar a -v5m; Linux split -b 5m file part- cuts any file into fixed pieces. The parts are uploaded individually and rejoined remotely. Campaign C0026 shipped encrypted archives as 3 MB parts this way, and LuminousMoth split archives specifically to bypass a 5 MB limit.

Mechanism 2: application-layer chunking

Implants and C2 frameworks cut data inside their own protocol, so no archive file ever appears. Cobalt Strike breaks large data sets into smaller chunks for transport, and the Mythic framework supports custom chunk sizes per payload. AppleSeed divides files once they reach 16 MB, APT41 moves post-exploitation files in fixed-size chunks, and Carbanak splits any message over 4096 bytes into compressed pieces.

Mechanism 3: tiny chunks over DNS

DNS tunneling hides data inside normal-looking name queries, and each query can carry only a few dozen characters — which makes DNS naturally chunked. Helminth sent data in DNS query chunks of up to 23 bytes, Kessel fit data into DNS subdomains, and Kevin exfiltrated in 27-character chunks. Volume is tiny per query, so every size-based rule in the network passes it.

Two more channels

  • Email drip: many small messages, each with one attachment of similar size, to an external mailbox. Slow, but mail gateways rarely count attachment sizes per sender.
  • Chunked transfer-encoding uploads: HTTP requests sent with Transfer-Encoding: chunked never declare a total body size, so body-size inspection at the proxy sees only stream fragments.

Where it applies and what it needs

  • Places: any endpoint or server with collected data — workstations, database hosts, file-transfer servers, cloud tenants. The technique is platform-independent (ESXi, Linux, Windows, macOS).
  • Preconditions: collection already done, code execution or a sync client on the host, and a working exit path. The attacker also needs the alarm line. It may come from a public policy page, test uploads, or product defaults.
  • Temperament: patience. The attacker trades speed for silence, often scheduling parts during backup windows so the rhythm blends in.

Practical variations and common attacker mistakes

  • Randomized part sizes: fixed sizes create fingerprints. LunarWeb splits data over 1.33 MB into random parts between 384 and 512 KB precisely to break uniformity.
  • Jittered intervals: uploading every 45 minutes on the dot is a rhythm, and rhythm is detectable (analytic AN0596).
  • Too many chunks to one destination: hundreds of uploads from one host to one new bucket is a streak, and streaks are countable (AN0597).
  • Reassembly notes left behind: README files listing part order next to the parts in a bucket give responders the full picture for free.
  • Retry storms: failed uploads retried immediately create bursts of identical transfers — loud, and easy to alert on.
Lab only — safe and legal

The commands below are a harmless classroom exercise on a test VM you own. They create one dummy file and cut it into fixed-size parts on local disk. No network connection is made, and nothing is uploaded. The artifacts also help with a purple-team test — a joint drill where your own SOC checks that the section 5.5 rules fire on the part names and sizes.

lab_split_local.sh — local file splitting practice (no network)
# LAB ONLY: mimics the *shape* of chunked exfiltration using a dummy file on local disk.
# Run on a Linux or macOS test VM. No external destination is contacted at any step.

mkdir -p ~/lab/chunking && cd ~/lab/chunking
head -c 52428800 /dev/urandom > demo.bin          # a harmless 50 MB dummy file
split -b 5m demo.bin part-                        # -> part-aa .. part-aj (10 parts of 5 MB)
sha256sum part-* > parts.sha256                   # macOS: use  shasum -a 256 part-*
ls -l part-*                                      # every part is identical in size: the fingerprint
rm -f demo.bin                                    # keep only the parts, as an attacker would

# Observe the artifacts a detector would see: ten equal-size files with sequential names.
# In a purple test, drop copies into C:\Windows\Temp (Windows lab) and watch your own
# Sysmon part-file rule (section 5.5, Rule 2) fire. Clean up:  rm -rf ~/lab/chunking

Blue team / SOC perspective

Chunked theft is invisible to per-connection rules, so the blue team view changes shape: you stop grading single uploads and start grading aggregates. This section shows where the evidence lands, which artifacts to hunt for, and a short triage flow for analysts.

How defenders can see it

Three shifts in thinking do most of the work. First, aggregate per host: to aggregate means to group many events into one summary. A 9 MB upload is noise. But "7 uploads of 9 MB from one host to one new destination in 6 hours" is a case. Compute totals, counts, and size standard deviation per source-destination pair. Second, streak analytics: a streak is a run of similar transfers over time. The size standard deviation across consecutive uploads is tiny for chunked theft and large for normal traffic. That makes it a clean feature (analytic AN0597). Third, DNS analytics: label length, entropy, and TXT volume per client catch the tiny-chunk variants that never touch your proxy (AN0596, and AN0598 for macOS launch-driven loops). Per-connection alerts still have value — but here they are the floor, not the detection.

Where to look first

  • Per-host egress totals for the last 7 days, ranked by upload count to single external destinations.
  • Sysmon EID 11 (or EDR file events) for multi-volume archive names in temp and user-writable paths.
  • DNS analytics: clients with high counts of TXT queries or subdomain labels longer than 40 characters.
  • Proxy logs: POST bodies within a narrow size band, repeated to one URI or host.
  • Cloud tenant logs: PutObject series with uniform byte counts and scripted user agents such as rclone.

Why it matters and how it fits the chain

A drip is a slow breach, and slow breaches are compliance problems. Regulators do not ask how big each upload was — they ask when the data left and how long the window stayed open. Five weeks of 9 MB parts is a five-week notification window that must be measured from logs, which is why retention matters as much as detection. The pattern is also a ransomware precursor: crews that split and throttle uploads (C0015-style) are copying leverage before encryption day.

ATT&CK's detection strategy DET0213 says the same thing in three analytic shapes. AN0596: a process makes outbound connections that send uniform packet sizes at a consistent interval, defeating threshold alerts. AN0597: processes that normally do no networking repeatedly send similarly sized payloads at uniform intervals. AN0598: on macOS, LaunchAgents or unexpected users drive external connections that consistently transmit fixed-size data. All three are aggregate shapes — none of them fires on a single transfer.

Short triage flow

  1. Confirm the streak. Pull 24 to 72 hours of proxy or flow records for the source host. Chunked theft shows as 5 or more uploads of near-identical size to one external destination, with a regular interval.
  2. Find the sender process. Map the upload windows to process telemetry. Name the executable, its parent, and the account — a sync client started by a server-side implant is the usual answer.
  3. Find the archive parts. Search Sysmon EID 11 or EDR file events for *.7z.00*, *.part-aa, or sequential part-* names in temp paths, and check whether they are equal in size.
  4. Rate the destination. Check the external host or bucket: age, reputation, TLS certificate, whether any other host in your estate talks to it.
  5. Contain on both ends. Block the destination, isolate the host, and revoke the cloud tokens the sync client used. Open the incident with a data-volume estimate: parts times size times days gives you the exposure window.

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

Each threat has at least one Sigma rule, and every rule ships with a matching Splunk SPL query. The split is deliberate: Sigma expresses the per-event shape, while the SPL below it carries the aggregate logic — counts, size standard deviations, and streak windows — that Sigma cannot express. SPL examples assume these index names: index=sysmon (Sysmon), index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns and sourcetype=zeek:http (Zeek), index=cloud sourcetype=aws:cloudtrail (CloudTrail), and index=mail (mail gateway). Swap them for your own names; field notes sit under each query.

Rule 1 — Archiver started with volume-split flags (Threat A) HIGH

Rule 3 — DNS TXT queries with long subdomain labels (Threat B) MEDIUM

Sigma YAML — dns_long_label_txt.yml
title: DNS TXT Query with Unusually Long Subdomain Label
id: c93d5e67-8f24-4b19-a6c0-47e2b8d19a05
status: test
description: Detects DNS queries whose name carries one very long label, which

  Kevin 27-character chunks, so real labels are often shorter than 40; the

  queries per host, so pair it with the Splunk query below.

  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1030/

  category: dns
  product: zeek
detection:
  selection_txt:
    qtype_name: 'TXT'
  selection_long:
    query|re: '^[a-z0-9]{40,}\.'

    query|endswith: '.corp.example'

Splunk SPL — per-host DNS label-length statistics
index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns qtype_name=TXT
| rex field=query "^(?<label1>[^.]+)"
| eval label_len=len(label1)

Splunk SPL — upload streak detection (5+ near-identical bodies in 6 h)
index=network sourcetype=zeek:http cs_method=POST request_body_len>=5000000
| bin _time span=6h
| stats count AS uploads stdev(request_body_len) AS sd_len avg(request_body_len) AS avg_len

Validate every rule before production. First replay one week of data and confirm the expected false-positive rate. Then run a purple-team exercise that splits a dummy file with the section 3 lab. 6

Real-world case study

The case below is a composite written for training: it merges patterns from public reporting on chunked exfiltration into one coherent story. Names, hosts, and numbers are fictional; the behaviors are not.

Context

Northlake Health Group runs three hospitals and a shared billing platform. Detection coverage was typical for healthcare: EDR on endpoints, proxy logs, mail security, and a SIEM. Egress rules alerted only on single transfers over 10 MB. Flow data was kept for 30 days, and nobody owned per-host egress analytics. Vendor remote access used password-only VPN accounts.

Week -5 — initial access

Vendor VPN account phished

A phished invoice site harvests the VPN password of a billing-software vendor. The account has broad reach into the hospital group's file-transfer segment. No MFA exists on vendor VPN yet, so one password is enough.

Week -4 to -3 — collection

Patient-billing extracts staged

The attacker loads a small service on three servers — the file-transfer host, the billing application server, and a backup staging host — and runs nightly extracts. Roughly 30 GB of patient-billing records is gathered into encrypted archives.

Week -2 — the drip starts

9 MB parts under a 10 MB alarm

The service reads the DLP policy (a partially public configuration page shows the 10 MB line) and splits each archive into 9 MB encrypted parts. A cloud drive sync client uploads about 40 parts per host per night during backup hours.

Week -2 to day 21 — quiet theft

Every part passes, every night

For five weeks, three hosts each drip around 40 uniform 9 MB uploads per night to the same attacker-controlled cloud app. The per-transfer DLP rule never fires, and the sync traffic blends with sanctioned backup windows.

Day 0 — analytics deployed

Streak analytics go live

Mid-theft, the team deploys its first aggregate analytics. These are per-host upload streaks — 5 or more near-identical transfers to one new destination in 6 hours — plus a Sysmon 11 watchlist for multi-volume archive names. Baselines take two weeks to settle.

Day 22 — detection point

The streak fires

The streak rule flags all three hosts on the same night: uniform 9 MB uploads to one new cloud app, plus matching part-file names in Sysmon 11. An analyst joins the two lanes, confirms the splitting service, and raises P1.

Day 22 — containment

Break the drip on both ends

EDR isolates the three hosts, the sync client is blocked at the proxy and removed, and the cloud app token is revoked tenant-side. Blocking the destination mattered: parts already staged on disk could no longer leave.

Day 23-26 — eradication

Remove, rotate, harden

IR removes the splitting service and loader from all three hosts and reimages them. The vendor VPN account is disabled, its credentials rotated, and phishing-resistant MFA is enforced for all vendor remote access.

Day 26-40 — recovery

Window measured, duties met

From proxy and cloud logs the team reconstructs the exposure window at about five weeks and roughly 30 GB confirmed off-network. HIPAA and GDPR notifications go out with the measured timeline, not an estimate.

Impact and consequences

  • About 30 GB of patient-billing records exposed over a five-week window.
  • Roughly 800,000 dollars in notification, legal, and credit-monitoring costs.
  • Six weeks of incident-response work across three hospitals and one vendor.
  • Regulator engagement with a measured exposure timeline, which kept penalties survivable.

Team response and communication

  • The compliance lead joined the incident bridge on day one, so every containment step was logged for the notification file.
  • The software vendor was brought in within 24 hours; their support account and installer package were checked and cleared.
  • A daily 16:00 written update replaced ad-hoc calls for the whole response, keeping the three hospital boards aligned.

Lessons learned

  • Size thresholds alone are bypassable. The 10 MB DLP line ran for five weeks without one hit; the aggregate streak rule found the theft in one night.
  • Archive part names are cheap, high-signal artifacts. Sysmon 11 catching *.7z.00* files in temp was the second confirming lane — almost no legitimate process writes those names.
  • Vendor and VPN accounts need phishing-resistant MFA. The whole incident started with one password, and the vendor relationship made lateral movement look normal.
  • DNS and proxy must share one analytics layer. The streak was visible in both lanes only after they were joined; separate tools kept each half looking innocent.
  • Purple-test the chunking path. The team now runs the section 3 lab quarterly and measures whether Rules 1, 2, and 4 still fire on part names, sizes, and streaks.

Mitigation and defense

T1030 cannot be patched, because it abuses ordinary features: archivers, sync clients, and normal protocols. ATT&CK therefore lists only one direct mitigation, M1031 Network Intrusion Prevention. The goal is layered friction: make the threshold smarter than the attacker, make the artifacts loud, and make the aggregate impossible to miss.

Policy and DLP

  • Treat size thresholds as tripwires, not walls: add aggregate and streak rules alongside them.
  • Extend DLP coverage to web and cloud uploads, not only email.
  • Classify regulated data and restrict which hosts may hold or move it.

Endpoint

  • Block or require review for volume-split tooling via EDR policy.
  • Apply Attack Surface Reduction rules; block unsigned binaries in user-writable paths.
  • Alert on multi-volume archive names created in temp folders (Sysmon 11).

Network and DNS

  • Run DNS analytics: label length, entropy, and TXT volume per client.
  • Build egress baselines per host; compare upload counts, not just bytes.
  • TLS inspection where lawful, or SNI-based filtering at minimum.

Cloud and identity

  • Set CASB upload baselines per app and alert on part-upload patterns.
  • Scope storage tokens and OAuth apps to least privilege; revoke fast.
  • Require phishing-resistant MFA for vendor and VPN access.

Logging

  • Keep proxy, flow, DNS, and Sysmon 11 logs for 180 days.
  • Retention must outlast the drip: a 5-week theft needs 5-week-old logs to quantify.
  • Verify log sources arrive daily; a silent parser hides a streak.

Monitoring

  • Deploy AN0596, AN0597, and AN0598 style analytics and validate them.
  • Baseline size variance per host-destination pair; alert on tight variance.
  • Review aggregate egress dashboards daily, per-host totals first.

Backup and response

  • Immutable, offline-verified backups — exfiltration often precedes encryption.
  • Runbook for drip theft, including exposure-window math (parts times size times days).
  • Pre-approve legal and communications steps for the 72-hour clocks.
Home and small office

You do not need a SOC. Keep the operating system and apps updated. Keep one offline backup. Run the built-in protection, such as Defender or an equivalent tool. Never install "free" sync or remote tools you did not look up. Check your router for unknown port-forwarding rules. If a computer uploads small files for hours every night, that is worth investigating.

Response checklist

  • Confirm the streak pattern: 5 or more near-identical uploads from one host to one destination.
  • Find the splitting process and the archive parts on disk; hash the binary and note the volume flag.
  • Quantify the total and the window: parts times size times days, first to last upload.
  • Block the destination at proxy and firewall, including the certificate if known.
  • Isolate affected hosts with EDR; stop the sync or upload client.
  • Rotate credentials used on the hosts, including service accounts.
  • Check and revoke cloud tokens or OAuth apps the sync client used.
  • Preserve proxy, DNS, and Sysmon 11 logs for the whole exposure window.
  • Notify legal and compliance with the measured volume and window.
  • Add the missed signal to the detection backlog and schedule a purple retest.
