MITRE ATT&CK · RECONNAISSANCE

Spearphishing Voice T1598.004

Spearphishing voice — often called vishing (voice phishing) — is a targeted phone call that impersonates someone trusted, such as the IT service desk or a company executive. The goal is information: a password, an MFA (multi-factor authentication) code, or an account change the caller did not earn. This guide explains how the trick works, why it leaves few digital traces, and how to detect it by joining telephony, identity, and help-desk logs.

Tactic · Reconnaissance Platforms · PRE (pre-attack; targets people, phone systems, and identity flows) Data sources · Telephony/CDR logs, IdP and MFA logs, help-desk ticket logs Parent · T1598

What it is

A planned phone call, or a voicemail, that pretends to be IT, security, or an executive to make a person share secrets or approve changes.

Where it fits

Reconnaissance — before Initial Access

Primary target

Help-desk staff, finance staff, and any employee whose identity unlocks systems

Main risk

Passwords, MFA codes, and resets that turn one call into full account takeover

Contents

    1

    Overview

    T1598.004 is the voice channel of phishing for information. The attacker calls a person, plays a role, and asks questions that sound routine. The call is short, personal, and hard to log, which makes detection a team effort across phone, identity, and ticket systems.

    What this technique is

    The attacker uses voice communications — a call to a desk phone, a mobile number, or a cloud calling platform such as Teams Phone — to ask an employee for sensitive information. The caller pretends to be IT support, security staff, a vendor engineer, or a senior leader. ATT&CK notes the calls can be made by a person, by a hired call center, or by robocalls (recorded messages that dial many numbers automatically). In a related variant called callback phishing, a text or email asks the victim to phone a number, and the call then collects the confidential information.

    The ask varies. It might be: confirm your MFA (multi-factor authentication) code, set a new password while I stay on the line, approve this payment, or name your VPN (virtual private network) group. Nothing is installed. No link is clicked. The voice itself is the tool, and the employee's answer is the deliverable.

    Because the conversation happens on the phone network, most of it never touches company systems. Only the side effects — a ticket, a reset, an MFA approval — are visible in logs.

    Why it matters

    Voice removes many defenses that work against email. Filters cannot read a phone call, and a confident caller can create pressure that a written message cannot. Attackers know this and save voice for their highest-value asks.

    The most damaging pattern is help-desk vishing. The caller impersonates an employee, asks the service desk for a password reset or an MFA re-enrollment, and walks away with working access. This exact pattern drove several major breaches in recent years.

    For a security operations center (SOC), the challenge is that the phone call is nearly invisible. Detection depends on noticing the identity changes the call caused, and on correlating them with telephony records such as CDRs (call detail records — per-call summaries kept by phone systems).

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Reconnaissance→Initial Access→Execution→Persistence→Lateral Movement

    Spearphishing voice sits in Reconnaissance (ATT&CK tactic TA0043). The attacker is collecting facts and access material before trying to enter. A successful call often hands the attacker a fresh password or MFA code, which they immediately spend in Initial Access. For example, they sign in to the identity provider (IdP — the service that checks usernames and passwords, such as Okta or Microsoft Entra ID).

    T1598.004 vs T1566.004

    Both voice techniques share a name and a channel, but they sit in different tactics. T1598.004 asks questions; T1566.004 gains entry.

    Terms in this guide

    Spearphishing

    A phishing message or call aimed at one chosen person, not sent to thousands. The story is built from real details about the target.

    Elicitation

    Getting information from people through normal-looking conversation. The caller asks small, harmless-seeming questions that add up.

    Pretext

    The invented role behind the call, such as "IT support closing your account". A good pretext explains why the caller is busy, in a hurry, and allowed to ask.

    Vishing

    Voice phishing: the same idea as email phishing, but carried out by phone call or voicemail.

    Caller ID spoofing

    Making the phone display show a number or name the caller does not own. The screen may show your real help-desk number while the call comes from anywhere.

    CDR

    Call detail record: a per-call log line kept by phone platforms, with caller, callee, time, direction, and duration. It is the main telephony evidence.

    3
    Red Team Perspective
    
      
    This section describes how professional social-engineering exercises stage voice attacks. It is written at planning level only, for defenders who must understand the pattern. Everything here is about defense: knowing the method is what makes the signals in Section 5 make sense.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    When and why attackers use it
    
          
    Voice is chosen when the ask is too sensitive for email. Examples: a password reset, an MFA code, a payment approval, or a verbal confirmation that a real employee is on staff. A caller can answer objections in real time, sound urgent, and use authority in a way text cannot.
    
          
    It is also the channel of choice when email defenses are strong. Groups that target help desks do not need to defeat a mail filter; they need a tired agent at the end of a shift who wants to be helpful.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Preconditions
    
          
    The attacker needs only a phone and public information, but better preparation raises the odds:
    
          
      
            
    • Names, roles, and shift patterns from public posts or the company website.
      • 
            
    • The real help-desk or reception number, so caller ID can be spoofed to match.
      • 
            
    • Knowledge of which IdP and MFA method the company uses.
      • 
            
    • Some stolen or leaked identity data — an employee ID, a partial address — to sound credible.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    How it is done
    
      
      
        
    1. Pick the target — Choose a person whose identity unlocks something: a service-desk agent, a finance clerk, an executive assistant, or any user with VPN access.
      2. 
        
    2. Collect the backstory — Gather names, team names, tools, and schedules from public sources so every answer sounds native.
      3. 
        
    3. Build the pretext — Write the role, the reason for urgency, and the exact ask. Good pretexts are boring: "routine check", "account lock", "payment confirmation".
      4. 
        
    4. Spoof the caller ID — Use a cheap online calling service that lets the displayed number be set to the real help-desk or an executive's number.
      5. 
        
    5. Choose the moment — Call at shift change, during an outage, or just after a real company email so confusion is at its peak.
      6. 
        
    6. Make the call — Open with authority, create pressure, then ask for one item. It might be a code, a reset, a confirmation, or a transfer to a colleague who "will take it from here".
      7. 
        
    7. Capture and use — Record the answer, spend the credential within minutes, and follow up with more calls while access is fresh.
      8. 
      
    
      
    Where it applies
    
      
    
        
    Help-desk reset calls
    The caller impersonates an employee and asks for a password reset or MFA re-enrollment. This is the highest-value variant and the one behind the largest breaches.
    
        
    MFA code harvesting
    A push notification is triggered (by a login attempt or by an attacker-generated prompt) and the caller asks the employee to "approve" or read the code. The employee believes the call proves it is legitimate.
    
        
    Executive and finance calls
    The caller sounds like a senior leader and asks finance staff to move money or send files. AI voice cloning now makes the voice itself convincing.
    
        
    Voicemail, robocall, and callback lures
    A voicemail claims a payroll change failed or a package is held, and asks for a callback. Robocalls can deliver the same lure to thousands of numbers at once. The callback harvests details or sets up the next call.
    
      
    
      
    
        Lab only
        
    A safe exercise for your team: run a phone server (PBX — private branch exchange, phone software for labs) in Docker on localhost. Then study the CDR output, so analysts learn the exact fields a voice log carries. Never point it at a real carrier or a real number; use the default lab extension and no external trunk.
    
        
      
    
      
    Common variations
    
      
    
        
    The IT support call
    "I am from IT. Your account is sending spam. I need to verify you and push a fix." The ask is a code, a password, or remote access approval.
    
        
    The vendor engineer
    "I am with your printer or VPN vendor. A patch needs the admin PIN." The caller borrows a real vendor name and a support ticket number.
    
        
    The executive in a hurry
    "I am boarding a flight. Do not email me about this." Pressure plus a request for payment data or file sharing, sometimes with a cloned voice.
    
        
    The recruiter or new hire
    A call "from HR" asks the new employee to confirm personal data, or the attacker impersonates a new hire and calls the help desk to be onboarded.
    
      
    
      
    
        Attacker mistakes that create noise
        
      
          
    • Calling too many employees from one number in a short window — this shows up clearly in CDR volume.
      • 
          
    • Keeping calls short and dense, which produces a burst of missed-call and short-duration records.
      • 
          
    • Triggering an MFA push and then staying on the line, so the approval sits seconds after a login attempt in IdP logs.
      • 
          
    • Asking the help desk for a reset without knowing details only the real user would hold, which agents start to notice.
      • 
          
    • Reusing the same spoofed number or voice across campaigns, which lets defenders link incidents.
      • 
        
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    4
    Blue Team Perspective
    
      
    Defenders cannot hear the call, but they can see what the call caused. This section maps the visible surface: which systems record something, which artifacts to pull, and how the first hour of triage should run. The core idea is simple — chase the side effects, not the voice.
    
      
    Where defenders can see it
    
      
    
        
    
          
    SignalWhere you see itWhy it helps
    
          
            
    Password reset or MFA re-enrollment for one userIdP audit log (Okta, Microsoft Entra ID)Reset plus new factor within minutes is the classic voice-attack outcome
    
            
    Help-desk ticket opened by phoneTicket system (for example ServiceNow, Zendesk)Ties a human conversation to the account change and shows who verified what
    
            
    Inbound calls to staff from new or spoofed numbersTeams Phone CDR or carrier recordsVolume, timing, and duration patterns expose calling campaigns
    
            
    MFA approval with no matching user actionIdP and MFA logsA code the user shared, or a prompt the user approved under pressure
    
            
    New session from an unfamiliar network right after a callIdP sign-in logsShows the harvested credential being spent
    
              
        
    
      
    
      
    Artifacts this technique leaves behind
    
      
    
        
    Reset and enrollment events
    The IdP records user.account.reset_password and MFA factor changes with actor, target, and client. If the actor is a service-desk agent and the target is a user, the phone call is the likely bridge.
    
        
    CDR lines
    Every call through a cloud phone platform writes a record: caller, callee, direction, duration, trunk. Call patterns — bursts, short calls, spoofed internal numbers — live here.
    
        
    Tickets with verification fields
    The help-desk ticket shows the channel (phone), the requester, and whether a callback or video check was done. A reset ticket without callback proof is a finding, not just paperwork.
    
        
    MFA challenge records
    MFA logs show each push, call, or one-time passcode, its result, and its source context. A satisfied challenge from a new country or device is the harvest moment.
    
      
    
      
    
        How this fits the attack chain
        
    T1598.004 usually ends with the attacker holding a working identity. The next steps are often an MFA request generation technique (T1621 — flooding prompts), credential use against VPN or email, or impersonation (T1656) to continue manipulation. Email-based variants T1598.001–T1598.003 often support the same campaign, and T1589 (Gather Victim Identity Information) is the quieter sibling that feeds the call script. Treat one confirmed vishing call as the start of an identity incident, not a single odd event.
    
      
    
      
    First-hour triage flow
    
      
      
        
    1. Confirm the report — Ask the employee when the call came, what number showed, and what was asked or shared. Write it down verbatim.
      2. 
        
    2. Pull the identity events — Get the IdP events for the user in a two-hour window around the call: resets, factor changes, sign-ins, MFA challenges.
      3. 
        
    3. Pull the phone records — Find the CDR entries for the user's extension and any forwarded numbers around the same time.
      4. 
        
    4. Find the ticket — Search the ticket system for the user and the time window. Check who verified the request and how.
      5. 
        
    5. Contain — Revoke sessions, force a reset, remove attacker-enrolled MFA devices, and re-enroll the user on a verified callback.
      6. 
        
    6. Widen the search — Hunt for the same caller number, the same agent, or the same reset pattern across all users in the past 48 hours.
      7. 
      
    
      
    
        One quick win
        
    Add three required fields to every phone-channel identity ticket: callback number used, verification method, and agent name. Then alert on any identity change whose ticket lacks a callback record. This one control converts the weakest human step into a logged, checkable decision — and it is the cheapest detection you can deploy this week.

    Confidence levels
    
          
    High means multiple independent sources agree; act on it now by tuning rules and briefing the help desk. Medium means one solid source or partial agreement; add it to hunts and watchlists. Low means rumor or single unverified claim; track it, but do not change controls yet.

    How to apply intel

    5.4 Logs and Patterns
    
      
    For each threat, this subsection lists the log sources, the key fields, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and the detection logic in plain words. Each block ends with a realistic example event. Domain and user names are fictional; hostile indicators are defanged (dots shown as [.] ).
    

      
    Threat 1 — Help-desk vishing to reset passwords/MFA
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    IdP audit log (Okta System Log, Microsoft Entra ID audit), help-desk ticket system, Teams Phone CDR
    
        
    Key fields
    eventType, actor.alternateId, target.alternateId, outcome.result, ticket channel, callback-verified flag
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Resets follow a ticket with a verified callback; agents process a few per day; MFA re-enrollment is rare and announced
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    Reset plus MFA factor change for one user within minutes; ticket opened by phone with no callback proof; actor is one agent processing an urgent request at shift edge
    
        
    Detection logic
    Join the IdP reset and factor events to the ticket that caused them. Flag when the chain (call → ticket → reset → factor enroll) completes fast or when the callback field is empty.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 2 — Voice MFA code harvesting
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    IdP and MFA logs (Entra ID sign-in logs, Okta user.mfa.* events), authenticator app telemetry
    
        
    Key fields
    mfaDetail.authMethod, Status.errorCode, location.countryCode, ipAddress, authenticationRequirement
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Users satisfy MFA from known countries on known devices; phone-call and OTP (one-time passcode) factors are uncommon
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    MFA satisfied by phone call or OTP from a new country or network; a burst of failed challenges then one success; approval seconds after an attacker-triggered prompt
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert on successful MFA where the method is phone call or OTP and the source context is new for that user, especially within minutes of an inbound call in the CDR.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 3 — AI-cloned executive voice authorizing payments
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Teams Phone CDR, email audit (follow-up messages), finance workflow logs, help-desk tickets
    
        
    Key fields
    CallerId, CallerDisplayName, CalleeId, CallDirection, Duration, TrunkFQDN, CallStartTime
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Executives call from listed numbers through the company trunk; finance callbacks go through known contacts
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    An inbound call that displays an internal or executive number but arrives through an external PSTN (public switched telephone network — the regular carrier network) trunk; short urgent call followed by payment-related activity
    
        
    Detection logic
    Flag inbound PSTN calls whose caller ID falls inside your own DDI range or matches an executive's listed number but whose trunk is external. Correlate with payment tickets raised within the hour.
    
      
    
      
      
    Reading the lines: two inbound calls display the real internal help-desk number, yet both arrive through external PSTN trunks — that mismatch is the spoof. The third line is the employee calling back a number the caller left in a voicemail, about fifteen minutes after the "urgent" conversation.
    

      
    Threat 4 — Caller-ID spoofing impersonating IT/security/vendor
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Teams Phone CDR, carrier or SIP (session initiation protocol — the call signaling protocol) trunk logs, help-desk tickets
    
        
    Key fields
    CallerId, CalleeId, CallDirection, Duration, CallStartTime, SessionId
    
        
    Normal behavior
    External callers reach a handful of staff through reception; internal calls never enter from a PSTN trunk
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    One external number (or number block) contacting many employees with short calls in an hour; the same display name appearing across unrelated extensions
    
        
    Detection logic
    Group inbound calls by caller ID over a one-hour window. Raise a finding when the distinct callee count crosses a threshold that normal business never produces (tune to your size).
    
      
    
      
      
    Six employees, one caller, calls under 130 seconds each, all inside 21 minutes. No normal process calls six people that briefly from one external number. The pattern is the indicator.
    

      
    Threat 5 — Voicemail or callback lures
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Help-desk ticket system, Teams Phone CDR and voicemail logs, employee reports
    
        
    Key fields
    ticket channel, u_phone_number_given, u_callback_verified, CallerId, CalleeId, short_description
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Voicemails come from known customers and vendors; callbacks are returned to listed switchboard numbers
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    Voicemails asking for callbacks on identity topics; employees calling back numbers that appear in multiple unrelated tickets; missed-call storms followed by callbacks
    
        
    Detection logic
    Cluster tickets and callbacks by the phone number inside them. Flag numbers that appear in several tickets or that received staff callbacks after a missed-call burst.
    
      
    
      
      
    The ticket records the callback number and the fact that verification never completed. Two more tickets quoting the same number would make this an incident, which is exactly what the detection logic clusters on.
    
      
    5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL
    
      
    Sigma is a YAML (a readable configuration format) rule language that many SIEMs (security information and event management platforms) can compile. SPL is Splunk's search language. Below, each of the five threats gets one Sigma rule and one matching SPL query. Remember the honesty note: these rules watch identity and telephony side effects, not the voice itself.
    

      
    Rule 1 — Reset then enroll chain (severity: critical)
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    
              Assumes index=okta sourcetype="OktaIM2:log"; uses eventType, actor.alternateId, target.id. Set the span to your reset review window (15 minutes here). The Sigma counts any two matching events (count() by target.id >= 2); the SPL mirrors that with the event count, so two resets also match, not only a reset plus an enrollment.
              Batch resets from a password policy push, or a user re-enrolling after losing a phone, both match the pattern.
              Join results to ticket data and drop events whose ticket shows a verified callback; suppress known policy-change maintenance windows.
            
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 2 — MFA satisfied by call or OTP from a new country (severity: high)
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    
              Assumes index=aad sourcetype="azure:aad:signin". Field names vary by tenant pipeline: some export mfaDetail, newer ones use authentication details arrays — adjust to your transform. The JSON example in 5.4 nests these fields under properties; the rule shows them hoisted to top level, which some ingest pipelines do. Align the paths with your own transform.
              Traveling staff and contractors on foreign networks with legitimate OTP use will match.
              Keep a lookup of traveler dates and managed device IDs, and filter matches where the device is compliant and registered.
            
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 3 — Inbound call wearing an internal number (severity: high)
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    
              Assumes index=voice sourcetype="teams:cdr". Replace +1425555 with your own DDI block; keep TrunkFQDN so external trunks stay visible in the output. The Sigma fires on one matching call; the SPL groups by caller so the analyst sees the campaign volume behind each hit. Assume Duration is recorded in seconds.
              Legitimate call forwarding into your range and transfer loops can display internal numbers.
              Build an allowlist of trunks known to forward calls for you, and exclude their TrunkFQDN values.
            
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 4 — One caller reaching many employees (severity: high)
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    
              Assumes index=voice sourcetype="teams:cdr" with CallerId, CalleeId, Duration. The one-hour span matches the Sigma description's intent. The rule counts calls, not distinct callees; repeat calls to the same employee also count. Assume Duration is normalized to seconds at ingest.
              Notification systems, outage call trees, and legitimate survey callers hit many callees fast.
              Exclude your notification service numbers and raise the threshold until only unlisted external numbers remain.
            
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 5 — Short-call burst suggesting callback lures (severity: medium)
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    
              Assumes index=voice sourcetype="teams:cdr"; Duration is seconds. Missed calls may not appear in some CDR exports — if so, raise the duration cut or add voicemail events as a source.
              Telemarketing, wrong numbers, and automated reminders will match the shape.
              Correlate the caller ID with the u_phone_number_given field in help-desk tickets before raising a case; suppress numbers with an existing business record.
            
    
              
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    6
    Real-World Case Study
    
      
    This is an educational reconstruction of the MGM Resorts incident of September 2023, written from public reporting. No private details are included, and no individuals are named as victims. The point is to show how one phone call to a help desk became a company-wide outage — and what defenders changed afterward.
    
      
    Case snapshot
    
      
    
        
    Victim sector
    Hospitality and casinos (large resort operator)
    
        
    Year
    2023 (September; attackers were active for days before disclosure)
    
        
    Threat actor
    Scattered Spider, a group of English-speaking social-engineering specialists, working with the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware operation
    
        
    Technique used
    T1598.004 Spearphishing Voice (help-desk vishing), followed by credential use against identity systems
    
        
    Outcome
    Credential and MFA reset obtained by voice; wide outages for days; reported impact around 100 million USD; data theft claims made by the attackers
    
      
    
      
    Timeline
    
      
    
        
    
          
    WhenWhat happenedDefender view
    
          
            
    Before the callThe group researched the company, its help desk, and employee identity details through public sourcesReconnaissance leaves almost no internal trace; OSINT hygiene and data-broker monitoring are the only early levers
    
            
    Day 0The attacker phoned the outsourced IT help desk impersonating an employee and requested a password resetA phone-channel reset ticket exists; callback verification was not strong enough to stop it
    
            
    Minutes laterThe attacker obtained the reset credentials and MFA enrollment for the impersonated identityIdP logs would show reset plus factor events in a short window — the chain in Rule 1
    
            
    Same windowThe group reached identity infrastructure, including Okta, and worked toward broader accessNew sessions from unfamiliar contexts appear in IdP sign-in logs right after the reset chain
    
            
    Following hoursSystems were disrupted; the company took services offline to contain the threatSudden shutdowns are a containment decision, visible as config and service changes
    
            
    Days 1–10Outages hit hotel keys, casino floors, websites, and payment systems while recovery ran in stagesAvailability loss dominates; identity evidence must be preserved during the chaos
    
            
    Weeks laterRecovery completed; the company disclosed an estimated impact of about 100 million USD; attackers claimed stolen guest dataPost-incident reviews rebuilt the chain from IdP, telephony, and ticket logs
    
              
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Attack chain
    One voice call, one impersonated employee, one compliant reset. The group used public information to sound authentic, called the help desk, and turned identity recovery flows into an entry path. No malware was needed to start.
    
        
    Detection point
    The clearest technical signal sat in identity logs: a reset and MFA change completing together for one user. Companies that alert on that chain catch this attack at minute one; companies that do not, learn about it from a slot machine going dark.
    
        
    Containment
    The response included isolating systems and taking services offline to stop spread. Containment at that scale is costly, which is exactly why early identity-chain alerts matter: they buy containment while it is still cheap.
    
        
    Eradication
    Removing the attacker meant clearing their sessions and devices, resetting affected identities again under stricter verification, and reviewing every change made during the intrusion window.
    
        
    Recovery
    Services returned in stages as trust was rebuilt: identity first, then core business systems. Recovery took days to complete across the estate, with manual checks where automation could not be trusted.
    
        
    Impact and consequences
    Reported impact was around 100 million USD for the quarter, plus one-time response costs and regulatory attention over the data theft claims. The reputational lesson was as large as the financial one.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Team response and communication
    
        
    The security team coordinated with executives, external incident responders, and law enforcement while engineering teams worked through staged recovery. Customers and partners received service-status updates as systems returned. Inside, help-desk staff were briefed fast, because the same identity could be attacked again. The public communication stayed factual and avoided naming individuals, which kept focus on service recovery.
    
      
    
      
    Lessons learned
    
      
      
        
    • Verify identities with a callback to a number already on file, never one offered during the call.
      • 
        
    • Apply least privilege to reset rights: agents should reset ordinary users, not VIPs, without extra approval.
      • 
        
    • Alert on the reset-plus-MFA-enrollment chain as a critical event, and join it to the ticket that caused it.
      • 
        
    • Keep CDR and IdP log retention long enough to reconstruct the call-to-reset window after an incident.
      • 
        
    • Run help-desk drills with realistic vishing scripts so the first real call is not the first attempt.
      • 
        
    • Treat outsourced help desks as in-scope for identity controls and monitoring.
      • 
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    7
    Mitigation and Defense
    
      
    You cannot patch a phone call. Mitigation for voice phishing is mostly process and identity design: make verification strong at the help desk, make weak MFA flows impossible, and make sure the calls that do happen leave records. The eight control areas below cover the full estate.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    Policy and people
    
          
      
            
    • Publish a rule everyone can repeat: IT will never ask for a password or MFA code.
      • 
            
    • Run vishing drills against staff and the help desk, with consent and after-action notes.
      • 
            
    • Define an approved verification script for phone requests and audit it quarterly.
      • 
            
    • Reward reports of suspicious calls, not only reports of email.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Identity
    
          
      
            
    • Require phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys or hardware keys) for admins and help-desk agents first.
      • 
            
    • Remove phone-call and SMS factors for sensitive roles where regulation allows.
      • 
            
    • Gate VIP resets behind a second approver or a video check.
      • 
            
    • Force re-authentication for every help-desk-initiated change.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Endpoint
    
          
      
            
    • Keep device compliance signals flowing into the IdP so stolen passwords alone cannot sign in.
      • 
            
    • Block enrollment of unmanaged devices as MFA factors.
      • 
            
    • Patch softphones and calling clients like any other software.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Network
    
          
      
            
    • Restrict the DDI range so external trunks cannot present internal caller IDs.
      • 
            
    • Ask your carrier about caller-ID attestation (STIR/SHAKEN — a caller-ID signing standard used by North American carriers).
      • 
            
    • Segment telephony gateways away from general user networks.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Cloud and SaaS
    
          
      
            
    • Turn on CDR export for Teams Phone or your calling platform before you need it.
      • 
            
    • Set conditional access to require compliant devices for MFA enrollment portals.
      • 
            
    • Review which SaaS apps allow help-desk-triggered resets, and tighten each one.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Logging
    
          
      
            
    • Ship IdP audit, MFA, sign-in, CDR, and ticket logs to the SIEM with 12-month retention where possible.
      • 
            
    • Record callback verification fields on every identity ticket.
      • 
            
    • Keep clocks synchronized so call, ticket, and identity events join cleanly.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Monitoring
    
          
      
            
    • Deploy the five rules from Section 5.5 and tune them monthly.
      • 
            
    • Alert on reset-plus-factor chains as critical, with page-on-call routing.
      • 
            
    • Track vishing reports as a metric, including near-misses.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Backup and response
    
          
      
            
    • Rehearse identity-centric recovery: revoke sessions, clear factors, and re-enroll users at scale.
      • 
            
    • Write the vishing runbook before the incident, with phone, IdP, and ticket steps.
      • 
            
    • Pre-agree who may order a service shutdown so containment is not improvised.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Home and small office
    
        
      
          
    • Hang up and call back on the number printed on the card, bill, or official website — never the number the caller gave you.
      • 
          
    • Never read a code to anyone who called you; banks and IT teams do not need it.
      • 
          
    • Use an authenticator app or passkey instead of SMS codes where you can.
      • 
          
    • Treat urgent demands about money or accounts as a reason to slow down, not speed up.
      • 
          
    • Report scam attempts to your carrier and national reporting service so patterns get blocked.
      • 
        
    
      
    
      
    Prioritized actions
    
      
    
        
    
          
    PriorityActionEffortOwnerExpected risk reduction
    
          
            
    1Require callback verification for every phone-channel reset and MFA changeLowHelp-desk managerHigh — removes the main path used in the biggest incidents
    
            
    2Alert on reset plus MFA enrollment chains in the IdPLowSOC engineerHigh — detects the attack minutes after success
    
            
    3Move admins to phishing-resistant MFAMediumIdentity teamHigh — kills code-harvesting for the most valuable accounts

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices
    
      
    Teams defending against voice attacks tend to repeat the same dozen errors. This table names each mistake, the symptom you will notice first, the root cause, the immediate fix, and the prevention that keeps it from returning.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    MistakeSymptomRoot causeFixPrevention
    
          
            
    Trusting caller ID as identity proofAgents comply because "the number matched"Assuming the phone network verifies numbersRewrite verification scripts to ignore displayed numbers entirelyState in every training that caller ID is a display, not a credential
    
            
    Verifying with data the attacker can findReset approved after the caller recites an employee IDUsing public or breached facts as secretsSwitch to callback verification against numbers already on fileReview scripts yearly against what is publicly known about staff
    
            
    No reset-then-enroll alertIncidents discovered by outage, not by alarmEach event was watched alone, never joinedDeploy the Rule 1 chain alert and page on itMap identity events into chains during control design, not after incidents
    
            
    Missing CDR retentionAnalysts cannot find the call that caused the resetTelephony logs were never considered security dataEnable CDR export now and backfill what the platform keepsAdd telephony to the logging standard with an owner and retention
    
            
    Alerting on every external callAnalysts silence the rule within a weekFalse-positive problem: the rule matches all inbound callsNarrow to spoof-shaped and burst-shaped patterns like Rules 3–5Baseline normal call volumes before choosing thresholds
    
            
    Alert fatigue from MFA noiseReal harvest events drown in push-alert spamWeak-tuning problem: default thresholds, no context filtersTune with device, traveler, and maintenance context; group burstsReview MFA alert quality monthly with a named owner
    
            
    No telephony log ownerNobody notices the CDR export silently stoppedOwnership-gap problem: phone systems sit between IT and securityAssign the telephony platform to a named engineer with a monitoring checkKeep a source-to-owner matrix and test each feed quarterly
    
            
    Treating the outsourced help desk as out of scopeThe weakest verification step is the one you never seeContract assumed security was includedExtend identity controls, ticket fields, and drills to vendor agentsPut verification requirements into the contract and audit them
    
            
    Believing MFA makes vishing harmless"We have MFA" ends the risk discussionNot modeling the human handing over the codeReplace SMS/voice factors for sensitive roles and monitor their useTest MFA flows with a red-team call each quarter
    
            
    Voicemail lures ignored as spamRepeated contacts to the same staff go unreportedNo reporting channel for callsAdd a one-click call-report flow next to the email-report buttonMeasure report volume; zero reports usually means zero awareness
    
            
    Evidence lost during outage responsePost-incident review cannot rebuild the chainLogs rotated or systems reimaged too fastFreeze and export IdP, CDR, and ticket data at incident startPut evidence preservation as step one of the vishing runbook
    
            
    One-and-done trainingStaff pass the yearly quiz, then still read out codesTraining without rehearsalReplace some e-learning with live call drills and feedbackTrack drill failure rate as the metric that matters

    • Check the reset-plus-enroll alert queue first.
      • 
            
    • Scan overnight inbound-call bursts for new spoof patterns.
      • 
            
    • Read new phone-channel identity tickets for missing callback fields.
      • 
            
    • Confirm the CDR feed arrived on schedule.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Weekly habits
    
          
      
            
    • Review all MFA factor changes and spot-check three with callbacks.
      • 
            
    • Hunt for caller IDs that appeared in more than one unrelated ticket.
      • 
            
    • Share one real vishing example with the help-desk team.
      • 
            
    • Tune one noisy rule and record why.
      • 
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Monthly habits
    
          
      
            
    • Run a live vishing drill and score the verification script.
      • 
            
    • Re-check actor tradecraft notes for new voice tactics.
      • 
            
    • Test rule logic against replayed incident data.
      • 
            
    • Review telephony log retention and source ownership.

    Cheat Sheet and Quick Reference
    
      
    The one-screen version of this page: key facts, the five detections that matter most, and the response checklist to keep next to the phone.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    Key facts
    
          
    
            
    ATT&CK ID
    T1598.004 (sub-technique of T1598)
    
            
    Tactic
    Reconnaissance (TA0043)
    
            
    Channel
    Phone calls and voicemail, often with spoofed caller ID
    
            
    Typical asks
    MFA codes, password resets, payment approvals, staff details
    
            
    Best evidence
    IdP and MFA logs, CDRs, help-desk tickets
    
            
    Best defense
    Callback verification plus phishing-resistant MFA
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Top detections
    
          
      
            
    1. Password reset plus MFA enrollment for one user in a short window (IdP).
      2. 
            
    2. MFA satisfied by phone call or OTP from a new country or device (IdP).
      3. 
            
    3. Inbound PSTN call displaying an internal or executive caller ID (CDR).
      4. 
            
    4. One external caller reaching many employees within an hour (CDR).
      5. 
            
    5. Short-call bursts that match voicemail and callback lure campaigns (CDR).
      6. 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Response checklist
    
        
      
          
    • Ask the employee for the call time, the displayed number, and exactly what was shared.
      • 
          
    • Pull IdP events for the user in a two-hour window around the call.
      • 
          
    • Pull CDR entries for the user's extension in the same window.
      • 
          
    • Find the help-desk ticket and record who verified what.
      • 
          
    • Revoke sessions and remove attacker-enrolled MFA devices immediately.
      • 
          
    • Re-enroll the user after a callback to the number on file.
      • 
          
    • Hunt the caller number and reset pattern across all users for 48 hours.
      • 
          
    • Log the case, brief the help desk, and update the drill script.
