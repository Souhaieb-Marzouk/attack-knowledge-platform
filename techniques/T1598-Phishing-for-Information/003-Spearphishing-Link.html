MITRE ATT&CK · RECONNAISSANCE

Spearphishing Link T1598.003

An attacker sends an email or chat message with a link. The link opens a page built to collect information: a copy of your sign-in screen, a fake form, or a fake job portal. This guide shows how the trick works, what it leaves in your logs, and how to detect it step by step.

Tactic · Reconnaissance Platforms · PRE (pre-attack; the click happens in mail clients and browsers) Data sources · Network traffic content (proxy/DNS), Email gateway logs, IdP (identity provider) logs Parent · T1598

What it is

A message with a link that leads to a fake page built to collect details from people, not to install malware.

Where it fits

Reconnaissance — before Initial Access

Primary target

People with useful accounts, data, or answers: staff, job applicants, and partner contacts.

Main risk

Credentials, MFA (multi-factor authentication) approvals, and personal or company data handed to the attacker through a web form.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    This page explains T1598.003, Spearphishing Link, in plain language for people new to security work. You will learn what the attacker wants to collect, how fake pages fool people, and which logs show the click. The focus is practical detection, not theory.

    What this technique is

    The attacker sends an email or chat message that contains a link. The link opens a website the attacker controls. Often it is a copy of a real sign-in page, a fake survey, or a fake job application portal.

    The goal is to make the target type information: a password, an approval on an MFA (multi-factor authentication — a second check like a phone prompt) request, or personal and company details. No malicious file is needed. The story and the page do all the work. The mail may also ask the reader to copy and paste the link instead of clicking it, which defeats simple link scanning.

    Why it matters

    A link is cheap to send and easy to personalize. It slips past tools that only scan file attachments. The collected data feeds the attacker's next moves: better fake stories, direct account takeover, or resale of the details.

    Because no malware runs inside your network, tools that hunt for hostile code see nothing. If your team only looks for infections, this technique stays invisible until the data is already gone.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Reconnaissance→Initial Access→Execution→Persistence

    T1598.003 lives in the Reconnaissance tactic (TA0043). The attacker is still studying you; nothing has broken in yet. A click caught here is the cheapest catch there is, because nothing inside the company has been touched.

    CompareT1598.003 Spearphishing Link (Reconnaissance)T1566.002 Spearphishing Link (Initial Access)

    The same click serves two very different plans. When the page only collects, it is T1598.003. When the page is a doorway into your systems, it is T1566.002. Watch the behavior after the click, not just the click itself.

    Terms in this guide

    Spearphishing link

    A message aimed at chosen people that carries a link, not an attachment. The link points to a site the attacker controls.

    Elicitation

    Gently pulling information out of someone through questions, forms, or friendly chat. The target often never notices what they gave away.

    Pretext

    The fake story that makes the request feel normal: a payroll check, a parking permit, a job interview, an IT verification.

    Lookalike domain

    A web address that imitates a real one, such as arclight-sso[.]com instead of the true sso.arclight-dynamics.com. Dots inside brackets mean the name is defanged so nothing here is clickable.

    IdP

    Identity provider. The system that checks your username and password, such as Microsoft Entra ID or Okta, and issues the session that apps trust.

    MFA

    Multi-factor authentication. A second check beyond the password. Useful, but reverse-proxy kits can still trick users into approving it.

    3
    Red Team Perspective
    
      
    This section explains how attackers plan and run a link-based collection campaign. It is written for defenders. If you understand the choices an attacker makes, you can predict the noise the campaign leaves behind. Then you can place your sensors where the noise appears first.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    When and why attackers use it
    
          
    Links are the cheapest way to collect. There is no exploit to buy, no malware to maintain, and one template can serve a hundred personalized pages. Attackers choose this technique when the goal is a list of facts. Examples: who works where, what tools you run, or which credentials might work later.
    
          
    Public reporting shows the pattern across very different actors: DPRK-aligned (Democratic People’s Republic of Korea — North Korea) clusters run job-offer link lures at engineers, Russia-aligned groups such as COLDRIVER run fake document portals at NGOs (non-governmental organizations) and universities, and commodity actors mass-produce lookalike brand pages. The common thread is collection, not infection.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Preconditions
    
          
    The attacker needs five things before the first message leaves:
    
          
      
            
    • Target research — names, roles, and projects taken from public sources (see T1589).
      • 
            
    • A pretext — a story that fits the target's week: benefits renewal, hiring, an IT check.
      • 
            
    • Lookalike infrastructure — a freshly registered domain, a TLS (transport layer security — the padlock protocol) certificate, and a cloned page or a kit.
      • 
            
    • A delivery channel — mail, a chat DM (direct message), SMS, or a QR (quick response — the square barcode) image.
      • 
            
    • Patience — pages stay up for hours or days, then vanish before blocklists catch them.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    How it is done
    
      
      
        
    1. Choose the target and the pretext — The attacker matches the story to the person: recruiters approach engineers, HR portals approach staff, "mailbox verification" approaches everyone.
      2. 
        
    2. Register the lookalike domain — They buy a typosquat (a misspelling of a real name), add a hyphen, or pick a cheap new TLD (top-level domain — the ending like .com or .top). Then they issue a certificate so the padlock shows.
      3. 
        
    3. Build the collection page — They clone the real SSO login pixel by pixel, or deploy a phishing kit. Known kits include EvilProxy and Evilginx2; some sit behind a reverse proxy that relays the real login and steals the session after MFA. Some pages use browser-in-the-browser (BitB) — a fake pop-up window with a drawn address bar — to hide the real URL.
      4. 
        
    4. Prepare the link — Direct URL, shortened link, a chain of redirects, or a QR code inside a PDF so scanners never read the text. They may also disguise the host itself: an integer or hex address, or text before “@” that browsers ignore — for example hxxp://google[.]com@1157586937, where the browser really visits the number 1157586937, the decimal form of an IP address.
      5. 
        
    5. Send the lure — Small batches from lookalike sender domains, sometimes with a personalized URL per target, such as firstname.lastname.arclight-careers[.]com. A hidden tracking pixel (a tiny invisible image in the mail) can also tell the attacker who opened the message and record the reader’s IP address.
      6. 
        
    6. Collect — Submitted passwords, personal details, and uploaded files land in the kit's control panel. Session cookies (the small tokens that keep you signed in) are saved for replay.
      7. 
        
    7. Rotate and clean — Pages go down quickly, domains that filters have flagged are abandoned, and the next wave is staged on fresh infrastructure.
      8. 
      
    
      
    Where it applies
    
      
    
        
    Email
    The classic channel. Benefits enrollment, payroll checks, and "re-verify your mailbox" lures land in every industry. Gateway logs make this the easiest channel to scope after the fact.
    
        
    Chat and social DMs
    LinkedIn, Teams, and Slack messages carry links too, and external messages there are logged far less carefully than email. Job-offer campaigns live here.
    
        
    SMS and mobile messaging
    Short texts with short links ("Your parcel is waiting", "HR message for you") push the click onto a personal phone, outside your proxy and DNS view. ATT&CK tracks this mobile variant as T1660 (Phishing).
    
        
    QR codes
    A QR image in a PDF or on a printed poster turns the URL into pixels. Text-based link scanners see nothing; the phone camera does the clicking.
    
      
    
      
    
        Lab only
        
    You can study the mechanics safely in an isolated lab. Run GoPhish (an open-source phishing simulator) together with Mailpit (a local test-mail catcher) in Docker on one machine. Send only to lab test mailboxes, never to real people, and put [LAB TEST] in every subject line. The command below starts both tools on localhost only.
    
        
      
    
      
    Common variations
    
      
    
        
    Lookalike SSO portal
    A pixel-perfect copy of your login page on a fresh domain. It captures the password, shows a fake error, and stays quiet. Pure and simple credential harvesting.
    
        
    Reverse-proxy kit (AiTM)
    An adversary-in-the-middle kit (AiTM — a relay that sits between user and real site) forwards every request to the real login. MFA completes for real, and the kit keeps the session cookie it just harvested.
    
        
    Quishing
    A QR-code phishing lure. The hostile URL hides inside an image, so text scanners pass the message and the camera becomes the browser.
    
        
    Shortened and chained links
    A public shortener or a redirect chain hides the final host. The attacker can also swap the destination after sending, so the click always works for them and never for your filter.
    
      
    
      
    
        Warning
        
    Mistakes attackers make that create noise:
    
        
      
          
    • They register the domain only days before sending, so Certificate Transparency and new-domain feeds flag it early.
      • 
          
    • They reuse the same hosting providers and IP ranges that reputation lists already distrust.
      • 
          
    • Phishing kits ship with identical HTML, page titles, and favicon hashes, which makes clones easy to fingerprint.
      • 
          
    • Sender domains and reply-to addresses rarely match the real brand exactly — one letter off is still off.
      • 
          
    • Personalized URLs follow one pattern, so a single example reveals the whole recipient list.
      • 
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    4
    Blue Team Perspective
    
      
    Defenders have three honest advantages here. The click must pass your network on the way out. The replay of stolen credentials must pass your IdP on the way in. The lure email itself is a full record of the campaign. This section shows where to look first and what to do in the first hour.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    SignalWhere you see itWhy it helps
    
          
            
    Outbound click to a new or lookalike domainDNS query logs and proxy logsThe earliest reliable signal, often hours before any credential abuse starts
    
            
    Lure email with an external linkEmail gateway logs and message traceGives the full recipient list, sender details, and every URL in one place
    
            
    Password submit (POST) to a hostile hostProxy logs showing network traffic contentSeparates "saw the page" from "typed the password" — a big jump in severity
    
            
    New sign-in from an unknown ASN (autonomous system number — the network’s provider ID) or countryIdP logs (Entra ID, Okta)Shows the attacker turning collected data into access
    
            
    MFA approval the user did not startIdP logsMeans the victim is still influenced; start incident response, not just a password reset
    
              
        
    
      
    
      
    Artifacts this technique leaves behind
    
      
    
        
    The lure email
    Gateway logs hold the sender address, sending IPs, subject, link list, and exact delivery times. This artifact scopes the campaign: every recipient is a case.
    
        
    The click
    DNS and proxy logs hold the hostile host, the requested path, the user agent, and the timestamp. Even a "harmless" click is a pointer to one compromised story.
    
        
    The submit
    Proxy logs show a POST request to /login or /api/auth on the lookalike host. The payload size of a form submit stands out from an image fetch.
    
        
    The replay
    IdP logs show sign-ins from a new ASN, a new device, or a new country. Sometimes an MFA challenge is satisfied with no user request at all.
    
      
    
      
    
        Note
        
    How this fits the attack chain: after collection, the attacker may move to direct pretexting over service platforms (T1598.001) or voice calls that pressure MFA approvals (T1598.004). If the plan shifts from study to entry, the same lookalike infrastructure often returns under T1566 and T1566.002. Harvested brand details also feed impersonation (T1656) — fake executive and help-desk personas built on real facts. Harvested session cookies feed Steal Web Session Cookie (T1539) and Web Session Cookie reuse (T1550.004).
    
      
    
      
    First-hour triage flow
    
      
      
        
    1. Confirm the domain — Check registrar data and certificate history. An age under 30 days, or a certificate issued this week, raises the case to High or Critical.
      2. 
        
    2. Scope the recipients — Search the gateway for the sender, subject, and URL across every mailbox. Build the full list of who received the lure.
      3. 
        
    3. Hunt the click — Search DNS and proxy for the hostile host and its redirect chain. Mark every user who fetched the page and, above all, every user who sent a POST.
      4. 
        
    4. Check identity — For each clicker, review IdP sign-ins, MFA prompts, and new device registrations in the last 24 hours. Treat an unexplained approval as an active intrusion.
      5. 
        
    5. Contain — Block the domain at DNS and proxy, revoke active sessions, force a password reset for submitters, and re-enroll MFA where an approval was harvested.
      6. 
        
    6. Record and report — Save the raw message, defang all indicators, tell the affected users what happened, and send a takedown request to the registrar and the hosting provider.
      7. 
      
    
      
    
        Quick win
        
    Turn on automatic blocking or alerting for domains registered in the last 30 days at your DNS resolver and proxy. It is one rule, it needs almost no tuning, and it catches most link waves hours before public blocklists update.

    Confidence levels
    
          
    High — several credible sources or your own telemetry agree. Act now: block, hunt, and add a detection.
    
          
    Medium — credible reporting but not first-hand. Add a lower-severity alert and watch for your own evidence.
    
          
    Low — a single unconfirmed source. Keep a watchlist entry only; do not build blocking on it.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    How to apply intel
    
          
    Turn indicators into detections with an expiry date, because hostile domains die fast. Turn behavior patterns — kit fingerprints, page styles, pretext wording — into rules that survive the infrastructure churn.
    
          
    Keep a short actor note in your phishing runbook: who targets your sector, with which story. Analysts read runbooks during incidents, not blogs.
    
        
    
      
    
      
    Intel cycle
    
      
      
        
    1. Collect — Pull feeds, vendor posts, ISAC notes, and your own logs into one place.
      2. 
        
    2. Process — Defang, deduplicate, and tag items; drop anything older than its relevance window.
      3. 
        
    3. Analyze — Ask what applies to you: which sector, which pretext, which kit, which confidence level.
      4. 
        
    4. Disseminate — Send a short note with indicators and actions to the SOC, and a plain-language warning to staff if the lure reaches inboxes.
      5. 
        
    5. Feedback — Record which indicators fired and which rules helped; cut the noise and repeat the winners.

    5.4 Logs and Patterns
    
      
    Each threat below gets its log sources, the fields that matter, what normal looks like, and the pattern that should fire an alert. All hostile indicators are defanged — a dot is written as [.] — so nothing in this guide is clickable. Examples use a fictional company, Arclight Dynamics, and documentation IP ranges (203.0.113.0/24, 198.51.100.0/24).
    

      
    Threat 1 — Lookalike SSO login page credential harvest
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Proxy logs (Zscaler-class), DNS query logs, email gateway logs
    
        
    Key fields
    user, url/host, action, method (GET or POST), response bytes (respbytes), DNS qname, response IP, domain age
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Sign-in pages load from a small, stable set of IdP hosts. Users rarely fetch brand-new domains, and password posts only ever go to the real IdP.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A user fetches a domain registered in the last 30 days that mimics the SSO brand, then sends a POST with a credential-sized payload to a login path on that same host.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert when a click to a new or lookalike host is followed by a POST to a login path. Join proxy events with a new-domain feed so the age is part of the alert.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 2 — Reverse-proxy MFA interception kit (session cookie theft)
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    IdP logs (Okta, Entra ID sign-in), proxy logs, DNS query logs
    
        
    Key fields
    eventType, outcome.result, actor.alternateId, client.ipAddress, client.device, client.userAgent, securityContext.asn and isProxy, externalSessionId
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Each user signs in from a small set of networks and known devices, and MFA is satisfied seconds after the user starts the sign-in themselves.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A successful single sign-on with an Unknown device profile from a hosting-provider network, minutes after a real MFA approval elsewhere — or the same session appearing from a second country the same day.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert on successful SSO where the device is unknown and the ASN belongs to a hosting provider. Correlate the user with recent link campaign recipients before paging.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 3 — QR-code "quishing" link in email bypassing link scanners
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Email gateway with QR-code decoding, managed mobile browser or MDM (mobile device management) logs, DNS on the scanning device
    
        
    Key fields
    gateway action, decoded URL, attachment name and type, device OS, user agent, follow-on fetch host
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Inbound mail almost never carries a QR image that decodes to a login page, and staff scan codes only from known vendors such as parking meters or payment terminals.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    An inbound message with a PDF or PNG whose QR decodes to a domain younger than 30 days or to a public shortener. A fetch from a corporate or MDM-managed device follows.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert at the gateway when a decoded QR URL lands on a young domain or a shortener. Add a second, quieter alert when the decoded host is later fetched by a managed device.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 4 — Click on newly registered domain hosting a fake portal
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    DNS resolver logs, proxy logs, new-domain (NRD) feed, Certificate Transparency log monitors
    
        
    Key fields
    qname, response IP, client IP or user, first-seen domain age, certificate age, query type
    
        
    Normal behavior
    The company queries a known set of domains, and genuinely new names appear slowly, mostly from CDN and patch vendors you already know.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    Several managed devices query one brand-new domain within minutes, and the name embeds the company brand or words like sso, login, benefits, or careers.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert when a domain younger than 30 days receives clicks from two or more managed devices, or when one click carries a login-path URL on such a domain.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 5 — Shortened-link lure to fake benefits/HR portal
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Office 365 audit log with Safe Links click events (or gateway click logs), proxy logs for the redirect target
    
        
    Key fields
    Operation, UserId, Url, AppId, ClickType, timestamp; proxy logs add the final host after redirects
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Users click a small set of approved shorteners — corporate tracking links and trusted partners — from known apps during work hours.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A click through a public shortener that no internal campaign uses, whose redirect later lands on an uncategorized or young domain with a login path.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert on URL Click events where the URL is a public shortener outside the approved list, then resolve the redirect through proxy data and judge the final host.
    
      
    
      

      
    5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL
    
      
    Sigma is a YAML rule format that most SIEMs (security information and event management systems) can compile, and SPL is Splunk's search language. Each of the five threats gets one rule and one query. Indicators stay defanged inside the YAML — remove the [.] brackets before you deploy, as noted under each rule.
    

      
    Rule 1 — Entra ID sign-in from an unexpected country
    
      
    This rule covers Threat 1 and runs at Critical severity: after a lookalike page collects a password, the replay usually shows up here first.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectNotes
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsindex=aad, sourcetype="azure:aad:signin"; fields arrive under the properties.* prefix (status.errorCode, location.countryOrRegion, userPrincipalName). If your ingestion flattens them, adjust the field names in both rule and query.
    
            
    False positivesTravel, VPN (virtual private network) exit nodes, new offices, and any successful sign-in from an unlisted country will fire until the list is tuned.
    
            
    TuningMove the approved countries into a lookup table (lookup country_allowlist country OUTPUT allowed) and review it quarterly with HR and IT.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 2 — Okta SSO from an unknown device on a hosting network
    
      
    This rule covers Threat 2 at Critical severity: a reverse-proxy kit replays a stolen session, and Okta records the sign — an Unknown device on a proxy or hosting network.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectNotes
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsindex=okta, sourcetype="OktaIM2:log"; requires eventType, outcome.result, client.device, and securityContext.isProxy fields, which the Okta System Log API provides.
    
            
    False positivesUnmanaged but legitimate new devices, IT cloud browser tools, and privacy proxies used by staff.
    
            
    TuningPage only when the user also appears in the last-7-day lure recipient list from the mail index; everything else stays a low-severity review item.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 3 — Gateway decodes a QR code pointing to a shortener
    
      
    This rule covers Threat 3 at High severity: the gateway QR defense caught the quishing lure before a person ever scanned it.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectNotes
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsindex=email, sourcetype="mimecast:log" normalized to a QR-protection event with qr_decoded, decoded_url, attachment_name. Map the names to your gateway's real QR feature if they differ.
    
            
    False positivesMarketing mail with shortened tracking links; partner surveys behind shorteners.
    
            
    TuningJoin decoded_url to your new-domain feed and require an age under 30 days for paging; shortener-only hits stay low severity.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 4 — Managed browser fetches a login page on a new domain
    
      
    This rule covers Threat 4 at High severity: the proxy category "Newly Registered Domains" does most of the work, and a login path makes it interesting.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectNotes
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsindex=proxy, sourcetype="zscalernss-web" with the built-in "Newly Registered Domains" category in urlcat and the user field populated. The SPL additionally requires two or more distinct users on the same URL (where users >= 2); the Sigma fires on a single fetch, so align whichever side you want to be authoritative. Other proxies expose a similar NRD flag; rename as needed.
    
            
    False positivesGenuine new SaaS vendors, launch-day marketing sites, and vendor domains used by procurement.
    
            
    TuningKeep the two-user threshold, and add an OR branch for any single click whose URL path contains login, sso, verify, or benefits.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 5 — Click through a public URL shortener
    
      
    This rule covers Threat 5 at Medium severity: Safe Links click telemetry shows the shortener hop, and the redirect target decides how serious it is.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectNotes
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsindex=o365, sourcetype="ms:o365:audit" with Defender for Office 365 "URL Click" events enabled. Without Safe Links licensing this event does not exist; use gateway click logs instead. Some Microsoft 365 exports name the same event UrlClick; align the string with your tenant’s data.
    
            
    False positivesApproved campaigns that use shorteners; note that the t\.co pattern can also match unrelated hosts ending in .co, so anchor the host before paging.
    
            
    TuningResolve the redirect with proxy data and alert on the final host only when it is young or uncategorized; allowlist corporate shorteners in a lookup.
    
              
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    6
    Real-World Case Study
    
      
    This is an educational reconstruction of Operation In(ter)ception, based on public reporting by ESET in June 2021. It describes organizations and roles, never individuals, and no instruction here helps an attacker. The campaign is a textbook T1598.003 case: job-offer lures, personalized fake sites, and a long form built to collect personal data — with no confirmed malware stage.
    
      
    
        
    Victim sector
    Aerospace and military-drone manufacturers, primarily in Europe and the Middle East
    
        
    Year
    2021 (campaign reported by ESET in June 2021)
    
        
    Threat actor
    Unknown group; ESET tracks the campaign as Operation In(ter)ception
    
        
    Technique used
    T1598.003 Spearphishing Link — job-offer lures with links to fake recruitment portals
    
        
    Outcome
    Extensive personal and professional data harvested from targeted staff; no confirmed malware delivery or intrusion reported at the time
    
      
    
      
    
        
    
          
    WhenWhat happenedDefender view
    
          
            
    Weeks before contactThe group built LinkedIn (a professional social network) personas copied from real aerospace recruiters, with stolen photos and historiesFake profiles live outside your company; watch what employees accept and what is public about them
    
            
    Initial contactTargets received LinkedIn messages and emails offering attractive job openings, with a link to "apply" or "see the offer"The gateway logs the email with a young link domain; chat platforms log little, so staff reports matter
    
            
    The clickEach target opened a personalized URL — the fake site mirrored pages built per target, often as name-based subdomainsProxy and DNS logs show one fetch per user to an uncategorized, days-old host
    
            
    Multi-stage formThe portal ran a staged application: create a password, then personal details, then professional details, then questions about current and past employersRepeated POSTs to the same host; each stage is one more proxy record with a form-sized payload
    
            
    "Encrypt your CV" stepThe final stage asked the target to upload their CV in an archive with a password the site supplied — harvesting the file itselfAn outbound upload to an uncategorized host; DLP (data loss prevention — tools that spot sensitive data leaving) can catch it on managed devices
    
            
    Collection completeThe persona went quiet, and the personalized pages were taken down quicklyNo internal logs exist for the quiet phase; the outbound trail is all that remains
    
            
    June 2021ESET published the campaign analysis with domains and page descriptionsDefenders can retro-hunt gateway, proxy, and DNS logs for the published indicators
    
            
    AftermathTargets who submitted data faced a real risk of follow-on pretexting and voice-based pressure campaignsRotate anything submitted, warn the affected staff, and add the domains to blocklists
    
              
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Attack chain
    Recruiter persona on a social platform, then a job offer by message or email. The link opened a personalized fake portal that walked the target through a long form. A final "encrypt your CV" step collected an uploaded document. Collection was the end goal.
    
        
    Detection point
    The strongest point was outbound network telemetry: personalized name-based subdomains, days-old registrations, and repeated form posts from one user to one host. Gateway logs confirmed the recipients. Identity logs stayed quiet — exactly why click telemetry matters for this technique.
    
        
    Containment
    For a recon campaign, containment means the data: block the reported domains, find every recipient, and treat submitters as affected. Sessions and passwords stay safe when nothing was replayed, but each submitter needs a watch on their identity events.
    
        
    Eradication
    With no confirmed foothold, eradication focused on the lure infrastructure: takedown requests to registrars, blocklist additions, and reporting the fake recruiter accounts to the platform. The persona accounts, not just the domains, had to go.
    
        
    Recovery
    Recovery meant people: refresher training built around the real lure, a one-click report button, and honest briefings that a CV upload is a reportable incident, not an embarrassment. Monitoring continued for voice and service-platform follow-ups.
    
        
    Impact and consequences
    Extensive personal data of skilled staff left the companies — names, phones, employment histories, and CVs. That inventory feeds future targeted campaigns and recruiting attacks. The reputational cost lands on security teams asked why a job ad knew so much about them.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Team response and communication
    
        
    The SOC told affected staff quickly and without blame: what was sent, what the fake site asked, and what to check. Recruiters and HR got a version of the story too, because they see the same personas first. Leadership received a one-page summary with the numbers that matter — recipients, clicks, submitters — and the takedown status. Every message used defanged indicators so nobody re-clicked the link in the briefing itself.
    
      
    
      
    Lessons learned
    
      
      
        
    • Treat job-offer lures as a real campaign type, not as harmless spam, even when no payload exists.
      • 
        
    • Watch Certificate Transparency and new-domain feeds for name-pattern subdomains that mirror your staff.
      • 
        
    • Log and alert on outbound form posts to uncategorized hosts — the form is the weapon.
      • 
        
    • Train staff that a real recruiter never needs your work password or a "secure CV" portal.
      • 
        
    • Keep the reporting button one click away in both mail and chat, and thank reporters fast.
      • 
        
    • Retro-hunt after every published vendor report; yesterday's indicators are in last month's logs.
      • 
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    7
    Mitigation and Defense
    
      
    No single control stops a link, so the defense is layered: make the fake page hard to reach, make the stolen data hard to use, and make reporting easy. The eight control areas below cover the full loop from policy to response.
    
      
    
        
    Policy and people
    • Run phishing drills quarterly and include link-only and QR lures, not just attachments.
    • Add a one-click report button to mail and chat, and never penalize a reporter.
    • Define which personal data may never be typed into a web form, and say it plainly.
    • Brief HR and recruiters on fake-recruiter personas every year.
    
        
    Identity
    • Deploy phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 security keys — hardware keys — or platform passkeys) for admins and executives first.
    • Set conditional access by location, device state, and risk level at the IdP.
    • Keep session lifetimes short for sensitive apps so a stolen cookie expires sooner.
    • Block legacy authentication protocols that skip modern checks.
    
        
    Endpoint
    • Run EDR (endpoint detection and response — device-level security tooling) on laptops and managed phones.
    • Turn on browser isolation or URL reputation warnings for uncategorized sites.
    • Alert on new MFA device enrollments and on clipboard or autofill use on unknown domains.
    • Keep managed browsers updated; old parsers miss new kit tricks.
    
        
    Network
    • Filter DNS with a newly-registered-domain category and log every query.
    • Use TLS inspection where law and policy allow, so proxy logs carry URL paths.
    • Block confirmed lookalikes at proxy and DNS the same hour you find them.
    • Restrict outbound access from sensitive segments to approved destinations.
    
        
    Cloud and SaaS
    • Enable URL rewriting and click telemetry (Safe Links or equivalent) for mail and Teams.
    • Turn on QR-code protection so images get decoded and judged like links.
    • Show an external-sender banner on every outside message.
    • Quarantine PDFs whose QR decodes to a young domain until an analyst reviews them.
    
        
    Logging
    • Centralize gateway, proxy, DNS, and IdP logs in the SIEM with time sync.
    • Keep at least 90 days hot and one year archived; campaigns are retro-hunted for weeks.
    • Preserve the raw message file for every reported lure.
    • Log chat and messaging platforms too, or the lure simply moves there.
    
        
    Monitoring
    • Alert on clicks to domains younger than 30 days, and on password POSTs to them.
    • Alert on sign-ins from a new country or hosting ASN within 24 hours of a campaign.
    • Alert on MFA bursts and approvals the user did not start.
    • Tune the rules monthly and record every change and its reason.
    
        
    Backup and response
    • Run a tabletop exercise for the "credentials harvested" scenario twice a year.
    • Pre-approve session revocation and password reset playbooks so no one waits for a meeting.
    • Keep registrar and hosting abuse contacts ready for takedown requests.
    • After a wave, hunt the same indicators across all logs, then refresh training with the real example.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Home and small office
    
        
      
          
    • Let the browser's password manager fill logins — it will refuse on the wrong site, which is the warning sign.
      • 
          
    • Use passkeys where a service offers them; they cannot be typed into a fake page.
      • 
          
    • Never scan a QR code from an unexpected email or flyer; type the known address instead.
      • 
          
    • Read the address bar spelling before signing in anywhere, especially on a phone.
      • 
          
    • Report scam messages to your provider's report address and delete them.
      •

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices
    
      
    Most link-detection programs fail in the same few ways: the wrong log, the loud rule, the ownerless domain. The table below lists twelve mistakes with their symptoms, root causes, fixes, and prevention. Some are general program failures; the rest are specific to spearphishing links.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    MistakeSymptomRoot causeFixPrevention

    Nobody owns takedown requestsThe lookalike domain stays live for weeks and keeps catching clicksNo owner or runbook step for registrar abuse reportsAssign a takedown owner with registrar and hosting contactsTest the takedown path once a year with a harmless lab domain

    Cheat Sheet and Quick Reference
    
      
    The page in one screen: the facts, the five detections, the response checklist, and every official link. Print it or pin it in the SOC channel.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    Key facts
    
          
    
            
    ATT&CK ID
    T1598.003 — Spearphishing Link (sub-technique of T1598)
    
            
    Tactic
    Reconnaissance (TA0043) — collect information, not access
    
            
    Key contrast
    T1566.002 sends the same link to gain access; T1598.003 sends it to study you
    
            
    Best log sources
    Proxy and DNS (network traffic content), email gateway, IdP logs
    
            
    Earliest signal
    A click to a domain younger than 30 days
    
            
    First response action
    Block the domain, then scope recipients, then check identity events
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Top detections
    
          
      
            
    1. Click to a newly registered domain, especially two or more users on one host.
      2. 
            
    2. Password POST to a login path on an uncategorized or young host.
      3. 
            
    3. Successful IdP sign-in from a new country or a hosting ASN after a campaign.
      4. 
            
    4. Okta or Entra session replay: Unknown device, proxy network, minutes after a real MFA.
      5. 
            
    5. Gateway QR decode or mail click through a public shortener to a young domain.
      6. 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Response checklist
    
        
      
          
    • Confirm the domain age and certificate history for every reported link.
      • 
          
    • Search the gateway for sender, subject, and URL to build the full recipient list.
      • 
          
    • Search DNS and proxy for clicks and password POSTs from all users.
      • 
          
    • Review IdP sign-ins, MFA prompts, and device enrollments for every clicker.
      • 
          
    • Block the lookalike domain at DNS and proxy, and request takedown.
      • 
          
    • Revoke sessions and force password resets for every submitter.
      • 
          
    • Defang and record all indicators in the case file, then share them defanged.
      • 
          
    • Tell affected staff what happened, and feed the real example into the next training.
