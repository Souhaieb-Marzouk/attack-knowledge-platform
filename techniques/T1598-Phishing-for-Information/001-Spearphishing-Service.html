MITRE ATT&CK · RECONNAISSANCE

Spearphishing Service T1598.001

An attacker creates a fake identity on a third-party service — a social network, a chat platform, or a personal email provider — and uses it to ask your employees for sensitive information. The goal is answers, not infections. Detection is hard because the attacker's platform keeps the logs, so user reporting becomes your main sensor.

Tactic · Reconnaissance Platforms · PRE (pre-attack; third-party services such as social media and personal webmail) Data sources · Application logs (SaaS/SSO), email logs, DLP alerts, user reports Parent · T1598

What it is

Phishing for information through a service run by another company, such as LinkedIn, X, or a personal email provider.

Where it fits

Reconnaissance — before Initial Access

Primary target

Employees with useful knowledge: engineers, recruiters, support staff, executives.

Main risk

Org charts, tool names, badge rules, and schedules handed to a stranger.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    This section explains the technique in plain words. You will learn what spearphishing through a third-party service is, how it differs from phishing that steals access, and which words this guide uses.

    What this technique is

    Spearphishing is a phishing message written for one person or a small team. In this sub-technique the attacker does not send it from a lookalike mail server. They use a third-party service: an online service run by another company, one you do not control. Examples are LinkedIn, X, Telegram, a personal email provider, or an in-app chat.

    The attacker builds a persona (a fake identity with a photo, work history, and contacts) and starts a conversation. The message asks questions instead of pushing malware. The answers are the payload (the thing the attacker really wants).

    Why it matters

    Answered questions leak facts that make every later attack sharper: team names, tool names, on-call schedules, badge rules, and reporting lines. Attackers use these details to write perfect lures and to impersonate your staff.

    The attack is also hard to see. The platform that carries the conversation keeps most of the evidence, and it sits outside your network. You must lean on compensating signals (extra signals that make up for the missing platform logs): user reports, data loss prevention (DLP) alerts, and SaaS (software as a service) audit logs.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Phishing for information happens early, before any malware or stolen login. The answers collected here shape the intrusion attempt that follows.

    Reconnaissance→Initial Access→Execution→Exfiltration

    MITRE ATT&CK lists this technique under Reconnaissance (tactic TA0043). The technique itself does not touch your systems. That is exactly why detection needs people and process, not only machines.

    T1598.001 vs T1566 — information or access?

    AspectT1598.001 — Spearphishing ServiceT1566 — Phishing

    Typical ask"Which team runs the badge system?" "What build tools do you use?""Confirm your password" "Open this invoice" "Approve this MFA (multi-factor authentication) prompt"

    The closest Initial Access cousin is T1566.003 — Spearphishing via Service: the same third-party channel, but the goal there is delivering a payload, not collecting answers.

    Terms in this guide

    Spearphishing

    A phishing message written for a specific person, using details that make it feel personal and real.

    Elicitation

    Getting information from people through normal-looking conversation, so the target does not notice they are answering a stranger's questions.

    Pretext

    The fake story that explains the contact: a job offer, a support ticket, a journalist request. The story is the weapon.

    Third-party service

    An online service run by another company: social networks, chat apps, personal email providers. Your logs there are limited or missing.

    SaaS

    Software as a Service: applications you use in a browser and rent from a provider, such as Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace.

    DLP

    Data Loss Prevention: tools that spot and block sensitive data as it leaves your environment, in mail, uploads, and screenshots.

    3
    Red Team Perspective
    
      
    This section describes how attackers plan and run information-gathering contact through third-party services. It is a planning-level view, written for defenders, and rebuilt from public reporting. It contains no instructions against real targets.
    

      
    
        
    
          
    When and why attackers use it
    
          
    Attackers pick this technique when they want facts that public sources do not hold: internal tools, staffing, schedules, and appetite for risk. A third-party service gives them a free, trusted channel with no spam filters and no logging on your side.
    
          
    It also lets them choose the exact person to talk to. They often run it in parallel with email lures, so one target may meet two versions of the same stranger.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Preconditions
    
          
    The attacker needs surprisingly little:
    
          
      
            
    • A target with public profiles on social networks or forums.
      • 
            
    • A free account on the same service, warmed up for a few days.
      • 
            
    • Time — rapport (a friendly, trusting relationship) can take days or weeks, and patient actors plan for that.
      • 
            
    • Optional extras: leaked staff lists, stock photos, and virtual phone numbers.
      • 
          
    
          
    No malware and no servers are required. That low entry cost is why the technique never goes away.
    
        
    
      
    

      
    How it is done — the attacker's plan
    
      
      
        
    1. Pick and study targets — Build a list of employees whose roles suggest useful knowledge. Read their public profiles, posts, and conference talks.
      2. 
        
    2. Build the persona — Create accounts with a coherent identity: photo, employer, colleagues, and a small history of normal activity.
      3. 
        
    3. Make first contact — Send a connection request or a short, friendly message with a believable reason to talk.
      4. 
        
    4. Build rapport — Chat about careers, salary, and industry news over days or weeks. Match the target's language and working hours.
      5. 
        
    5. Ask for the information — Slip in questions about teams, tools, badge rules, projects, or schedules. Keep each ask small.
      6. 
        
    6. Move the channel — Offer to continue by personal email, phone, or an external form, away from platform moderation and away from your visibility.
      7. 
        
    7. Record and hand off — Save the answers in a target profile. Pass them to the intrusion or fraud team for the next stage.
      8. 
      
    

      
    Where it applies
    
      
    
        
    Social networks (LinkedIn, X)
    Recruiter personas approach staff with job offers. This is the classic channel for long-term elicitation from professionals. Public job boards and résumé sites also help attackers pick targets with the right skills.
    
        
    Chat and messaging apps (Telegram, WhatsApp, Discord)
    Fast, informal, and rarely logged by companies. Good for urgent-sounding support stories and quick credential asks.
    
        
    Personal email services (Gmail, Proton Mail)
    Usually the second stage. The attacker moves the talk here to leave both the platform and your email gateway (the security appliance that filters company mail) behind.
    
        
    Support and vendor channels
    In-app chat, community forums, and vendor portals let a fake "support agent" ask for account details with borrowed authority.
    
      
    

      
    
        Lab only
        
    You can study the mechanics safely in an isolated lab. Run a local mail sink (a service that only receives test mail) and send yourself a fake job-offer message, then read its headers. Use test domains and test mailboxes only. Never send anything to real people.
    
        
      
    

      
    Common variations
    
      
    
        
    The agency recruiter
    Claims to work for a staffing agency with a real-looking website and a company page. Offers a high salary and a fast process.
    
        
    The support agent
    Pretends to be internal IT or a SaaS vendor. Asks for confirmation of account details, one-time codes, or remote-access steps.
    
        
    The journalist or analyst
    Asks for expert comment about your industry, then requests "background" facts about teams and projects.
    
        
    The community contact
    Uses event chats and alumni groups to make contact, then asks about your employer in harmless-looking follow-up messages.
    
      
    

      
    
        Warning
        
    Mistakes attackers make that create noise for you to detect:
    
        
      
          
    • Rushing rapport and asking about internal systems in the first exchange.
      • 
          
    • Reusing the same photo, phone number, or profile text across many targets.
      • 
          
    • Sending connection requests to dozens of staff in one department within days.
      • 
          
    • Moving to personal email before any trust exists.
      • 
          
    • Keeping a persona active after being reported, so the same handle resurfaces.
      • 
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    4
    Blue Team Perspective
    
      
    This section shows what defenders can actually see when the conversation happens on someone else's platform. Expect thin logs. The plan is to stack weak signals: user reports, DLP alerts, mail and proxy records, and identity events.
    

      
    Where defenders can see it
    
      
    
        
    
          
    SignalWhere you see itWhy it helps
    
          
            
    User report of a suspicious contactReporting mailbox or the report button in your mail clientThe only direct view into the conversation. Fastest and richest signal for this technique.
    
            
    DLP alert on outbound dataDLP console; email and upload logsCatches documents and details leaving, even when the chat itself is invisible.
    
            
    New external job-offer mailEmail gateway or mail-flow logsMany campaigns echo the social approach with an email follow-up that your gateway can read.
    
            
    Traffic to messaging endpointsWeb proxy logs (a proxy is a server that handles all staff web traffic)Shows who is chatting on which platform, how much, and when.
    
            
    Risky sign-in after contactIdP or SSO logs such as Okta or Microsoft Entra IDTies collected credentials or session data to a real login attempt.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Artifacts this technique leaves behind
    
      
    
        
    The report ticket
    Holds the persona's handle, profile URL, screenshots, and the reporter's account. This is your primary evidence and the seed for every hunt that follows.
    
        
    Persona indicators
    Handles, profile URLs, phone numbers, and personal email addresses used by the fake identity. Share them with the platform and with peers.
    
        
    The questions asked
    What the persona wanted tells you what the attacker lacks. Log the topics: badge rules, org charts, VPN (virtual private network) details, schedules.
    
        
    The data that left
    File names, screenshots, and message excerpts showing what was shared. DLP and upload logs often hold these even when chat logs do not exist.
    
      
    

      
    
        Note
        
    How this fits the attack chain: information collected here feeds the next technique, not an alarm today. Expect tailored phishing (T1566), impersonation of staff or support (T1656), or a voice call built on the shared facts (T1598.004). Treat one report as a sample: assume the persona talked to others.
    
      
    

      
    First-hour triage flow
    
      
      
        
    1. Thank and interview the reporter — Ask for screenshots, the profile URL, and the full thread. Reassure them; reports must stay cheap to make.
      2. 
        
    2. Preserve the evidence — Export the thread per policy and store it in the case ticket before the account disappears or the platform removes it.
      3. 
        
    3. Measure the exposure — List exactly what was shared: names, tools, schedules, credentials? If credentials or one-time codes left the building, page the on-call responder (send them an urgent alert).
      4. 
        
    4. Sweep for other targets — Search reports, mail logs, and proxy logs for the same handle, email, or phone number across the whole company environment.
      5. 
        
    5. Report and block — Report the account to the platform, warn the affected team, and apply mail and DLP rules for the persona's addresses.
      6. 
        
    6. Record and improve — Add the persona to your intel notes, update training with the real example, and check whether any control should have fired earlier.
      7. 
      
    

      
    
        Quick win
        
    Deploy a one-click "report suspicious contact" button in your mail client and on your intranet, and route reports straight to the SOC. For this technique, reports are the sensor. The cheapest button you can build is also your best detection.

    Confidence levels
    
          
    High: several credible sources agree. Act on it — hunt and block.
    
          
    Medium: one solid source or partial detail. Watch and verify before you block anything shared.
    
          
    Low: a single report or an unclear link. Keep it in your notes, not in blocking rules.
    
          
    Always record why you set the level. Confidence drifts as new reports arrive, and auditors will ask for your reasoning.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    How to apply intel
    
          
    Turn personas into data: handles, profile URLs, email addresses, phone numbers, and lure stories. Search your proxy, mail, and report archives for each one.
    
          
    Feed confirmed hits to the rules in 5.5. Add new lure texts to training. Share useful findings back through your ISAC, so peers can watch for the same strangers.
    
        
    
      
    

      
    Intel cycle
    
      
      
        
    1. Collect — Pull persona reports, vendor posts, and feed entries into one place with a date and a source link.
      2. 
        
    2. Process — Clean and deduplicate: one row per handle, email, phone number, or domain, defanged with [.] so nothing is clickable.
      3. 
        
    3. Analyze — Score each item for relevance to your staff and assign High, Medium, or Low confidence with a written reason.
      4. 
        
    4. Disseminate — Send the short version to the SOC, the tuned version to your rules, and the plain-language version to awareness training.
      5. 
        
    5. Feedback — Track which intel items produced hits or blocks, and use that to pick better sources next quarter.

    5.4 Logs and Patterns
    
      
    Each block below matches one threat from 5.3. Hostile domains are defanged with [.] so nothing is clickable — remove the brackets before testing. Users, IPs, and domains are fictional; IPs use documentation ranges such as 203.0.113.0/24 and 198.51.100.0/24.
    

      
    Threat 1 — Recruiter impersonation on LinkedIn/X
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    User report tickets; web proxy logs; platform takedown confirmations; SaaS audit logs for shared files.
    
        
    Key fields
    user, url (messaging endpoints), action, bytes_out; report fields: persona handle, profile URL, screenshots.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Staff use messaging endpoints in steady patterns: recruiters message candidates, sales staff chat with clients, and contacts are known people.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A staff member exchanges many messages with a brand-new profile; the same persona handle appears for several staff; large screenshot or document uploads follow a chat.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Count messaging-endpoint activity per user per day and flag sudden spikes joined to new-contact profiles; anchor every hit to a user report. The report is the sensor; the count is only support.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 2 — Job offer moves to personal email or an external form
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Email gateway logs (Proofpoint or Mimecast class); mail-flow (message trace) logs; DLP alerts on outbound replies and uploads.
    
        
    Key fields
    sender, recipient, subject, verdict, attachments, links, direction; DLP: rule hit, file name, destination.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Real job offers come from known agency domains, match open job postings, and rarely arrive cold with urgent deadlines.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A clean, link-bearing offer from a first-time-seen sender domain; offers that ask the user to continue on personal email; work-mail replies that carry documents to consumer mailboxes.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert on clean inbound messages with job-offer wording from first-time-seen domains, and on outbound replies that carry documents from corporate mail to consumer providers.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 3 — Support impersonation DMs collecting credentials or session data
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    IdP and SSO logs (Okta, Microsoft Entra ID); MFA logs; DLP or clipboard tooling for credential-like strings; proxy logs for phishing pages.
    
        
    Key fields
    userPrincipalName, ipAddress, deviceDetail, authenticationRequirement, status.errorCode; Okta: eventType, client.device, outcome.result.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Most staff sign in from managed, registered devices, with MFA satisfied, inside familiar networks and working hours.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A successful sign-in from an unregistered device without MFA; an MFA prompt approved minutes after an unusual DM contact; several failed logins followed by one success from a new network.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert on successful interactive sign-ins that skip MFA from unregistered devices, and on MFA approvals that follow reported impersonation. These events are the reuse side of stolen credentials or session data.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 4 — OSINT plus DM probing for badge, systems, and schedule information
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Web proxy and DNS logs; DLP alerts; file-upload audit logs; badge and access-control reports where exportable; public exposure reviews.
    
        
    Key fields
    url, filename, bytes_out, urlcat; DNS: queried_domain; badge logs: badge ID, door, timestamp (if you can export them).
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Facilities and roster documents move inside sanctioned storage; staff almost never upload them to consumer file-sharing services.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    Rota, badge, or network documents uploaded to personal file-sharing or form services; DNS or proxy hits to paste sites right after a DM conversation.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Flag POST uploads to consumer file-sharing domains when the file name or the DLP rule suggests facilities, rota, or network data. This compensates for the probing chat that no platform log records.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 5 — Long-term persona cultivation building a trusted external contact
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Mail-flow and SaaS audit logs; Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace admin logs; DLP; your report archive.
    
        
    Key fields
    Operation (New-InboxRule), Parameters, UserId, IPAddress; Gmail: label actions; contact age and external domain.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Users create rules to file newsletters and real recruiters; long external threads usually belong to known vendors or candidates.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A rule that quietly moves or hides mail from a new external contact; a months-long external chat with rising internal detail; rules created outside working hours or from unknown IPs.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert on inbox rules that move, hide, or delete mail from external senders, and re-review long-lived external contacts whenever any related report appears.
    
      
    
      

      
    5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL
    
      
    Sigma is a YAML rule format that many SIEMs (security information and event management systems) can compile into their own query language. SPL is Splunk's Search Processing Language; each query below mirrors one Sigma rule. Hostile indicators stay defanged with [.] — remove the brackets when you test. Index and sourcetype names are assumptions; check the small table under each rule and adjust for your own stack.
    

      
    Rule 1 — Social messaging volume per user (Threat 1)
    
      
    Severity: low. Threat 1 ranks High, but this rule is intentionally lower: the proxy count is a weak compensating signal, because the content stays on the platform. Use it to enrich user reports, not to page anyone.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    Zscaler web logs land in index=proxy with sourcetype="zscalernss-web"; url holds the full request URL; user is the SSO-mapped identity. One event equals one HTTP request.Recruiters, sales staff, social media managers, and shared kiosks all chat heavily by design.Require at least one distinct new-contact thread per user: join the output with your report archive handles, and raise the threshold during hiring season.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 2 — Job-offer mail from a first-time-seen domain (Threat 2)
    
      
    Severity: medium. Threat 2 ranks High; this rule stays Medium because it only flags first contact for review. Clean verdicts are the point — the lure is the story, so keyword plus new-sender logic does the work.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    Proofpoint message logs land in index=email with sourcetype="proofpoint:pps:message"; direction, verdict, and sender_domain are extracted fields. The SPL mirrors the Sigma allowlist (not filter_known) with two example lab domains; replace them with your own approved agencies. For Mimecast, use index=email sourcetype="mimecast:log" and rename fields.Real recruiters from new agencies, university career offices, and conference follow-ups use the same words.Add a reviewed allowlist of agency domains with expiry dates, and add a first-time-seen check on sender_domain so known agencies stop alerting after their first message.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 3 — Sign-in without MFA from an unregistered device (Threat 3)
    
      
    Severity: high. A success here means harvested credentials or session data may already be in use, so treat it as a page.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    Entra ID sign-in logs land in index=aad with sourcetype="azure:aad:signin", keeping the raw JSON properties structure. deviceId: null means the field is absent or empty.Break-glass admin accounts, legacy kiosks, vendor lab devices, and brand-new laptops before enrollment.Exclude managed-but-unregistered device names by list, require the sign-in country to be outside your trusted list, and suppress known break-glass accounts by name with an expiry.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 4 — Internal files uploaded to consumer file-sharing (Threat 4)
    
      
    Severity: medium. This is the compensating control for probing chats that no platform log will ever show you.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    Zscaler web logs in index=proxy with sourcetype="zscalernss-web"; filename and bytes_out are extracted fields. The SPL anchors the file-type match at the end of the URL ($) to mirror the Sigma url|endswith values. If your proxy does not capture the file name, match on the URL path only.Approved marketing or event files shared with outside partners; job applicants sending their own documents.Allowlist your sanctioned enterprise tenants by domain, and restrict alerting to document types joined with DLP rule hits for facilities, rota, or network terms.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 5 — Inbox rule hides or moves mail (Threat 5)
    
      
    Severity: medium. Users file real recruiter mail too, so this rule asks for review rather than panic.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    Mapping and field assumptionsFalse positivesTuning
    
          
            
    Microsoft 365 Unified Audit Log events land in index=o365 with sourcetype="ms:o365:audit"; Parameters is indexed as a multivalue field by the Splunk Add-on for Microsoft Office 365.Users filing newsletters, real recruiter mail, and vendor updates into folders.Alert only when the rule references a sender domain that appears in your report archive, or when the rule was created outside working hours from an unknown IP address.
    
              
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    6
    Real-World Case Study
    
      
    This is an educational reconstruction of Operation Dream Job. It is built only from public vendor reporting, including Google Threat Analysis Group and Mandiant-class reports, published between 2020 and 2022. No victim individuals are named, and details are simplified for teaching.
    

      
    Case snapshot
    
      
    
        
    Victim sector
    Aerospace, defense, military, government, and media organizations across multiple countries
    
        
    Year
    2020-2022, with public reporting peaking in 2020-2021
    
        
    Threat actor
    Lazarus Group (DPRK-linked), tracked by some vendors under other names
    
        
    Technique used
    T1598.001 — Spearphishing Service: fake recruiter personas on LinkedIn, X, and other services
    
        
    Outcome
    Sensitive details elicited through conversation; some targets also received documents that led to intrusions
    
      
    

      
    Timeline
    
      
    
        
    
          
    WhenWhat happenedDefender view
    
          
            
    2019-2020Personas appear: polished recruiter profiles backed by fake agency websitesNothing in your logs. Persona creation is free and silent.
    
            
    2020-2021Targets receive connection requests and attractive job offersFirst weak signal: a new external contact with urgency and a high salary.
    
            
    Days to weeksFriendly chat builds rapport: salary, career goals, industry newsNo corporate telemetry on the platform. Only the user sees this stage.
    
            
    Mid-conversationQuestions turn to employer, team structure, tools, and schedulesThe elicitation step. A reported transcript is your best evidence.
    
            
    Offer stageAn "offer letter" or "salary details" file or link moves to personal email or external sitesGateway and proxy may see the link; DLP may see the upload.
    
            
    2020-2021Some targets open documents that run code; intrusions followEndpoint and identity logs light up here, far from the original chat.
    
            
    2020-2022Vendors publish campaign analyses; platforms remove persona accountsTakedowns confirm the patterns; the intel feeds your rules and training.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Attack chain
    A persona met the target on a professional network, built rapport, elicited employer details, then delivered a document disguised as an offer letter. Some documents carried code; others simply collected answers. The chain mixed reconnaissance and access attempts in one story.
    
        
    Detection point
    Most detections started with a user report or with the moment the offer file was opened — not with the chat itself. Vendor reporting later mapped the persona set, handing defenders ready-made indicators to hunt.
    
        
    Containment
    Responders blocked the persona accounts and lure domains, quarantined offer mail, and pulled delivered files. Where credentials or session data were involved, sessions were revoked and passwords reset.
    
        
    Eradication
    Teams hunted the delivered document family across endpoints, removed persistence, and closed the entry path. Platforms were asked to remove the persona accounts, and case numbers were tracked to confirm removal.
    
        
    Recovery
    Organizations rebuilt affected devices, re-issued credentials, and refreshed training using the real lure texts. Several shared sanitized details with their ISAC and industry peers.
    
        
    Impact and consequences
    The technique itself handed attackers staffing, tools, and project details; where documents ran code, full intrusions followed. Public reporting in 2020-2022 made fake-recruiter contact a permanent part of security awareness.
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Team response and communication
    
        
    Leadership was told what left the building: which details were shared, and with whom. The affected team heard it first, without blame, within the first day. Legal and communications joined before any external statement, because job offers touch employment topics. Finally, the SOC published a short internal advisory with a picture of the lure and a one-line reporting instruction.
    
      
    

      
    Lessons learned
    
      
      
        
    • Treat one report as a sample, and sweep the whole environment for the same persona.
      • 
        
    • The channel switch to personal email is the warning sign worth teaching.
      • 
        
    • Preserve the conversation content before takedowns erase it.
      • 
        
    • Pair recruiter-themed awareness with real, reported examples, not cartoons.
      • 
        
    • Watch outbound uploads, not only inbound mail.
      • 
        
    • Share persona indicators with peers and your ISAC so the next team sees them sooner.
      • 
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    7
    Mitigation and Defense
    
      
    You cannot log inside someone else's platform. The controls below reduce exposure, create early signals, and make reporting cheap. They are grouped by control area, then prioritized in a table at the end.
    

      
    
        
    Policy and people
    • Publish a clear rule: recruiters and support staff never need your internal secrets.
    • Set a data-sharing floor: no org charts, rotas, or tool names in any external chat.
    • Define what staff should report, and state that reporting is blame-free.
    
        
    Identity
    • Require MFA everywhere, and prefer phishing-resistant methods such as security keys.
    • Block interactive sign-ins without registered devices for normal users.
    • Review MFA prompts: an approval the user did not start is an incident.
    
        
    Endpoint
    • Limit screen-capture and clipboard tools to approved users.
    • Deploy DLP agents that watch uploads to consumer services.
    • Patch fast: elicitation often precedes malware delivery.
    
        
    Network
    • Log and inspect outbound traffic to messaging platforms and file-sharing services.
    • Block known persona infrastructure at DNS.
    • Route social platform traffic through an authenticated path you can log.
    
        
    Cloud and SaaS
    • Turn on audit logging for Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace mail and drives.
    • Use SaaS DLP to flag org charts and rotas leaving sanctioned storage.
    • Review third-party app grants that can read mail, and remove what you do not need.
    
        
    Logging
    • Ship mail-flow, proxy, DNS, IdP, and audit logs to your SIEM.
    • Keep at least 12 months searchable for slow persona campaigns.
    • Keep report tickets structured so handles and domains can be searched later.
    
        
    Monitoring
    • Run the Sigma rules in 5.5 and tune them monthly.
    • Alert on inbox rules, unregistered-device sign-ins, and document uploads.
    • Track personas in an intel register with confidence levels and takedown case numbers.
    
        
    Backup and response
    • Keep a tested playbook for reported personas: preserve, sweep, block, warn.
    • Practice the first-hour flow with a short desktop exercise twice a year.
    • Pre-write the platform takedown request so reporting takes minutes, not days.
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Home and small office
    
        
      
          
    • Check a stranger's profile before replying: photo age, number of connections, and a real company page.
      • 
          
    • Keep work and personal email separate, and keep work secrets off personal apps.
      • 
          
    • Never share codes, passwords, or remote-access steps in any chat.
      • 
          
    • Turn on MFA for your email, bank, and social accounts.
      • 
          
    • Report fake profiles to the platform; it protects the next person too.

    9
    Cheat Sheet and Quick Reference
    
      
    Everything important from this page in one place. Print it, pin it, and keep it next to your queue.
    

      
    
        
    
          
    Key facts
    
          
    
            
    ATT&CK ID
    T1598.001 (sub-technique of T1598)
    
            
    Tactic
    Reconnaissance (TA0043)
    
            
    Platforms
    PRE (pre-attack; third-party services such as social media and personal webmail)
    
            
    Data sources
    Application logs (SaaS/SSO), email logs, DLP alerts, user reports
    
            
    Core difficulty
    Platform logs are external, so user reporting is the main sensor
    
            
    Case study
    Operation Dream Job — Lazarus Group fake recruiters, 2020-2022 reporting
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Top detections
    
          
      
            
    1. Report of a fake recruiter or support persona — anchor every other signal to it.
      2. 
            
    2. Clean inbound "job offer" mail from a first-time-seen sender domain (Sigma rule 2).
      3. 
            
    3. Successful sign-in without MFA from an unregistered device (Sigma rule 3).
      4. 
            
    4. Internal-looking files uploaded to consumer file-sharing services (Sigma rule 4).
      5. 
            
    5. Inbox rules that move, hide, or delete mail — possible channel hiding (Sigma rule 5).
      6. 
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          
    Response checklist
    
          
      
            
    • Interview the reporter and collect screenshots, profile URLs, and the thread.
      • 
            
    • Preserve the evidence in the case ticket before any takedown.
      • 
            
    • List exactly what was shared; page on-call if credentials or codes left.
      • 
            
    • Sweep mail, proxy, and reports for the same handle, email, or phone.
      • 
            
    • Report the persona to the platform and keep the case number.
      • 
            
    • Block persona addresses and domains in mail and DNS.
      • 
            
    • Warn the affected team within one working day, without blame.
      • 
            
    • Add the persona to the intel register and update training with the real example.
