Overview

This page explains MITRE ATT&CK sub-technique T1591.004, Identify Roles. It is written for defenders, SOC analysts, and IT staff who are new to the topic. You will learn what attackers do, why it works, and which logs, rules, and habits reduce the risk.

What is T1591.004 in plain language?

T1591.004 is the part of an attack that happens before any system is touched. The attacker studies your organization to learn who does what. Per MITRE ATT&CK, the adversary gathers information about identities and roles within the victim organization: the key individuals, their roles, and their duties. Attackers collect it from professional networks, press releases, and similar public sources, and they use it during targeting. The sources are public and free. Common examples are professional networking sites, press releases, conference talks, and the hidden details inside published documents.

This work belongs to OSINT (open-source intelligence: facts anyone can collect from public sources). Nothing is hacked at this stage. No exploit runs. The attacker simply reads what your company has chosen to publish, and turns it into a target list of people.

Roles are valuable because a role tells an attacker three things at once:

  • Who to contact. A role names a real person with real authority, such as a CFO (Chief Financial Officer) who can approve payments.
  • How to sound. If you know someone handles purchase approvals, an invoice email sounds natural to them.
  • What they can give. A help-desk role can reset passwords. An admin role can grant access. A speaker role proves expertise and gives personal detail for a lure.

MITRE lists typical sources for this technique: professional networks, social media, press releases, conference proceedings and talks, internet searches, and document metadata (the hidden author and company fields inside files). The attacker is not looking for every employee. The attacker is looking for the few people whose jobs give the easiest way in.

Why it matters

Role knowledge is what makes social engineering feel personal. A generic spam email says "Dear user". A researched email says "Hi Maria, saw your talk on cloud cost control at the fintech summit; our hiring team wants to share a role". The second one is far harder to refuse, because it names a real job, a real event, and a real skill.

This research powers several high-damage attacks:

  • BEC (business email compromise): attackers learn who approves payments and who reports to whom. Then they send payment requests that fit the real approval chain.
  • Vishing (voice phishing): attackers call the help desk or an employee. They sound credible because they know names, titles, and projects. Then they ask for a password reset or an MFA (multi-factor authentication) approval.
  • Targeted phishing: the lure is built around the person's real duties, so the message and attachment match what they expect to receive.

In plain terms: T1591.004 is the step that turns public facts into a target list of people. Every later step is sharper because of it.

Where attackers find roles: typical public sources

Each source below is free, legal to read, and already used by recruiters and sales teams. That is exactly what makes the technique hard to stop: the traffic looks ordinary.

Leadership pages

"Our team" and "About us" pages list names, titles, photos, and reporting lines in one tidy place.

Professional network profiles

Profiles show current job, history, skills, and connections. Search filters make it easy to list everyone with a given title.

Conference speaker lists

Agendas and speaker bios give full names, roles, employers, and often direct quotes about internal projects.

Whitepapers and papers

Published research names authors and their specialties, and often thanks internal teams by name.

Document metadata

The author, company, and sometimes internal file paths stored inside published Office and PDF files.

Job ads

A posting reveals the team, the tool stack, and sometimes the manager the new hire will report to.

Press quotes

Interviews and news items name spokespeople and link them to projects, budgets, and upcoming changes.

Webinars and podcasts

Guest intros often spell out names, titles, and current initiatives in a natural, quotable way.

Note

This page is defensive. It describes these sources so you can review and reduce your own exposure. Section 3 includes lab-only exercises that stay inside your own organization.

Three misconceptions worth clearing up

"It is just reading, so it is harmless"

Reading is harmless; arming is not. The same reading, organized into a target list with authority levels and reset duties, is the setup for fraud and takeover. The output, not the act, is the risk.

"Only big companies are researched"

Role research scales down perfectly well. A ten-person firm still has one person who approves payments and one person who runs IT. Small targets simply get shorter, faster versions of the same work.

"A tool will block this"

No tool sees a profile view or a phone call. Products reduce exposure and catch echoes, but the two controls that actually break this technique are human: fewer public details, and staff who report contact.

The same story, written out step by step

  1. Step 1 - Scrape the leadership page

    The attacker loads your public "Team" and "About" pages, again and again, and saves every name, title, and reporting line. This is fast, free, and needs no login. In minutes there is a first draft of the target list.

    Defender view: fast repeated reads of people pages show up in CDN (content delivery network) logs as one address pulling many similar URLs in a short window.

  2. Step 2 - Mine professional profiles

    Public profiles add depth that websites miss: exact job titles, tenure, past employers, skills, and who connects to whom. The attacker filters by title and builds a shortlist of high-value roles.

    Defender view: profile views never reach your logs. Your lever here is an exposure review of what your staff profiles publish.

  3. Step 3 - Enumerate speakers and authors

    Conference agendas, speaker bios, and published papers give rich personal detail: what the person works on, what they are proud of, and what tools they use. This is perfect material for a believable pretext.

    Defender view: bulk downloads of papers and bios appear in web logs as one source fetching many PDFs in a burst.

  4. Step 4 - Read document metadata

    Published files quietly carry hidden fields: author name, company, sometimes internal paths and usernames. The attacker harvests them to confirm real identities behind the public titles.

    Defender view: scrub metadata before publishing; the bulk PDF pulls that lead to it are visible in web logs.

  5. Step 5 - Build the target shortlist

    The attacker ranks the collected people by authority, access, and how much pressure they work under. Who approves payments? Who resets accounts? Who is too busy to check carefully? A few names land at the top of the list.

    Defender view: nothing reaches you at this step. The list now exists entirely outside your network.

  6. Step 6 - Stage recruiter personas

    Fake recruiter profiles and fresh sender domains are prepared. Photos, job posts, and a small website make the persona look real. The domain is registered shortly before the campaign starts.

    Defender view: first-seen recruiter-like domains can surface in mail gateway data before the first message lands.

  7. Step 7 - Contact the chosen roles

    Personal, polite messages reach the selected employees. Each message names a real role, a real skill, or a real event, which is why so many look harmless at first glance.

    Defender view: your best sensor is people. Make reporting one click and reward it, so the first contact becomes your alert.

  8. Step 8 - Hand-off to the lure phase

    Role research is complete. The attacker now moves to phishing and vishing phases (T1566 and T1598), with a document, a link, or a phone call aimed at each chosen person.

    Defender view: from here, the later-stage detections take over. Correlate reports, mail gateway hits, and endpoint alerts in the SOC investigation.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains how attackers think when they identify roles, and why the method works so well. The goal is defensive: if you understand which people and which details attract attackers, you can protect exactly those. All exercises here are lab-only and limited to your own organization.

Why attackers identify roles at all

Breaking in with pure technical attacks is expensive. Asking people for access is cheap. A targeted approach has a much higher success rate than mass spam, so serious attackers invest time in choosing the right person first. The choice follows two simple rules:

Choose a person with authority

A CFO (Chief Financial Officer) can approve a payment. A system admin can grant access to almost anything. A vice president's name in an email makes staff act fast. Authority turns one message into a big result.

Choose a person under pressure

Help-desk staff are trained to solve problems quickly, and a busy receptionist or a new employee has not learned to distrust strangers. Speed and politeness can defeat caution.

Three roles appear again and again in real incidents:

  • The CFO and finance team - targets for payment fraud and BEC (business email compromise) schemes that fit real approval chains.
  • The help desk - the people who can reset passwords or approve an MFA (multi-factor authentication) push. One convincing call can hand over an account.
  • System and cloud admins - one admin credential is worth more than fifty ordinary ones, so attackers study exactly which staff hold admin rights.

How the research is done, conceptually

The workflow is slow and simple on purpose. It is mostly reading, searching, and taking notes. A typical pass looks like this:

1

Collect the org surface. Copy names and titles from the leadership page, the press section, and job ads. Job ads often name the manager a new hire will report to, which quietly confirms reporting lines.

2

Enrich from professional networks. Search the network by title and company. Save skills, tenure, connections, and activity. Activity posts reveal current projects and tools.

3

Harvest talks, papers, and metadata. Speaker bios and published papers add personal detail. Document metadata (hidden author and company fields inside files) can confirm usernames and internal paths.

4

Map authority and access. Turn the notes into a rough org chart: who approves payments, who runs IT, who holds admin rights, who answers the help-desk phone.

5

Rank and pick. Score each person by authority, access, and how much pressure they are under. The top names become the targets for the next phase, and each one gets a custom lure plan.

Prerequisites

The technique has almost no technical bar, which is why it is so common:

  • Nothing but internet access. Every core source is public and free.
  • Patience and note-taking discipline. The work is collection and ranking, not coding.
  • A persona (a fake identity) prepared before contact, usually a professional-network profile with photo and history.
  • Optional tools: site-copiers for the public pages, metadata readers for published files, and a domain registered to look like a hiring or partner company.

Lab-only exercises for your own team

These three exercises are safe, legal, and useful. They stay inside your own organization and use only what your company has published about itself. Run them as a short workshop with HR, IT, and communications in the room.

Exercise 1: The leadership page review

Open your own "Team", "About", and "Contact" pages. Write down what they reveal: reporting lines, direct phone numbers, who signs contracts, who runs security.

Then ask: could a stranger use this page to pick a person and sound credible to them? List the three riskiest details and decide if each one needs to stay public.

Exercise 2: The PDF metadata check

Download two or three PDFs or Office files from your own public website or press page. Open the document properties and look at author, company, and any path or template fields.

If an author field shows a full name and email, or a path shows an internal server name, you have found exactly what attackers harvest. Fix it by scrubbing before publication.

Exercise 3: The friendly recruiter role-play

One person plays a fake recruiter contacting a volunteer employee over chat or email, using only public information. The volunteer answers honestly about what would convince them.

Discuss afterwards: which details made it believable, when did doubt appear, and how would the employee report it? This produces better awareness content than any generic slide deck.

Warning

Keep these exercises inside your organization. Never use real employee data from other companies, and never contact outside people. For anything broader, work with your legal and security teams and use an authorized engagement.

Common attacker mistakes

Attackers rush too. The same mistakes keep appearing, and each one is a defender opportunity:

  • Contacting too many employees at once. High volume breaks the disguise. Several staff compare notes, and several reports about the same story arrive within days.
  • Using stale org charts. Data older than a year often names people who left. A message aimed at a former employee is a free, high-quality signal for you.
  • Recycled lure domains. Domains used in earlier campaigns are already on blocklists, so the first mail gateway lookup can fail before any employee sees the message.
  • Metadata that contradicts the story. A document whose author field shows a different company name than the persona claims is easy to catch once staff know to look.

Danger

Using these methods against real people, or against systems and organizations you do not own, is illegal in most countries. Laws against fraud, unauthorized access, data protection violations, and impersonation apply even when nothing is "hacked" and even when the information is public. Collect and test only on your own organization, with permission, as described in the lab exercises above.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

From the SOC (Security Operations Center: the team that watches alerts) this sub-technique is one of the hardest to see. Almost all of it happens on other people's websites. This section shows why visibility is so poor, which faint artifacts remain, and how to run a fast triage when one appears.

Why detection is hard

Profile views are invisible to you. When someone reads a staff profile on a professional network, opens a press quote, or saves a conference agenda, no event is created in any system you own. There is no log line, no alert, and no dashboard entry. The research phase can take days or weeks and still produce zero events on your side.

The first victim-side echo is often a user report. An employee receives a friendly recruiter message, finds it odd, and mentions it. If reporting is easy and rewarded, that report is your earliest reliable signal. If reporting is hard, the echo may only appear later, as a phishing alert or an account-takeover investigation, when the attack has already moved past the reconnaissance phase.

The faint artifacts that do exist

Four weak signals are worth wiring up. None is perfect on its own, but together they raise the odds of seeing the harvest phase:

People-page scraping in web logs

Your CDN or WAF (web application firewall) sees every request to your public site. One address pulling /leadership, /people, and /team URLs in a burst shows a harvest pattern, not the behavior of a normal visitor.

Bulk CV and PDF downloads

Published CVs, papers, and whitepapers can be pulled in bulk. One source fetching thirty PDFs in an hour is a researcher at best and an attacker at worst.

First-seen recruiter-like sender domains

Recruiter pretexts need fresh domains. Your mail gateway knows which sender domains appear for the first time, and which of them address several staff with hiring-style subjects.

Employee reports

The report mailbox or ticket system captures staff who received odd recruiter or IT-support contact. Multiple reports naming the same domain form a strong, high-confidence signal.

How this fits the attack chain

T1591.004 sits at the very start of the chain, and its output is the input for everything that follows:

  • Feeds T1589 (Gather Victim Identity Information): the role shortlist decides whose personal details to collect next.
  • Feeds T1598 (Phishing for Information): messages that ask questions or pull files go to the exact people the roles revealed.
  • Feeds T1566 (Phishing): the lure itself, with a document or link shaped by the person's job.
  • Ends in T1078 (Valid Accounts): when a reset or a credential works, the attacker logs in with a real account, and account takeover begins. Most role-research incidents are detected only here, which is exactly what early reports and gateway alerts are meant to prevent.

Six-step triage flow when a signal appears

Use this flow when an employee reports recruiter contact, or when a web or mail rule fires. It is written so a junior analyst can follow it without help.

1

Employee reports recruiter contact. The ticket arrives from the report button or mailbox. Treat it as a possible recon signal, not as noise, even if the message looked harmless.

2

Thank the reporter and collect details. Ask for the sender address or profile link, the channel, the time, and any attachment. Praise the report; a thanked reporter reports again next time.

3

Check the mail gateway for the same domain. Search the last 14 days for the sender domain across all recipients. Count how many staff were contacted and whether anything was delivered or opened.

4

Block and report the domain. Add the domain and any sender addresses to the mail gateway blocklist, submit the indicators to your threat platform, and purge delivered copies where your tools allow.

5

Brief the targeted people. Send the contacted employees a short note: what the pretext looked like, that no action is needed, and how to report a repeat. Ask help desk to raise verification for any caller citing the same story.

6

Update awareness content. Add the real subject line and story to the next awareness note. A real example from your own mail flow always beats a generic sample.

Good practice

Track two numbers every month: how many reports arrived, and how fast the first report led to a block. Rising reports with fast blocks mean the human sensor is working.

Detection readiness checklist for this technique

Before you claim coverage of T1591.004, walk this list. Each item is binary: done or not done.

  • Web and CDN logs for the public site are in the SIEM, searchable, and normalized to one schema.
  • The people-page paths (/leadership, /people, /team, /management) are listed in one place so rule R1 stays correct after a site redesign.
  • Mail gateway logs include sender domain, subject, recipient, disposition, and first-seen domain stamps.
  • The report mailbox and ITSM tickets flow into the SIEM with a stable report_type field.
  • An HR and talent-acquisition allow-list exists, is machine-readable, and is refreshed quarterly.
  • Rules R1 to R7 are deployed, tested with controlled events, and have named owners.
  • The six-step triage flow is written into the on-call runbook, not just into this guide.
  • Awareness content includes at least one real recruiter pretext example from your own environment.

Note

If three or more boxes are unchecked, your practical coverage of this technique is close to zero, even if your tools list says otherwise. Coverage here is built from humble sources: access logs, mail headers, and the willingness of people to report.

How to read confidence badges

High means the claim is supported by multiple independent reports or primary evidence, such as captured infrastructure or incident response findings. Moderate to high means a strong single-source report, possibly with vendor marketing interests or partial attribution. Moderate means one source with indirect evidence; treat details as indicative, not proven. Never treat a single blog post as a fact base for blocking decisions; look for a second source before you act.

From vendor report to working detection

A campaign report is only useful if something in your stack changes the same week. Use this short pipeline for every relevant report:

1

Extract the role-research facts. Note which roles were approached, which channels were used, and which subject lines appeared. Ignore file hashes for now; this technique leaves behavior, not binaries.

2

5.4Logs and Patterns

Seven log sources cover this technique. For each one you get the fields to care about, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, the detection logic in one sentence, and a realistic sample event. Three honest notes up front. Volumes here are small compared to phishing. Thresholds need tuning to your site. Profile views on professional networks never produce any of these events.

a) CDN and WAF web logs: people-page harvesting

Fields that matter: client IP (c-ip), requested path (cs-uri-stem), HTTP method (cs-method), user agent (cs(User-Agent)), timestamp, status code.

Normal: a handful of humans viewing a few profile pages each; search-engine crawlers with known ranges and stable agents; job candidates hitting one or two pages.

Suspicious: one address requesting many different people-page URLs in minutes, especially with a generic agent, no referrer, and near-equal status codes. Sequencing matters too: harvesters walk lists of paths, humans click links.

Detection logic: count GET requests to people-page prefixes per client IP over ten minutes and alert above your tuned threshold.

In the sample, the first address walks six people pages in ten seconds with a script user agent and no referrer. The last line is what an ordinary visitor looks like, for contrast.

b) Web logs: bulk document downloads

Fields that matter: client IP, requested file path (cs-uri-stem), byte count (sc-bytes), user agent, timestamp.

Normal: readers arrive from search or social media and take one or two files; bots from search engines fetch a spread of pages over hours.

Suspicious: one address pulling many PDFs from a papers, press, or CV directory in under an hour, often alphabetical, often with a headless or script user agent.

Detection logic: count GET requests ending in .pdf (or your document extensions) per client IP over an hour and alert above your tuned threshold.

The pattern is alphabetical, quick, and machine-like. A human reader rarely downloads five PDFs in fourteen seconds from the same directory.

c) Mail gateway logs: first-seen recruiter-like sender domains

Fields that matter: event time (ts), sender domain (sender_domain), number of internal recipients (recipients_count), subject pattern (subject_pattern), gateway decision (disposition).

Normal: known applicant-tracking and HR domains recur for months; single-recipient messages with varied subjects.

Suspicious: a brand-new domain whose first activity is three or more staff receiving hiring-themed subjects on the same day.

Detection logic: flag sender domains seen for the first time whose subjects match hiring keywords and whose recipient count is three or more.

The first two events are the recruiter wave that Sigma rule R2 in section 5.5 is built to catch: same new domain, growing recipient list, hiring subjects, and deliveries still going out outside office hours.

Fields that matter: scanned file (file), author field (author), company field (company_field), leaked internal path (path_leak).

Normal: scans of your public document library that return no personal author fields and no internal paths.

Suspicious: any published file whose properties still contain a real person's name, an internal hostname, or a build path.

Detection logic: run a scheduled scan over the public document directory and alert on every file with a non-empty author or a path-shaped string.

The first two events hand an attacker a confirmed username, an email pattern, and two internal hostnames. The third event is the goal: a generic author field with nothing else.

f) Help-desk identity requests: MFA and password resets

Fields that matter: event time (ts), requester (requester), target account (target_account), request type (request_type), risk flags (risk_flags), callback check (verified_by_callback), agent decision (agent_action).

Normal: resets where the verification script ran in full: callback done, ticket linked to the employee, flags empty.

Suspicious: any reset request with risk flags such as an unverified caller, a skipped callback, or artificial urgency, especially for admin or executive accounts.

Detection logic: alert on every MFA or password reset request whose risk flags show weak or missing verification.

Event one and event three are the echoes this page warns about: role knowledge turned into reset attempts. Event three is worse, because the target is a service account with admin rights. Event two is the same request done properly, for contrast.

g) Identity and HR audit logs: account lifecycle gaps

Fields that matter: event time (ts), account (account), event type (event_type), account status (account_status), HR record check (hr_record_present), last logon (last_logon).

Normal: every active account has a matching HR record, and disabled accounts never log on.

Suspicious: an active account with no HR record, or any logon by an account marked disabled. Public joiner and leaver announcements make these windows visible to attackers.

Detection logic: run a nightly audit that joins active accounts against HR records and flags logons from disabled accounts.

Event one is a likely missed leaver. Event two may be a service account that was never registered, which the allow-list in rule R7 should cover. Event three is the serious one: a disabled account tried to log on, which often means the credential is in the wrong hands.

Note

An honest limitation: none of these logs show the research itself. Profile views, site-clipboard copies, and notes on a professional network leave no trace on your side. The detections above catch the mechanical harvest and the outreach echoes only. That is still valuable: an attacker who skips the harvest and goes straight to outreach triggers the mail rule, and one who harvests carelessly triggers the web rule.

Reading the alerts: what a true positive looks like

Rules alone do not make a case. These short profiles show how each rule's alert should read before you escalate it.

  • R1 true positive: one external address, forty-plus GET requests across three or more people-page prefixes in ten minutes, script-like user agent, no referrer, all status 200. The same address did nothing else on your site.
  • R2 true positive: a sender domain first seen that morning, three or more distinct staff recipients, hiring-themed subjects, disposition delivered, and no prior history anywhere in your mail index.
  • R3 true positive: three or more tickets within seven days naming one domain. Channels are mixed (mail, phone, chat), and reporters hold the roles that section 5.3 calls high-value.
  • R4 true positive: one address, thirty-plus PDF GETs inside an hour, alphabetical or directory-walk order, byte counts in the megabytes, and an agent that is not a browser.
  • R5 true positive: a reset request for a named account, risk flags set, callback not done, and the caller number unknown. If the target account is an admin or an executive, escalate at once.
  • R6 true positive: a published file with a personal author or an internal path, found by the scheduled scan, and no allow-list entry covering it.
  • R7 true positive: an active account with no HR record beyond its first week, or any logon attempt by an account marked disabled.

Everything else is tuning work. An alert that fails two or three of these profile points is usually a false positive, and the tuning rows above tell you which setting to change first.

Good practice

Test the seven rules before you rely on them. Run a controlled scan of your own site from a lab address. Send test mail from a lab domain. File test tickets, and run the metadata and lifecycle scans once against a copy of real data. Confirm each rule fires, then record the test in your detection register. Deployment notes

Small practical points that save hours when you ship these rules:

  • Convert, do not rewrite. Feed the Sigma YAML to a converter (sigma-cli with pySigma pipelines) and let it generate your backend queries. Hand-written duplicates drift apart.
  • Map fields once. Build a pipeline that maps cs-uri-stem to your web index field and sender_domain to your mail index field. The same pipeline then serves every future web or mail rule.
  • Give each rule an owner and a runbook link. An alert without a runbook trains analysts to ignore it.
  • Watch costs. R1 and R4 run over high-volume web data; schedule them as saved searches rather than real-time alerts until you know their hit rate.
  • Schedule the audits. R6 and R7 are nightly audit jobs, not real-time alerts. Run them after the HR export and the publishing window close, so both joins are current.
  • Version the allow-list. The HR and TA domain list belongs in a lookup file with change history, not in the rule text.

Real-World Case Study

Operation Dream Job is the clearest public example of T1591.004 in action. Fake recruiters approached real experts whose jobs were public knowledge, and the offer was convincing precisely because the research was good. This section retells the case from a defensive point of view. The facts come from public vendor reporting, mainly ClearSky's Operation Dream Job report of December 2020, later write-ups by other vendors, and the MITRE ATT&CK campaign page C0022.

Context: who ran it and what it was

The actor: Lazarus Group, activity linked to North Korea, tracked by governments and vendors for years. The group runs campaigns that mix espionage with direct financial gain.

The operation: Dream Job, known in MITRE ATT&CK as campaign C0022. First activity is documented from September 2019, and the main waves ran through August 2020. ClearSky Security gave the operation its name in a December 2020 report. Later researchers used Dream Job as an umbrella name for related job-lure waves, such as Operation Interception and Operation North Star.

The hook: the "job description" arrived as a document or an archive, often hosted on ordinary file-sharing services. Opening it began the compromise. The facts above come from vendor reporting and the MITRE ATT&CK campaign page C0022.

Map to T1591.004: first the roles, then the lure

The target list was not random. Each approached person matched an exact job function: experts in aerospace, defense, or government work whose expertise was visible in public bios, papers, and conference programs.

The recruiter pretext only worked because the role research was solid. A recruiter who knows your specialty, your employer, and your recent talk does not need luck to sound real. That is the entire lesson of this case for defenders: the strength of the lure was manufactured in the research phase, before a single malicious file was sent.

Campaign timeline

  1. September 2019

    First activity documented in ATT&CK: fake recruiter personas begin contact on professional networks. The personas carry photos, employment histories, and connection building, and look like ordinary talent-acquisition staff.

  2. 2019 to 2020

    Targets at defense, aerospace, government, and other organizations are approached with genuine-looking job offers. The approach messages name each target's real field and experience, which is the signature of prior role research.

  3. 2020

    Interested targets are asked to open documents or download a "job description" archive. The files arrive through the chat or links hosted on attacker-controlled infrastructure and ordinary file-sharing services.

  4. 2020

    The documents abuse remote templates and malicious macros (a macro is a small program inside office documents; a remote template lets the document pull code from outside). Victims who enable content run the code.

  5. 2020

    Cobalt Strike C2 (command and control: the attacker's channel to a compromised machine) beacons are installed on victim hosts, giving the operators a stable remote channel.

  6. 2020 to August 2020

    In several cases credentials and data are stolen. ATT&CK documents at least one case where the access was monetized as a business email compromise operation, and some reported cases add ransomware or destructive follow-up.

  7. December 2020

    ClearSky Security publishes its "Operation Dream Job" report and names the pattern. Researchers also note overlapping methods with Operation North Star and Operation Interception.

  8. Through 2021 and beyond

    Campaigns continue in waves with new personas and fresh domains; by 2022 researchers describe Dream Job as an umbrella term for the job-lure family. Each wave repeats the same research-then-lure rhythm, which is why the pattern matters more than any single indicator.

The attack chain, step by step

1

Role recon (T1591.004). The operators list experts whose jobs are public: defense and aerospace engineers, government advisers, and other named specialists. Names, employers, and specialties are confirmed from bios, papers, and conference programs.

2

Persona build. Each recruiter identity gets a photo, a career history, and weeks of normal-looking networking. The persona is ready before any target is contacted.

3

Contact. The recruiter approaches the chosen expert with an offer that matches the person's field. Rapport is built politely over days or weeks. No pressure and no urgency; patience is the method.

4

Deliver. The "job description" arrives as a document or an archive link. The file is expected, which is the whole point of the research: nobody questions a document they were promised.

5

Exploit. Opening the document triggers the remote template or macro path. The victim's consent to "enable content" was the only protection the attacker needed to get past.

6

C2 and follow-through. A beacon gives the operators a channel. Credentials and data are collected. In at least one documented case the access was monetized as business email compromise, and some reported cases ended in ransomware or destructive attacks.

Where detection actually happened

In reported cases, the first reliable signals were user reports ("a recruiter I never applied to sent me this file") and blocked macro executions on endpoint controls. Beaconing was flagged later, once command-and-control traffic had already flowed. The practical lesson: your mail, reporting, and macro controls fire before your network tools do. Give them budget first, and treat every recruiter report as the first sign of a campaign.

Response, in three phases

Response runs in three phases, and each has a plain meaning. Containment means stopping the attack from spreading right now. Eradication means removing everything the attacker left behind. Recovery means getting back to normal, safely.

Containment

Isolate hosts that opened the documents. Disable the affected accounts and revoke active sessions. Block the sender domains, the archive host, and the beacon addresses at mail, proxy, and firewall. Preserve the lure files for analysis.

Eradication

Rebuild or clean the compromised hosts. Reset every credential the accounts could touch, with priority on privileged ones. Search the estate for the beacon indicators and for other recipients of the same personas. Close the macro path that worked.

Recovery

Restore systems from verified clean sources. Re-enable accounts with new credentials and MFA re-enrollment. Monitor the returning users closely for two weeks. Report the indicators to your sector community so others can block them early.

Impact

Multiple organizations across defense, aerospace, government, and other sectors were affected, in several countries. Reported outcomes include stolen credentials and data, one documented business email compromise operation, and ransomware or destructive follow-up in some reported cases. For a research-driven campaign, the cost was not one exploit; it was months of patient persona work turned into long-lasting access.

Team response and communication

Organizations that handled the case well did three things. They told contacted staff quickly what had happened and what to watch for, which produced more reports instead of fewer. They coordinated statements with legal and communications before the story spread externally. They shared sanitized indicators with vendors and sector partners, which raised everyone's detection quality.

What public reporting can and cannot tell you

Be precise about the evidence. Vendor reports and government campaign records document the personas, the targeting pattern, the lure files, and the outcomes. They do not document how long the role research took, which exact public pages were used, or every organization approached. Treat this case, like all intelligence, as a proven pattern rather than a complete record. For your own planning, the proven pattern is enough: role research, then persona, then contact, then document, then C2. Each stage has a control and a detection on this page.

Lessons for defenders

  • Run role-exposure reviews: know what your public pages, bios, and documents reveal about who does what.
  • Teach staff to verify recruiters through official channels, and to treat unexpected job documents as suspicious by default.
  • Tighten macro settings: block macros from the internet with policy, so the enable-content step fails even for willing users.
  • Make reporting one click, thank every reporter, and feed their reports into mail blocks and awareness content.
  • Protect executive and expert identities with aliases or shared contact points where the role allows it, so a fake persona has fewer accounts to take over.

Mitigation and Defense

You cannot stop someone from reading public pages, but you can make the research less useful and the outreach less effective. This section ranks the controls by effort and payoff, lists the moves to make first, and adds a shorter list for home offices and small teams.

Do this first

If you can only do six things this quarter, do these:

  • Turn on the one-click report button and measure reports per week. The human sensor is free and gets better with use.
  • Deploy phishing-resistant MFA for finance, help desk, and admins first, then everyone.
  • Write and rehearse the help-desk verification script, including the no-callback-no-reset rule for MFA changes.
  • Run the document metadata scan from section 5.4e and scrub what it finds.
  • Build the R1 to R7 Sigma rules against your logs and test them with a controlled scan and test tickets.
  • Review your leadership page and team pages with HR and communications; remove what a stranger does not need.

A 30-60-90 day rollout

Turn the table above into a plan you can start on Monday:

1

Days 1 to 30: turn on the report button and the same-day queue. Deploy the external-sender banner. Run the first metadata scan and scrub the results. Book the exposure review with HR and communications.

2

Days 31 to 60: onboard web, mail, ITSM, and identity logs if missing. Deploy R2, R3, and R5 first, because they catch the outreach and reset half. Write the help-desk verification script and drill it twice.

3

Days 61 to 90: deploy R1, R4, R6, and R7 with tuned thresholds. Run the first controlled detection test. Publish the first awareness note built from a real report. Set the monthly metrics baseline.

Home office and small organizations

Small teams are targeted with the same method but have fewer tools. Three habits cover most of the risk. First, trim personal titles from public profiles and company pages; "Accounting" can be published, "Approves all payments" does not need to be. Second, verify every recruiter by phone using the official number of the claimed company, never the number in the message. Third, never open job documents you did not expect; if the offer is real, the sender can resend after you verify them. If you run a small business, ask your IT provider whether mail from brand-new sender domains can be flagged, and whether your website logs are kept anywhere at all.

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

These mistakes come up again and again in assessments and incidents. Each card gives the symptom you will notice, the root cause, the fix, and the prevention habit that keeps it fixed. Skim the symptoms first; if one sounds familiar, your organization probably owns that mistake today.

1. The full org chart is public

Symptom
A stranger can reconstruct reporting lines from one website visit.
Root cause
The "About us" page grew over years and nobody reviewed it as an attack surface.
Fix
Publish functions and a generic contact route instead of complete reporting chains.
Prevention
Quarterly exposure review of people pages, jointly owned by communications and security.

2. Published PDFs keep author metadata

Symptom
Document properties show real author names, emails, or internal paths.
Root cause
The publication workflow has no scrubbing step; files go from laptop to web server as-is.
Fix
Scrub the current library and add an automated metadata-clean step to publishing.
Prevention
Scheduled scan from section 5.4e with an alert on every non-clean file.

3. Recruiter reports are ignored or delayed

Symptom
Tickets sit for days; staff stop reporting because "nothing happens anyway".
Root cause
Suspicious-contact reports have no priority class and no named owner.
Fix
Give the report category its own queue and a same-day triage promise.
Prevention
Track report-to-block time as a monthly metric with a target under 24 hours.

4. There is no one-click report button

Symptom
Staff forward suspicious mail in random ways, or tell a colleague and never security.
Root cause
Reporting was never built as a proper service; it relies on remembering an address.
Fix
Deploy the report add-in for mail and add a report link in chat tools.
Prevention
Praise and publish report outcomes so the habit spreads; measure volume.

5. First-seen sender domains are never surfaced

Symptom
Nobody can answer "is this sender domain new to us?" without slow manual searching.
Root cause
The mail gateway tracks the data but nothing ships it to the SIEM.
Fix
Enable first-seen stamping and route it into the mail index (rule R2).
Prevention
Include first-seen fields in the log-source checklist for every new mail tool.

6. Real hiring noise trains analysts to ignore the rules

Symptom
R2 fires daily during a hiring drive, and analysts lower the thresholds until the rule never fires.
Root cause
No allow-list of approved HR and TA domains exists.
Fix
Build the allow-list with HR, apply it to R2 and R3, and review it quarterly.
Prevention
Make "new recruiting agency" a workflow item that updates the list automatically.

7. CDN logs are missing or unnormalized

Symptom
Rule R1 has no data, or every analyst writes a different query for the same field.
Root cause
CDN logs were never onboarded, or each site ships a different format.
Fix
Onboard all sites to one normalized web schema with client IP, path, agent, and status.
Prevention
Add web logs to the standing log-source inventory with a quarterly health check.

8. Executives have no alias or protection basics

Symptom
The CFO's direct line, email pattern, and travel plans are all one search away.
Root cause
Executive visibility was treated only as a marketing asset, never as a risk.
Fix
Introduce shared or alias contact points; register lookalike domains; brief the executives' assistants.
Prevention
Executive protection review at every role change, owned jointly by security and communications.

9. Awareness training ignores role-specific pretexts

Symptom
Staff can spot an obvious scam message but fall for a recruiter who names their latest talk.
Root cause
Training uses generic examples and never shows the real pretexts for each function.
Fix
Add recruiter, invoice, and IT-support scenarios, using real sanitized examples from your own reports.
Prevention
Refresh scenario content every quarter from the ticket data and vendor reporting.

10. Help-desk verification is too weak

Symptom
A caller with a name and a confident tone gets an MFA reset.
Root cause
Verification is informal; politeness and queue pressure defeat the checks.
Fix
Written verification script, mandatory callback to the number on file, no exceptions for "the CEO".
Prevention
Monthly speed-interview drill where security plays the caller and scores the desk.

11. Nobody validates that the detections actually fire

Symptom
Rules exist on paper; nobody knows they break after the next log-source change.
Root cause
No purple-team test (a controlled exercise where the defense validates its own detections) is scheduled.
Fix
Run the controlled scan, test mail, and test tickets from section 5.5, and record results.
Prevention
Quarterly detection-validation calendar with named owners per rule.

12. Legacy documents stay published forever

Symptom
A 2019 whitepaper still lists a former employee's name and internal project paths.
Root cause
Publication is treated as permanent; nothing ever retires old files.
Fix
Archive or replace outdated papers; republish cleaned versions where content still matters.
Prevention
Annual review of the public document library with explicit keep, clean, or remove decisions.

13. HR records and accounts are never compared

Symptom
People keep working accounts months after they leave, and nobody notices until an audit.
Root cause
The HR export and the identity directory are never joined, so leavers stay active.
Fix
Run a monthly join between HR records and active accounts; disable or re-own what has no record.
Prevention
Rule R7 from section 5.5 plus a monthly owner sign-off on the gap list.

Make these habits

Daily

Triage every suspicious-contact report the day it arrives. Skim the mail gateway for new recruiter-like domains in the last 24 hours. It takes ten minutes and keeps the human sensor trusted.

Weekly

Review the R1 to R7 alerts together, and correlate domains across mail, reports, web logs, and identity data. Update the awareness channel with one real example from the week.

Monthly

Check report volume and report-to-block time. Re-run the metadata scan on newly published files. Drill the help-desk script once, and read one current vendor report on role-targeted campaigns.
