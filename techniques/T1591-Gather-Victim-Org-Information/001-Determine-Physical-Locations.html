Overview

This page explains sub-technique T1591.001 in plain language. It shows how attackers map the places where a company works, why that matters, and what defenders can see and do. Every technical term is explained the first time it appears, so you can read top to bottom without outside help.

What this technique is

T1591.001 – Determine Physical Locations is a sub-technique of T1591, Gather Victim Org Information. MITRE’s description, in plain words: before an attack, adversaries may gather the victim’s physical locations that can be used during targeting. The information includes where key resources and infrastructure are housed, and it can show which legal area the victim works in.

MITRE lists the ways this happens: direct asking, such as phishing for information, and reading online or other accessible data, such as victim-owned websites or social media. In practice that means public records, press releases, maps and imagery, and job advertisements. Much of the work is open-source intelligence (OSINT: facts anyone can collect from public sources). No hacking is needed. A browser, a map app, and patience are enough.

It helps to picture the output. The attacker ends with a list. It holds the headquarters with two entrances, the plant with a guarded gate, the depot with open docks in the evening, and the data center in a business park. Each line in that list was built from things the company itself published.

Why it matters to defenders

Location mapping looks harmless. It is only reading, after all. But the output powers the next phases of an attack in three serious ways.

Physical risk

Someone who knows your sites, doors, docks, and quiet hours can plan a break-in, place a device, or walk in behind a badged employee. The recon turns a building into a target.

Credible lures

A phishing mail or phone call that names your real plant, your real depot, and a real shift is far more convincing than a generic scam. Site knowledge is fuel for social engineering.

Single points of failure

Pipelines, ports, and single data centers can be the one thing everything depends on: a place where a small action has a big effect. Knowing that place is knowing where the business hurts.

Notice that none of these harms need a broken system. They only need published facts, which is why defense here is mostly about publishing less and noticing more.

Common misconceptions

“We have nothing worth mapping”

Every organization has a door, a delivery point, and a server room. Value is relative: your site only needs to be softer than the next one, and easier to reach than your data center.

“Our security tools would see it”

Your tools see your network and your endpoints. A browser on a map site triggers none of them. Only the echoes described in section 4 ever reach you.

“Recon means someone hacked us”

No. Reconnaissance happens before any compromise, by people who may never touch your systems. Treating it as an early-warning problem, not a breach, is the healthy stance.

Typical public sources attackers use

All of the sources below are legal to read. That is exactly why this technique is common: the collection step breaks no law by itself, and it looks like ordinary browsing.

Contact pages

Company websites list office addresses, phone numbers, and driving directions.

Press releases

Announcements name new plants, expansions, and depots, sometimes with full addresses.

Map imagery

Street-level imagery shows entrances, docks, fences, and the view a visitor sees.

Satellite views

Overhead photos reveal yard layout, fuel tanks, generator pads, and camera posts.

Job ads

Job ads with site addresses reveal new sites before the website mentions them.

Business registries

Company registers list registered offices and branch entities, with filings that name sites.

Delivery notices

Logistics notices and dock booking pages reveal where and when goods move.

Published photos

Press images may keep EXIF (Exchangeable Image File Format: hidden data inside photos, like GPS coordinates) from the camera.

Every node also shows a short description when you point at it. If you use a screen reader, the written story below carries the same content, and the figure itself is labeled as decorative.

The story in words

Each step below matches one step of the animation. It ends with a short defender view, so the whole list doubles as a written, screen-reader-friendly version of the simulation.

  1. Step 1 · Search public records

    The attacker starts with business registries, news archives, and map services. The goal is a short list of real sites: a headquarters, two plants, and a depot. Almost nothing in this step touches the victim’s own systems.

    Defender view: This step leaves almost no trace at the victim, so there is no alert to expect and nothing to triage yet.

  2. Step 2 · Study imagery of HQ and plants

    Public satellite and street imagery shows entrances, docks, fences, and parking. The attacker notes which gate looks weakest and where guards seem to sit.

    Defender view: You cannot see this activity. Physical awareness training and a check of what your imagery reveals are your counters.

  3. Step 3 · Collect site facts from press

    Press releases and job ads confirm addresses and expansion plans. A job ad that names a new automated warehouse hands the planner a free fact.

    Defender view: Fast repeated reads of news and location pages show up in CDN (content delivery network: the service that serves website copies worldwide and logs requests) logs.

  4. Step 4 · Harvest geotagged photos

    The attacker downloads press photos in bulk and reads their EXIF (Exchangeable Image File Format: hidden data inside photos, like GPS coordinates) metadata. One unscrubbed photo of a data center can reveal exact coordinates.

    Defender view: Bulk media downloads are visible in web logs. Scrubbing EXIF at publish time removes the risk at the source.

  5. Step 5 · Note doors, docks, and guards

    The attacker turns images into notes: door counts, camera gaps, quiet hours, and dock schedules. The site profile now exists entirely outside your network.

    Defender view: Nothing reaches you at this step. Staff reports and reception notes are the only sensors that can still catch the follow-up contact.

  6. Step 6 · Test site-name look-alike domains

    A look-alike domain that mixes your brand with a site word, such as the name of a depot, is registered for later lures. It may get a valid TLS certificate so it looks trusted in a browser.

    Defender view: Brand monitoring and CT logs (Certificate Transparency: public list of TLS certificates) can reveal the new domain early.

  7. Step 7 · Plan the approach

    The attacker links sites, timings, and pretexts into one plan: which site, which door, which story. A first mail or call may quietly test the story on staff.

    Defender view: Watch for site-themed lures in mail gateway logs, and for staff reports of odd calls about visits, tours, or badges.

  8. Step 8 · Hand-off to the operation

    The physical map is ready. Follow-on phases can be a physical intrusion attempt or targeted phishing that names real sites and shifts.

    Defender view: From here your network detections take over. Report what you have, correlate the artifacts, and brief facilities and the SOC (Security Operations Center: the team that watches alerts) together.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section looks over the attacker’s shoulder. The goal is not a how-to manual: every exercise here is marked lab-only and uses your own organization’s data. Knowing what a planner values tells you what to protect, what to publish less of, and what to watch.

Why attackers map physical locations

Choose the weakest site

Headquarters get guards, cameras, and laminated visitor rules. A small depot or a half-finished warehouse often does not. Recon lets the attacker compare several sites and pick the least protected one instead of the famous one.

Plan the physical approach

Entrances, fence lines, parking flows, loading docks, and camera blind spots all come from imagery. Quiet hours and shift patterns can be read from lighting, traffic, and job ads that name shifts.

Make lures local and believable

A call that says “I am the new courier for the Riverside depot, which gate do I use?” works only if the depot is real and the name is right. Site detail turns a scam into a story.

Understand single points of failure

For extortion and sabotage, knowing that one pipeline, one port, or one data center carries the whole business tells the attacker where pressure does the most damage.

How it is done, conceptually

The method is a funnel that moves from broad to narrow. Written as steps, it looks like this:

1

Scope the organization. Registries and news give the list of legal entities and places. The attacker learns site names like “Site B” or “North campus” before knowing what is inside them.

2

Confirm with official pages. Contact pages and press releases turn names into addresses. Job ads add sites that are too new for the website, and they often name the exact street.

3

Study imagery. Map and satellite views add layout: gates, docks, fuel tanks, generator pads, camera posts, and how traffic enters and leaves.

4

Enrich with people data. Photo EXIF (Exchangeable Image File Format: hidden data inside photos, like GPS coordinates), delivery notices, and staff posts add timings, doors, and visit-worthy rooms.

The output is a modest document. It has one row per site, with address, layout notes, guard patterns as far as they are visible, and a shortlist of entry stories to test later. Nothing here touches your firewall, which is the whole problem.

A worked walk-through, kept abstract

To make the funnel concrete without naming any real company, here is a fictional manufacturer with three sites. Notice how little effort each row takes:

SiteWhere the fact came fromWhat the planner wrote down

Three searches, one imagery session, and the planner already prefers a site. Every cell in the table was published by the company on purpose; that is the essence of this technique.

Prerequisites for this technique

  • Time and patience: reading is slow, and no attack tools are needed at this stage.
  • A simple note system to link sites, addresses, and observations into one picture.
  • Basic map and image reading: what a dock, a bollard, a guard post, or a generator yard looks like from above.
  • No budget and no victim contact: this phase uses free public services and leaves the target untouched.

That low bar is the reason the technique appears in nearly every serious physical intrusion or targeted lure operation.

Lab-only exercises

Do these against your own organization only, with written permission from your security lead. They teach the skill without harming anyone, and each one ends with an output you can actually use.

Inventory your public site data

Pretend to be a planner with one afternoon. Collect every address, map pin, and site name your organization publishes: website, press kit, job ads, filings. Score each item: would it help someone visit uninvited? Deliver the top five leaks to their owners.

Check EXIF in your own photos

Download press images from your own site. Read their metadata with a free tool. List which files carry GPS coordinates or device details, then fix the publishing pipeline, not just the files. Repeat monthly until the pipeline is trusted.

Review a job ad as a recon object

Take one live ad for a warehouse or data-center role. List everything it reveals: address, shift times, badge systems, vendors, equipment. Ask: could a caller use this to sound like an insider? Then rewrite the ad to keep the reach but drop the detail.

Common variations

VariationWhat the attacker wantsWhere it shows up

The variation matters for defense. A team that only protects the headquarters, because it feels like the target, leaves exactly the sites that planners prefer.

Common attacker mistakes

Recon operators are often careful, but they do slip. These are the slips defenders can actually catch:

  • Leaving map-service request patterns: scripted, fast, machine-like bursts against map tiles from one address, instead of slow human browsing.
  • Over-focusing on the HQ while branch sites, depots, and new builds stay unwatched and weaker.
  • Reusing the same staging domain and user agents across targets, which links separate campaigns together for analysts.
  • Keeping the site profile in one file: recovered laptops and shared notes have ended physical plans mid-way.

Danger

Using these methods against real sites or systems you do not own is illegal. Trespass, unlawful surveillance, and unauthorized access all carry criminal penalties in most countries, and civil claims can follow. Practice only in the lab, on your own organization with written permission, or on published examples.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Now flip the view. A SOC (Security Operations Center: the team that watches alerts) mostly cannot see someone reading a map. This section lists the faint signals that do exist, where to find them, and a calm triage flow for the day one of them shows up on your dashboard.

Note · why detection is hard

The victim’s servers are not involved when an attacker reads a public map, a registry, or a news article. There is no packet to your network, no login, and no process. You are left with echoes: the short moments when the collection touched your published data instead of a third-party site. Design your monitoring around those echoes, and accept that silence is normal here.

The faint artifacts worth hunting

Site-page scraping

One address pulls /locations, /facilities, /offices, and /contact in fast sequence. The CDN or the WAF sees this even when the site itself feels fine. CDN means content delivery network, the service that serves website copies worldwide and logs requests. WAF means web application firewall, a filter that sits in front of a website.

Bulk image downloads

Press and facility photos pulled in bulk, often with simple command-line downloaders rather than a browser. Each image may carry EXIF (Exchangeable Image File Format: hidden data inside photos, like GPS coordinates) metadata.

EXIF you find first

Run your own scan of published media. If you discover photos with GPS data before an attacker uses them, that is a finding you can fix the same day. Discovery-by-self is a success, not a failure.

Look-alike domains

New domains that mix your brand with a site or city word. Brand monitors and CT logs (Certificate Transparency: public list of TLS certificates) surface them days or weeks before they are used.

Site-survey calls

Reception or the help desk reports a caller asking detailed questions about tours, badge issuance, or delivery gates. People are the sensor that never needs a license and covers places your logs cannot.

Correlation is the win

Any one artifact is weak. A scrape, a new look-alike domain, and a survey call in the same week tell one story. Feed them all into the SIEM (Security Information and Event Management: the central log platform) as low-level context events so an analyst can join them.

Where to look

  • CDN and WAF logs, for repeated reads of location, facility, and contact pages from one address.
  • Media or asset-server logs, for bulk downloads of press and facility images.
  • Brand and digital-risk alert feeds, plus CT log monitors, for site-named look-alike domains.
  • Help-desk tickets and reception notes, for site-survey style contacts from unknown people.
  • Physical security reports: badge anomalies and tailgating (walking in behind a badged person) attempts, used as context rather than proof.
  • Your own published content: photos, job ads, and press pages, reviewed on a schedule.

How this feeds later stages

Physical intrusion leads to insider risk

A mapped site becomes an entry plan. An entered site becomes a planted device, a cloned badge, or a new hire who was never screened. Every later insider alert is cheaper to investigate if you already recorded the earlier physical signals.

Site knowledge powers vishing

Vishing (voice phishing: phone calls that trick people) gets far better when the caller names the right depot, the right shift, and the right manager. Expect the follow-up to arrive by phone, not only by mail, and train the people who answer.

Triage flow: from signal to action

Use this when any artifact above fires. It is written for the finding most teams meet first: geotagged photos you discover yourself. The same shape works for the other artifacts.

1

Confirm the finding. List the exact photos and the data they expose. A coordinate of a public office may be low risk; a coordinate of a data-center entrance is not.

2

Scope which photos. Crawl your own domains and any press kit mirrors. Find every copy, including partner sites that republish your media without changes.

3

Scrub and republish. Strip the metadata, replace the files, and fix the pipeline so new uploads are clean by default. A one-time cleanup is not a control; the pipeline is.

4

Check who downloaded. Pull media logs for the affected files. Note bulk downloaders and their addresses, and add them to a watchlist rather than a blocklist: they may be customers.

5

Send the awareness note. Short, factual, and blame-free: what was exposed, what changed, and what staff should report if someone asks about the site.

6

Record and tune. Log the case, add the detection if it does not exist yet, and set a review date for the publishing process and the ad templates.

Good practice

Treat physical and cyber signals as one picture. A badge anomaly means little alone, and a web scrape means little alone. Together, with a look-alike domain, they describe a plan. Your SIEM is the only place where that join can happen cheaply.

Metrics that matter

Four numbers keep this work honest over a quarter. Count them from the artifacts above, not from effort.

  • Published photos with GPS metadata: the count should reach and stay at zero.
  • Median time from a staff report to a recorded ticket: minutes, not days.
  • Look-alike domains actioned within your target time: track the percentage.
  • Cases where at least two sources were joined: the measure of correlation actually happening.

Note · what the confidence badges mean

High means several independent, credible sources describe the same behavior, or a government body has published it. Moderate means one or two good sources, or reporting whose details differ between tellings. Treat both levels as planning input: they help you guess what may happen to you, and they never prove anything about your own organization.

The intelligence cycle for this technique

Intel work has three stages, and each one has a job:

1

Collect. Pull the sources above on a schedule. Save the actor names, the site types they study, and the follow-up actions they chose.

2

Analyze. Compare what you collected with your own sites and sector. Keep what matches, drop what does not, and mark how much you trust each fact.

3

Apply. Turn each match into work: a rule to tune, a review to schedule, or a briefing to write. Intelligence that changes nothing is only reading.

From actor to detection

Intelligence is only useful when it changes what you build. The short path from the actor table above to the rules in 5.5 looks like this:

1

Read the behavior, not the name. “APT28 researches embassy locations” becomes, for you: location pages are read in bursts before lures appear. That behavior has a rule (R1) whether or not APT28 ever calls you.

2

Match the site type. Scattered Spider stories involve depots, stores, and staff-level pretexts. If those are your sites, R3 and R4 are the best first rules to tune.

3

Set expectations from cases. The DarkSide case shows the recon leaving no trace, so you plan for silence and spend effort on exposure controls instead of chasing an impossible map-viewing alert.

Applying this intelligence

Use the tables in three passes. First, match: if your sector or your sites resemble an actor’s pattern, raise the weight of the detections in 5.5 that catch their style of recon. Second, sequence: let the actor habits order your exposure reviews, so the review that matters most happens first. Third, tune: the same reporting gives you realistic false-positive stories, which you will need when the rules fire on a real job applicant. Write the sector match down in the detection notes, so the next engineer knows why the thresholds are what they are.

6 Map and imagery service scraping Low Network Automated collection of site imagery and map tiles at scale, faster than any human could look. None: the services are public and the collection is cheap. Faster, cheaper recon for whoever bothers to script it. Almost invisible to the victim, which is why it ranks low here: you rarely even know it happened.

Note · how to read the severity badges

Severity here mixes likelihood and damage, chosen to fit a mid-size organization with several sites. A High threat is one that has hurt organizations like yours often and hurts badly. A Medium threat needs a mistake on your side, like an unscrubbed photo, before it bites. A Low threat is real but narrow: it applies to few organizations or yields little on its own. If your most important system is one single site, re-rank with that site in mind.

Platform categorization summary

Each threat above lands in one of three buckets, and each bucket has a different owner. Knowing the bucket tells you who to call when the detection fires.

Physical (you own it)

Tailgating checks, visitor badges, dock supervision, and guard patrol routes. These controls sit on sites you control, so you can measure and improve them directly.

Network / Cloud (you can log it)

CDN, WAF, and media logs, plus brand and CT feeds. You may not see the recon itself, but you see the echoes where it touches your own services. These logs feed the rules in 5.5.

People (you train it)

Reception, help desk, guards, and staff who take the calls. A trained person converts a survey pretext into a report, which is the highest-value signal on this page.

5.4Logs and Patterns

Six log sources carry the faint signals of this technique. For each one you get four things: the fields to care about, normal next to suspicious, the detection logic in one sentence, and a realistic sample event. Sample addresses use the documentation ranges, so nothing here points at a real person.

a) CDN and WAF web logs

Fields to use: client address (c-ip), timestamp, method, requested path (cs-uri-stem), status code, response bytes, referrer, and user agent.

PatternWhat it looks like
NormalMany addresses spread across all pages; humans wander, pause, and come back on different days; mobile agents dominate.
SuspiciousOne address requests /locations, then /facilities, then /offices, then /contact in seconds, often with a script-style user agent and map-tile requests mixed in.

Detection logic: count GET requests to location-style paths grouped by client address in a short window, and alert when one address exceeds what a reading human does. The same logs also carry reads of event and leadership pages, which rule R6 in 5.5 uses for executive travel tracking.

The sample is written in CLF (Common Log Format: the classic one-line-per-request web log format). Six location-style pages in thirty-one seconds from one address is the shape to alert on. Embedded map-tile calls from the same address are a bonus signal, because they suggest the visitor is building a picture, not reading an article.

FieldMeaningWhy it helps here
c-ipAddress of the requesterOne address touching many pages is the scraper signature.
cs-methodHTTP verb, here GETLocation mapping is read-only, so only GET matters.
cs-uri-stemPath of the requested pageLocation-style paths group together and name the interest.

b) Media and photo library logs

Fields to use: event time (ts), requester address (ip), user agent, asset path (asset_path), HTTP status, bytes served, and a running count per requester.

PatternWhat it looks like
NormalImages load in ones and twos as readers browse articles; addresses are diverse; agents are browsers.
SuspiciousOne address pulls dozens of /media files in minutes with a downloader agent, including plant and facility shots.

Detection logic: count media downloads grouped by client address per hour, and flag requests whose asset paths include facility or press photography directories.

The agent name is the loudest detail here. A scripting library is a legitimate tool for many jobs, so treat it as a reason to look, not as proof of anything. The asset paths are what make the look worthwhile: press and facility directories are exactly what a site profile needs.

c) Brand and digital-risk feed

Fields to use: event time, feed name, alert type, registered domain, registrar, creation date, mail records present (mx), brand tokens found, and the feed’s own risk score.

PatternWhat it looks like
NormalAlerts about brand typos that misspell the company name; most never get mail records or certificates.
SuspiciousA domain that joins the brand with a site or city word, registered days ago, with MX records ready for lures.

Detection logic: match feed alerts whose domain contains both a brand token and a site or city token, and prioritize the ones that already have mail records. Typosquatting means registering a look-alike domain, and the site-word variant is the version this technique feeds.

The second record is the one to work first. It is new, it carries the site word “hq”, and it already has mail records. Mail records usually mean someone plans to send lures from it.

d) Help-desk and ITSM tickets

Fields to use: event time, ticket id, channel, report type, requester (often a receptionist), subject, named site, free-text summary, handler, and a risk tag. ITSM means IT service management: the systems behind the help desk.

PatternWhat it looks like
NormalVisitors booked through the process, with company names, purchase numbers, and named contacts.
SuspiciousAn unknown person asking where the delivery gate is, how badge issuance works, and whether a tour can happen today.

Detection logic: tag tickets whose subject mentions a site visit, tour, survey, or badge process and whose requester has no verified company behind them. Route those tickets to the SOC as context.

These tickets are useful information that costs nothing extra. They name the site the attacker finds interesting, which is often not the site security would have guessed.

e) Physical security reports

Fields to use: event time, source system, report type, site, free-text detail, gate or camera reference, and the action taken.

PatternWhat it looks like
NormalBadge events inside booked windows, visitors signed in, and tours that match the calendar.
Context-worthyBadges active outside booked windows, repeated dock visits without deliveries, and tailgating attempts at the same lobby.

Detection logic: none for this technique directly. Use these reports as correlation context in the SIEM, joined by site name and time window, so a cyber alert can inherit the physical context.

f) Mail gateway logs

Fields to use: event time, sender address, sender domain, recipient, direction (inbound or outbound), subject line, verdict, and any flags the gateway adds. The gateway is the service that receives outside mail before it reaches staff inboxes. This source feeds rule R5 in 5.5.

PatternWhat it looks like
NormalInbound mail from known partners and customers; subjects name orders, tickets, and meetings; verdicts are mostly clean; sender domains match the sender’s company.
SuspiciousInbound mail whose subject names a real site, a depot, a dock, or a badge process. Or a sender domain that mixes your brand with a site word: hq, depot, plant, campus.

Detection logic: flag inbound messages whose subject names a site, a dock, a warehouse, or a badge process. Also flag messages whose sender domain contains your brand plus a site word. Route both for review next to the brand-feed alerts.

The first record is the one to work: the domain carries the brand plus a site word, the subject pushes a gate pass, and the flags show a new external sender. Compare it with the brand-feed sample above: the same site word in both is one actor at two stages.

Joining the sources: where the value is

Each source above is weak alone. The engineering win is the join, and the join lives in the SIEM. Store every source with three shared fields: event time, site name, and requester or actor. Then a single query can line up the story across sources.

The exact syntax differs by platform, but the shape is stable: one row per site-and-actor pair, with a count of the sources that saw them. Two sources in a week is worth a look; three is a case.

Weekly hunt queries

Beyond the six alert rules, run these quiet hunts once a week. Each answers one question you will otherwise never think to ask.

Keep the results in a note file with the date. Trends matter more than single weeks: an address that returns every quarter is more interesting than one busy Tuesday.

Warning · what these logs cannot show

None of these sources shows someone viewing a map service, a satellite image, or a registry page. They show the edges where collection touched your own data or your staff. Silence here is normal and proves little. That is why this page pairs the logs with the controls in section 7: the publishing side matters as much as the watching side.

5.5Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

Sigma (a YAML text format that describes detection rules) lets you write a rule once and translate it into many SIEM dialects. Splunk Search Processing Language (SPL) is the query language of the Splunk platform, one common SIEM. Each detection below ships as a Sigma rule plus a matching SPL query, with the mapping, false positives, and tuning notes next to it. The counting rules (R1, R2, and R6) use a counting condition, so give them a time window in your SIEM and a limit that keeps alert volume sensible.

Real-World Case Study

The Colonial Pipeline incident of April and May 2021 is the clearest public example of how physical geography shapes a cyber extortion. The facts below come from reporting by Mandiant, CISA, and major press. The framing is defensive: what the story teaches people who watch networks and sites.

Context: who and what

DarkSide was a ransomware group that ran a RaaS (ransomware as a service: a kit and platform that affiliates rent to launch attacks) operation. Colonial Pipeline operated the largest refined-product pipeline system in the United States, roughly 5,500 miles long, moving fuel from the Gulf Coast to the Northeast.

The company is infrastructure in the plain sense of the word: its product leaves through a fixed corridor into storage farms and delivery points that are marked on public maps.

Map to the technique

The corridor, its storage, and its delivery geography were public knowledge long before the incident, and understanding them did not require any intrusion. An attacker who studied the company’s real-world sites knew the single point that everything depends on: halt the line and several states notice shortages within days.

That is T1591.001 thinking applied to a nation-scale target. The mapping made the extortion credible and the shutdown decision inevitable.

Timeline of the incident (widely reported dates)

  1. 2021-04-29

    Initial access. Attackers used a leaked account for a virtual private network (VPN: a private tunnel into a company network). The account had no multi-factor authentication (MFA: a second check, like a code from an app, beyond the password), so the password alone opened the door.

  2. 2021-05-03 to 05-06

    Movement and theft. The actors moved across the IT network and pulled out about 100 GB of data, much of it billing and invoice records, to use as pressure later.

  3. 2021-05-07

    Ransomware and the physical decision. DarkSide ransomware started encrypting IT systems. Colonial halted all pipeline operations: a physical decision made because of a cyber event.

  4. 2021-05-08 to 05-09

    Shutdown continues. Lines stood still while forensics and recovery planning started. Emergency declarations began as supply planning failed.

  5. 2021-05-10 to 05-11

    Panic buying. Fuel shortages and price spikes appeared across several states, and some stations ran dry even where supply existed.

  6. 2021-05-12

    Restart begins. Colonial started restarting lines, with full service still days away and terminals working through backlogs.

  7. 2021-05-15

    Back toward normal. The company announced that most systems were back to normal operation across the network.

  8. 2021-06-07

    Partial recovery of funds. The Department of Justice announced it had seized about 2.3 million US dollars in bitcoin, part of the roughly 4.4 million dollar ransom.

The attack chain in steps

1

Learn the target. Public geography and the company’s own disclosures explain what the pipeline carries and what a halt would cost. This is the T1591.001 shape: cheap, legal, invisible.

2

Find a way in. A leaked VPN account without MFA gives the first way in. No exploit is needed, only a password someone else already lost.

3

Move and map the inside. The actors explore IT systems and find where the valuable files live.

4

Steal pressure material. About 100 GB of billing and invoice data leaves the network before any encryption starts.

5

Trigger the encryption. The ransomware locks IT systems, and a note names the group and the terms.

6

Apply pressure. Double pressure of encryption and stolen data meets a company that has physically stopped moving fuel. The threat is credible because the geography is understood.

Tools used (from public reporting)

Public reporting by Mandiant and CISA names the tools. A leaked VPN credential gave the first way in. Cobalt Strike (a paid testing tool that attackers abuse to control computers) ran the hands-on activity. Built-in Windows administration tools moved the attackers around. The DarkSide locker did the encryption. About 100 GB of billing data was staged and removed before encryption started. No exotic tool was needed: the chain worked with rented, ordinary parts.

Detection opportunities that existed before the outage

Reported details suggest several small signals came before the encryption, each of which maps to this page:

  • A VPN login without MFA is exactly the identity gap the controls in section 7 close first.
  • Large data transfers before encryption fit the classic exfiltration window that SIEM rules watch.
  • The company’s own public materials explained the corridor and its delivery points, which is the T1591.001 exposure itself.
  • The billing data that later backed the extortion sat where ordinary access review would have questioned who could read it.

None of these would have stopped the recon of the corridor. All of them would have shortened the outage or softened the pressure.

Warning · where the detection point really was

The door was a VPN account without MFA: plain identity hygiene, long before any alert. The physical-location recon itself left no trace for the victim, and no product would have flagged the corridor maps. What made the event historic was physical: one single point that everything depended on, and a rational operator chose to halt it. Read this as three lessons at once: protect identity, assume recon is invisible, and map your own single points of failure before someone else maps them for you.

Containment (stopping the attack from spreading)

Halting operations contained the physical risk of moving fuel while attackers held access. On the cyber side, Colonial isolated and took systems offline. The ICS/OT network (operational technology: the systems that run machines, like pumps and valves) was reported not directly hit, which kept the physical harm to a controlled stop.

Eradication (removing the attacker for good)

Systems were rebuilt from clean sources, credentials were rotated, and remote access paths were reviewed before restoration. The stolen data could not be pulled back, which is why the ransom demand still had force.

Recovery

Lines restarted in stages, terminals worked through backlogs, and some operations ran manually until systems were trusted again. Full service returned over several days, not hours.

Impact and consequences

The impact had several parts. There was a multi-day national fuel disruption on the East Coast. A ransom of about 4.4 million US dollars was paid, and about 2.3 million of it was later recovered. The event also brought lasting policy attention to pipeline and infrastructure security. The indirect costs, from panic buying to deferred deliveries, were larger than the ransom itself.

Team response and communication

The company published frequent, honest updates and worked with federal partners, including CISA and the FBI. Saying what was known, and what was not, kept rumor from filling the gap. For a physical-infrastructure business, that clear, regular messaging is a control, not an extra.

What regulators and CISA advised after

CISA and the FBI published a joint advisory (AA21-131A, June 2021) with the attack details and concrete advice. It says: enforce MFA on every remote access path, keep a tested response plan, and segment networks so a workstation compromise cannot reach critical systems. TSA followed with security directives for pipeline operators. The lesson generalizes beyond pipelines: the same advice covers any business with one physical point of failure.

Lessons learned for defenders

  • Put MFA everywhere on remote access. A password alone is not a door, and leaked passwords are sold online.
  • Map your most important physical dependencies: know which single asset, corridor, or site can stop the business.
  • Segment IT and ICS/OT (operational technology: the systems that run machines, like pumps and valves) so IT ransomware cannot reach the machines that move the product.
  • Rehearse manual operations, so a halt does not become a blackout of every process at once.
  • Review how much site detail you publish about critical sites, and who approves it.
  • Prepare the message: frequent, honest updates reduce panic and leave more room for recovery.

Six moves that cost little and remove most of the value an attacker gets from this technique:

  • Turn on MFA for all remote access, especially VPN accounts that predate the policy.
  • Ask PR and HR to review how many site addresses they publish, and agree on a region-level default.
  • Retain and search CDN logs for bursts against location, facility, and contact pages.
  • Add brand monitoring for domains that mix your brand with site or city words.
  • Brief reception and the help desk on site-survey pretexts, and give them a one-click way to report.

Small organizations and home offices publish site data too, just with fewer people watching. Turn off location tags on phone cameras before posting photos of your workshop or storefront. Use registered-address privacy options where your registry offers them. Keep delivery schedules and holiday closures out of public posts. And treat a caller who knows your address like a phishing mail: verify through a number you already have, not the one they gave you.

8

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

Every mistake below was seen in real programs, and each one is fixable in days. The cards name the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that keeps it fixed.

1. Full addresses of every site in press releases

Symptom
A growth announcement lists street addresses for plants and depots that security never reviewed.
Root cause
PR copies legal entity data, and nobody asks whether the detail serves the story.
Fix
Republish with region-level locations, and keep full addresses in private facility records.
Prevention
A standing review step: security checks site mentions in anything public before release.

2. EXIF left in published photos

Symptom
A scan finds GPS coordinates in press images, often of exactly the buildings you would rather not map.
Root cause
The publishing pipeline forwards the original files, and no step strips metadata.
Fix
Scrub and replace the files, then check the mirrors that copied them.
Prevention
Strip metadata on upload by default; allow exceptions only for approved images.

3. Job ads naming secure facilities

Symptom
An ad says a data-center technician will work at a named site with named systems.
Root cause
Recruiters paste site details because they are proud of the project.
Fix
Republish the ad with a region-level location and a generic role title.
Prevention
Ad templates with location guidance, and a quick HR-security check for facility ads.

4. Badge anomalies never correlated with cyber alerts

Symptom
Investigations miss that a door event happened during the same hour as a suspicious login.
Root cause
Physical and cyber events live in separate queues with separate owners.
Fix
Feed badge alerts into the SIEM as context events, joined by site and time.
Prevention
A weekly joint review between facilities and the SOC, with a shared case log.

5. Brand monitor ignores site-name look-alikes

Symptom
Alerts fire on brand typos, but a domain with your brand plus a depot word sits unnoticed.
Root cause
The keyword list contains the brand only, never the site and city words.
Fix
Add site and city tokens from your exposure inventory to the watch list.
Prevention
A quarterly keyword review that follows changes in your published site names.

6. CDN logs not retained or not normalized

Symptom
Nobody can answer who read the locations pages last month, because the logs are gone or unsearchable.
Root cause
Short default retention, and fields never mapped for search.
Fix
Ship CDN and WAF logs to the SIEM with parsed fields and a documented retention period.
Prevention
A logging policy with owners per source, tested by a quarterly restore-and-query drill.

7. Visitor data not in the SIEM, even as context

Symptom
An analyst working a mail alert has no idea a tailgating attempt happened the same morning.
Root cause
Physical systems export nothing, or exports stop after the first setup sprint.
Fix
Export daily visitor and badge reports as low-cost context events.
Prevention
Monitor the export job itself, so silence means breakage, not calm.

8. Awareness training never mentions site-survey pretexts

Symptom
Staff happily explain the badge process to a stranger who knew the site name.
Root cause
Training covers phishing mails only, so phone and door pretexts stay invisible.
Fix
Add a short module with two real-shaped stories: the courier and the surveyor.
Prevention
Refresh the module when staff reports or intel show a new pretext in your sector.

9. No owner between facilities, PR, and security

Symptom
An exposure finding bounces between teams for months, and everyone agrees it matters.
Root cause
Public site information has no named data owner, so no one can approve a change.
Fix
Name an owner for public site information and a deputy for reviews.
Prevention
Write the ownership into the publishing workflow, not into a meeting note.

10. Alert thresholds never tuned

Symptom
The page-scrape rule fires on every scanner, or never at all, and analysts ignore it either way.
Root cause
Counts were set once from a blog post and never matched to your traffic.
Fix
Baseline a normal week, tune counts and windows, and tag known friendly scanners.
Prevention
A quarterly tuning pass, written up next to each rule like in section 5.5.

11. No takedown process for site-themed domains

Symptom
A confirmed look-alike domain stays alive for weeks because nobody knows who to call.
Root cause
No retainer, no template, and no practice run of the takedown path.
Fix
Contract a takedown service and file the first request as a drill.
Prevention
A runbook with the registrar contact path, target times, and an annual test.

12. Partner sites republish your photos untouched

Symptom
You scrub your media library, but the geotagged originals still live on a distributor’s page.
Root cause
The press kit was shared as original files, and nobody told the partners.
Fix
Send scrubbed replacements and ask for removal of the originals; check the biggest mirrors yourself.
Prevention
Publish only scrubbed files in the kit, and list partner mirrors in the exposure inventory.

Best-practice habits

Daily
Skim new alerts, the brand feed, and help-desk tickets for site-survey words.
Weekly
Review top talkers on location pages, badge anomalies with facilities, and confirm the EXIF scrub pipeline ran.
Monthly
Re-run the public exposure inventory, review new photos and ads, tune thresholds, and test the takedown contact.

Response checklist

  • Confirm the artifact: what exactly was seen, on which site, and when.
  • Check who else touched the same data in the same window.
  • Fix the exposure: scrub or unpublish the photo, adjust the ad, review the release.
  • Add the look-alike domain to blocklists and monitors, and start the takedown path.
  • Brief facilities and reception, with the site name in the note.
  • Record the case and set a review date for the control that failed.

Questions people actually ask

Can we block this technique?
Not the reading itself. You reduce its value by publishing less, scrubbing photos, and watching the edges where collection echoes back to you.
Should we alert on every location-page visitor?
No. Read counts by address over time; one human visit is nothing, and a burst from one address is the signal.
Who owns this problem?
Public site detail belongs to PR and HR; the echoes belong to the SOC; the doors belong to facilities. One named owner coordinates the three.
Is a single geotagged photo a real incident?
Grade it by what the photo shows. A public lobby is trivia; a data-center entrance or a generator yard is a finding worth a case.
Do the six rules replace physical controls?
No. They tell you the mapping is happening. Badges, visitor process, and training are what stop the follow-up.
