Overview

T1591.003 describes a quiet step that attackers take before any attack: they learn the rhythm of the target business. This section explains the technique in plain words, shows why timing matters so much in security, and lists the public sources that give a schedule away.

What the technique is

MITRE ATT&CK is a free, public catalog of real attacker behavior. Every behavior gets an ID, so defenders, auditors, and security tools can talk about attacks in one shared language. This page covers T1591.003 - Identify Business Tempo. The official MITRE description says that adversaries may gather information about the victim's business tempo. That information includes operational hours and days of the week. It can also show when the victim buys and ships hardware and software. Attackers gather it in different ways. They can ask people directly, for example through phishing for information (T1598). They can also read data the victim exposes online, such as social media and victim-owned websites. That makes this technique a form of OSINT (open-source intelligence: facts anyone can collect from public sources).

The output of this step is not a stolen password or a hacked server. It is a timing calendar. Picture a page in the attacker's notebook. It reads: quiet between 23:00 and 05:00, small crew on Fridays, plant closed the first week of August, payroll on the 25th. Nothing in that note breaks a law by itself. The danger comes later, when the attacker uses the note to pick the moment of a real attack.

Who does this? Everyone from curious teenagers to organized groups, because the price of entry is a browser. The pattern shows up in ransomware operations (ransomware is malware that locks files and demands payment), in fraud campaigns, and in espionage cases. The table in Section 5.3 ranks which uses hurt the most, and the case study in Section 6 shows the pattern operating at national scale.

Note

Schedules are only one kind of public data. Attackers also collect staff names, host names, and exposed certificates. For example, a CT log (Certificate Transparency: a public list of TLS certificates, the encryption certificates behind HTTPS) shows which internal system names a company has published. Those facts belong to other reconnaissance techniques: staff names to T1589 (Gather Victim Identity Information), host names and certificates to T1592 (Gather Victim Host Information). This guide stays on timing.

Why it matters: response speed is a security control

Detection alone does not stop an attack. Someone must see the alert, understand it, decide, and act. That speed depends on people. A SOC (security operations center: the team that watches alerts and coordinates response) with enough people on shift reacts in minutes. The same team on a holiday reacts in hours. Attackers know this, so they look for the hour when the fewest people are watching.

Several normal business rhythms stretch the gap between "alert fires" and "attack contained":

  • Holidays and weekends. IR (incident response: the planned actions a team takes during a security incident) runs on fewer people, and specialists are hard to reach.
  • Night shifts. The night team is smaller, and escalation paths are longer at 03:00.
  • Change freezes and patch freezes (a period when system changes are not allowed). A critical fix can wait days when the freeze rules are strict and no emergency path exists.
  • Payroll and quarter ends. Finance staff work under deadline pressure, which is exactly the state that BEC (business email compromise: fraud where attackers impersonate staff or vendors to move payments) needs to succeed.
  • Maintenance windows. Systems sit in planned, half-changed states that look odd and can hide real oddness.

Attackers do not need to break anything during recon. They only need to know WHEN the defenders are few. That is why tempo work is cheap, legal-looking, and hard to punish, and why defenders must treat staffing as part of the security design.

One contrast is worth stating. Most recon techniques ask WHAT or WHO: which hosts exist, which people work here, which software runs. Tempo asks WHEN. That makes it both easy to collect and hard to attribute: there is no stolen document to find later, only a pattern of attention. It also means the defense is mostly operational rather than technical, which is why this guide keeps returning to staffing, verification habits, and rehearsal.

Typical public sources of tempo

Each source below is ordinary business communication. Each leaks a small piece of the schedule, and an attacker who joins the pieces gets a full calendar without touching your network. Most of these pages sit behind a CDN (content delivery network: servers around the world that cache and serve your web pages). Its logs matter later in Section 5.

Company social posts

"We are closed for the holiday", "see you on Monday", birthday shout-outs with job titles. Closure posts often carry exact dates, and sometimes exact hours and site names.

Job ads with shift patterns

"Night shift 22:00-06:00", "weekend cover required", "hybrid, Tuesday to Thursday on site". Ads describe the working rhythm with surprising precision.

Careers pages

Team pages, office photos with wall calendars, and "day in the life" stories. They show which teams exist and when those teams work.

Event calendars

Public webinars, open days, town halls, and factory open-house dates. Each one marks when staff attention goes elsewhere.

Out-of-office patterns

Autoreplies on mailing lists and support addresses name the absent person, the dates, and often the deputy. A week of replies maps a vacation season.

Conference attendance

Sponsor lists, speaker bios, and booth rota pages show which engineers will be out of the office and for how many days.

Published maintenance windows

"Planned downtime: Saturday 02:00-04:00" tells an attacker when the network is already in an approved odd state.

Review sites and forums

Employee reviews describe shifts, peak seasons, and management response times. Local news adds seasonal patterns for factories, ports, and shops.

Good practice

Read your own public footprint (everything your organization shows online) once per quarter, as an outsider would. Mark every date your posts reveal. Then ask one question: if an attacker had this calendar, which weekend would they pick? That answer is your priority staffing weekend, and Sections 7 and 8 tell you what to do about it.

Animated Simulation

The animation above shows the full reconnaissance story in eight steps: what the attacker reads, what they build, and what your team can still see. Press Play to watch it run, or click a numbered chip under the canvas to jump straight to any step.

How to read it

Each box is a player in the story. Red is the attacker. Blue is a target service, such as a public web page or a job board. Violet is attacker infrastructure, such as a staging server. Green is the defender side: your SOC and on-call staff.

A glow marks the boxes that are active in the current step. Moving dots are data flows. Green tags, such as "Web log alert", mark defender evidence: something your side could actually record while that step happens.

The same story in words

This list repeats every animation step in text form, so screen readers, printouts, and anyone who prefers text still get the full story. Each step ends with a "Defender view" line: what reaches your logs, or what to improve.

  1. Step 1 - Read company posts

    The attacker opens the company's public channels and reads like an interested customer. Celebration posts, closure notices, and "back on Monday" updates reveal when offices sit empty. The attacker writes each date into a private timing calendar.

    Defender view: this step leaves no trace at the victim at all. The only fix is upstream: publish closures with fewer details, and never pair site addresses with exact hours.

  2. Step 2 - Extract shift patterns

    Job boards and careers pages come next. Ads that mention night shifts, weekend cover, or "quiet night hours" describe the staffing rhythm of the target. The attacker copies each shift line straight into the calendar.

    Defender view: careers-page scraping shows up in CDN and web logs as a burst of requests to /careers and /shift paths from one address. Rule R1 in Section 5.5 catches it.

  3. Step 3 - Collect closures and events

    Holiday calendars, maintenance notices, and public event pages complete the picture. Each closure adds an "empty office" block to the attacker's map. Repeat visits to the same schedule pages are the visible echo.

    Defender view: watch for repeat requests to schedule and events pages. If the same address returns week after week, treat it as recon, not curiosity.

  4. Step 4 - Check finance deadlines

    Payday patterns, quarter ends, and invoice runs are noted as pressure windows. An urgent "payment change" email lands best when the finance team already faces a deadline. This is the raw data behind BEC timing.

    Defender view: nothing reaches your sensors in this step. Finance calendars are often public, and nobody meant them to be. Audit who can see them and keep exact dates out of public material.

  5. Step 5 - Build the timing calendar

    Alone, each fact is harmless. Joined together, holidays, shifts, freezes, and paydays become a quiet-week map of the whole company. The attacker marks the weeks where coverage is weakest.

    Defender view: the plan now exists entirely outside your network, and no signal reached you. Your counter is readiness that does not depend on knowing the attacker's plan.

  6. Step 6 - Pick the quiet window

    The attacker chooses the moment: often a holiday weekend with a small on-call team and a change freeze that slows fixes. The window becomes the anchor date for the whole operation.

    Defender view: you cannot cancel holidays, but you can staff them. A holiday rotation with the same depth as weekdays removes most of this advantage.

  7. Step 7 - Stage the operation

    Lures are written, payloads are prepared, and everything is timed to launch inside the chosen window. The attacker rehearses the launch hour in detail.

    Defender view: odd-hours ticket and login alerts are your earliest echoes of staging. Report and correlate them early, even when each one looks small.

  8. Step 8 - Hand-off to the attack

    Timing is locked. Phishing waves, vishing calls, and ransomware phases begin, now tracked under techniques like T1566. Reconnaissance ends; intrusion starts.

    Defender view: from here, standard network and identity detections take over. Correlate the earlier tempo signals with the first intrusion alerts and start your IR process on purpose, not by surprise.

Watch the pattern across steps, not just one frame. Steps 1, 4, and 5 are invisible to you by design. Steps 2, 3, and 7 are the ones your logs can catch, and step 8 is where conventional detections begin. That split is the whole defense problem of T1591.003 in one animation.

3

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section shows the attacker's side of the technique. It covers why timing is worth studying, how the collection work is done, and what a realistic tempo operation looks like. Everything here is written for defenders, and the lab exercises at the end touch only your own public pages.

Why attackers study tempo

Attacks are contests of speed. The side that reacts faster wins, and reaction speed follows staffing. A careful attacker therefore hunts for three kinds of window:

Fewest responders

Nights, weekends, and holidays cut the size of the team on watch. Ransomware that starts at 02:00 before a holiday gives the attacker hours of quiet work before anyone can stop it.

Freeze cover

Change freezes and patch freezes slow the fix. When the emergency path is unclear, a broken system stays broken while the attacker moves sideways.

Deadline pressure

Payday and quarter ends put finance teams under time stress. BEC fraud rides that stress: a "last-minute bank change" email works best when the deadline is real.

Tempo study also improves scam quality. A vishing caller who knows the night-shift schedule knows exactly when the help desk is staffed by one junior agent. A phishing mail about "the plant closure" lands better when a closure is actually happening. Good timing turns average attacks into effective ones.

How it is done, conceptually

Tempo collection needs no special tools. The basic method has four moves:

1

Harvest. Collect every public date the company has published: social posts, job ads, event pages, press releases, maintenance notices, conference rosters.

2

Normalize. Convert everything into one timezone and one calendar. Resolve conflicts, for example a holiday post that matches only one country office.

3

Score. Rate each week by expected defender capacity: on-call size, freeze status, finance deadlines, planned staff absences.

4

Validate. Confirm quietness with cheap probes: how fast support email is answered, how long the main phone rings, when auto-replies switch on.

None of these moves touches victim systems. That is the uncomfortable part: the whole technique lives on the public internet, outside every sensor you own.

Prerequisites for the attacker

Access
Only the public internet. No victim credentials and no victim contact are needed.
Skills
Reading, patience, and a spreadsheet. This is one of the cheapest techniques in the whole catalog.
Time
Hours for a small company, days for a large one. The data is stable, so it can be refreshed slowly.
Cost
Near zero, which is why low-skill groups use it constantly.
Risk to attacker
Very low. Reading public pages looks like ordinary web traffic and is rarely attributed.

Lab-only exercises

These three exercises are safe and legal because every target is your own organization, seen from the outside. They teach the defender's version of the skill: seeing your own schedule the way an attacker would.

Exercise 1: the outsider calendar

Read your company's public posts for the last twelve months. Mark every date an outsider could learn. Compare the list with your real quiet weeks, and score how close the outsider calendar gets to the truth.

Exercise 2: the job ad audit

Open every live job ad. Underline shift patterns, on-call duties, and site names. Ask: do these lines reveal when a site is empty? Rewrite one ad so it hires without publishing the rota.

Exercise 3: the calendar audit

Export the sharing settings of every team calendar in your productivity suite. List which ones are external or public, and revoke anything without a business owner. This exercise feeds directly into Rule R2 in Section 5.5.

Note

Keep lab work inside your own organization. Even passive recon against a company you do not own can break local computer-misuse laws, and many employers test exactly this in internal audits and interviews.

Common variations

Holiday-weekend ransomware

Launch late on the Friday before a long weekend. Encryption runs unattended while the fewest people are on call, and backups or fixes wait for the freeze to end.

Night-shift vishing

Call the help desk at 02:00 and ask for an MFA reset. Night queues are small, tired, and often measured on speed, so pressure works better than at noon.

Payday BEC

Send bank-change or urgent-invoice fraud during the payroll week. Deadline stress plus routine approval flows push a bad payment through quickly.

Maintenance-window cover

Act inside an announced change window. Scanners, restarts, and odd logins are expected then, so attacker noise hides inside approved change traffic.

Mistakes attackers make

Note

Some tempo data never touches the public internet: shift rota tools, workforce apps, and internal newsletters. Attackers reach that tier through other techniques, such as phishing for credentials or abusing a partner account. If your public footprint is clean, the next thing they probe is your people and your vendors, which is why Sections 7 and 8 pair publication hygiene with verification habits.

Single-timezone thinking

They plan for one timezone while the staff is global. A quiet night at the headquarters is a busy afternoon in another office. Defenders exploit this: alerts from the awake region still fire and can page someone who cares.

Assuming freezes mean silence

They read a change freeze as "nobody is watching". Smart defenders do the opposite: a freeze is exactly when extra monitoring and hunting run, because attackers like freezes too.

Danger

Using these methods against real systems or organizations you do not own is illegal in most countries, even when no damage is done. Computer-misuse laws cover unauthorized access and interference, and recon is often counted as the first step. Practice only on your own organization, on lab systems you built, or under a written, authorized engagement.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Now the defender's side. The hard truth is that tempo recon itself is almost invisible from inside the victim network. What you get are faint echoes in ordinary logs. This section explains what those echoes look like, where they live, and a simple triage flow for the day one shows up.

Why detection is hard

Reading a schedule leaves no victim-side trace. No malware lands, no packet crosses your firewall, no account fails a login. The attacker's browser loads public pages that thousands of honest visitors also load. So the first detection point is not the technique itself; it is the attacker's infrastructure touching your public web estate, and later, the timing being used.

The second problem is volume. Web logs and ticket queues already carry a lot of noise. A burst of careers-page hits looks like recruiting season. Ten night tickets look like a bad release. T1591.003 detection is therefore about joining weak patterns: single events are innocent, combinations are not. The rules in Section 5.5 encode exactly those combinations.

The faint artifacts

Careers and schedule page scraping

One address pulling dozens of /careers, /shift, and /schedule pages within minutes, often with a script's user agent, at hours that match no audience.

Externally shared calendars

Audit log entries where a calendar becomes external or public. Calendars carry rota, holidays, and cover plans, which is the raw material of a timing calendar.

Odd-hours ticket patterns

Night bursts of reset and access tickets from one source, or one phone number across several reset calls. This is often the first echo of tempo abuse, not recon itself.

Holiday-period alert gaps

Alerts that fire but wait hours for a first look during closures. Measuring that wait tells you the exact window an attacker would choose, so measure it on purpose.

Measure your own quiet window

Four numbers tell you how attractive your quiet hours are. Pull them from the SIEM once, then watch the trend month over month.

Median first-touch
Minutes from alert to first human action, weekdays versus holidays. The holiday number is your exposure.
Night ticket share
Percentage of reset tickets raised between 22:00 and 06:00, and how many had a verified callback.
Alert backlog slope
How fast unreviewed alerts pile up per watch hour at night and on weekends.
Coverage ratio
On-call responders available per shift on holidays versus weekdays.

Share the four numbers with leadership. They turn "we need holiday staffing" from an opinion into a measurement, which is a much easier budget conversation.

How it fits the attack chain

Tempo feeds the WHEN of later techniques, not the HOW:

  • T1566 phishing and T1566.004 spearphishing voice. The lure or call is timed for the quiet hour, when people check less carefully and verification is slow.
  • T1078 valid accounts. Credentials or resets obtained through timed pressure get used at odd hours first. Those first odd-hour logins are the strongest downstream signal you own.
  • Ransomware launch. The encryption moment lands inside the planned window, maximizing unattended runtime before someone stops it.
  • BEC fraud. Payment-change fraud runs in payroll weeks, when urgency is expected and normal.

Because the connection is timing, correlate across time as well as across hosts. The question that matters: did the odd-hour login follow a night ticket burst two hours earlier? That question is what the rules in Section 5.5 encode.

Triage flow: from quiet signal to decision

1

Spot the after-hours spike. A rule or a reviewer notices a night burst of tickets or careers-page requests. Note the source, the exact hours, and the topics.

2

Check related phone and credential activity. Search the same source and the same window for reset calls, approved MFA pushes, VPN logins, or new forwarding rules.

3

Verify the requester by callback. Call back on the number from your HR or vendor system, never the number in the ticket. Verify identity first; act second.

4

Raise holiday readiness. If a pattern is confirmed near a public closure, add watch depth: pull the on-call rotation forward, warn the help desk, raise MFA alerting.

5

Brief the on-call team. One short message: what was seen, what to expect, what to do if a reset request arrives. Keep it in the shift handover channel.

6

Record and tune. Close the loop: was it a recruiter crawler, a global teammate, or real recon? Update baselines and thresholds so the next review is faster.

Good practice

Write the triage steps above into your holiday runbook now, not during the incident. A one-page version pinned inside the help-desk tool is worth more than a perfect policy nobody can find at 03:00.

Note

The honest link between these controls and T1591.003 is response capacity. No auditor will ask "who read our holiday posts". They will ask "show me your December on-call coverage, your holiday tabletop record, and your last restore test". A tabletop (a tabletop exercise: a team rehearsal of an incident on paper) is the cheapest way to prove that coverage. Tempo attacks exploit exactly the gaps those questions probe, so treat staffing evidence as security evidence.

When you use the regulation table in an audit, keep the wording honest: the controls above do not mention T1591.003 by name, and that is fine. What you certify is that response works at any hour, which is precisely what tempo attacks test. Map each control to one artifact you can open on demand: a rota, a test report, a ticket with timestamps. Auditors trust artifacts more than adjectives.

How to read the confidence badges

High means government advisories or direct incident data, often with public artifacts. Moderate-High means consistent reporting from several reputable vendors without primary artifacts. From collection to application

Threat intelligence only works as a cycle, and each stage needs its own habit:

1

Collection. Pull the sources above on a fixed schedule: advisories and vendor blogs weekly, ISAC reports as they arrive, your own logs continuously.

2

Analysis. Filter for timing behavior. Ask of every new actor or campaign: did they pick the hour, the day, or the season on purpose? Keep the timing facts, not just the malware names.

3

Application. Turn each timing fact into one change on your calendar: a staffed weekend, a finance warning, or a monitoring rise during a freeze. Intelligence that changes nothing is only reading.

Applying tempo intelligence

Intel only helps when it changes a calendar. Three concrete uses:

  • On-call planning. If advisories say a crew targets holiday weekends, staff the named weekends first, before adding routine days.
  • Freeze policy. Write the freeze calendar so every freeze comes with extra monitoring, and rehearse the emergency-change path before the freeze starts.
  • Finance briefings. Before quarter end and payroll weeks, send finance a two-line warning with the callback rule. Most BEC pain is preventable with that one habit.

Refresh this picture twice a year. Actor names change, but the timing behavior persists: crews that learned to strike on quiet weekends keep doing it, because the economics still work. When a new name appears in advisories, ask one question first: did they use timing? That question sorts fast.

The honest PRE gap

While the recon happens on PRE platforms, most of your evidence appears only downstream, when the timing is used. Say this openly to leadership: the rules in this section detect echoes, and staffing closes the gap itself.

Use the ranking in two ways. For planning, it sorts the backlog: controls for ranks 1 and 2 come before anything for ranks 5 and 6. For reporting, it sets expectations: a leader who sees "Critical" on rank 1 understands why holiday staffing is a security budget line, not a scheduling detail. Re-score the table yearly, because your own published calendar changes every year too.

5.4Logs and Patterns

Six log sources carry the faint signals of tempo work or its first abuse. For each source you get the fields to keep, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, the detection logic, and a realistic sample. Web samples use the classic CLF (Common Log Format: the standard one-line web server log format). The rest use JSON (a plain text data format that uses braces, quotes, and commas).

a) CDN and WAF web logs

CDN (content delivery network: servers that cache and serve your web pages worldwide) and WAF (web application firewall: a service that filters web traffic to your apps) logs record every request to your public estate. That includes the careers and schedule pages.

Fields
timestamp, client IP (c-ip), method, URI path (cs-uri-stem), status code, bytes, referrer, user agent
Normal
Candidates open two or three career pages; search crawlers arrive with known agents and respect robots; traffic follows your audience's timezone curve.
Suspicious
One IP pulls dozens of /careers, /working-at, /shift, or /schedule paths within minutes; headless-browser agents; request times that match no audience timezone.

Detection logic: group requests by client IP over a short window. Alert when the count passes a threshold (40 in 10 minutes here) and at least three distinct schedule-related paths were touched. This is Rule R1 in Section 5.5.

b) Productivity-suite audit logs

Audit logs from Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace record sharing changes. A calendar set to external or public can leak a whole rota in one click. This is a recon gift to outsiders and a policy break you can detect today.

Fields
ts, user, action, target_calendar, visibility, grantee, source IP
Normal
Internal shares to company domains; shares with expiry dates; few external shares with a named business reason.
Suspicious
Any external or public visibility on calendars named rota, coverage, shift, on-call, or holiday; repeated re-shares after revocation.

Detection logic: alert on share and permission-update actions where visibility contains external or public. This is Rule R2; tune it with an allow-list of partner domains.

c) ITSM and help-desk tickets

ITSM (IT service management: the platform that runs help-desk queues and tickets) data is underused in detection. Night bursts of reset topics from one source are the classic echo of timed vishing, and this data usually already exists.

Fields
ts, ticket ID, source_ip, requester, subject, channel, assigned group
Normal
Tickets spread across requesters and sources; night volume matches your real night shift; reset topics match known staff and known problems.
Suspicious
Ten or more tickets from one source IP in two hours at night; reset or MFA topics mixed with urgency words; requester values that do not match your directory.

Detection logic: filter tickets to hours 00-05 local, group by source IP, and alert when the count passes ten in two hours. This is Rule R3. Keep the timezone caveat in mind: a global team's working day creates night stamps at the headquarters.

d) Identity and VPN logs

VPN (virtual private network: the remote-access door into company systems) and identity logs show when accounts move. Be precise about what this is: the downstream echo of tempo abuse under T1078 (valid accounts), not recon itself. Tempo recon decided the hour; these logs show the hour being used.

Fields
ts, user, auth type, source IP, geo, MFA result, outcome
Normal
Logins cluster in working windows per office; known devices; geographies that match each user's history.
Suspicious
First-time logins at 02:00-05:00 local for office-based staff; MFA push approved shortly after a night reset call; impossible travel between night logins.

Detection logic: baseline per-user and per-site local hours; alert on first success outside the baseline within days of a night ticket burst. Correlation beats single-event alerts here.

e) Brand and digital-risk feeds

Digital-risk services watch forums, paste sites, and new domain registrations for your brand. Tempo leaks surface there as closure chatter or as domains dressed up for payroll season. Security teams write hxxp instead of http in quotes, so readers cannot click live links by accident.

Fields
ts, feed, type, content excerpt, source URL, matched terms, registration data
Normal
Job-listing reposts, general brand chatter, typosquat domains that copy your name but not your calendar.
Suspicious
Posts that quote your exact closure dates, rota details, or site shift patterns; domains that mix your brand with payroll, shift, or schedule words.

Detection logic: keyword feeds for brand plus schedule terms; route matches to a weekly review, and straight to your holiday runbook whenever a closure date appears.

f) Email gateway logs

An email gateway (the service that receives, checks, and delivers your email) logs every message it lets through. Payment-change and urgent-finance fraud pass through it, and the subject line plus the sender domain carry the signal. This is the source for Rule R6 in Section 5.5.

Fields
ts, sender, sender_domain, recipient, subject, spf_result, attachment_names, verdict
Normal
Finance mail comes from known payroll and bank domains; urgent wording is rare; external senders pass SPF (a check that the sending server is allowed to send for the domain).
Suspicious
Subjects mixing payment or banking words with urgency words; look-alike sender domains; payment-detail changes requested close to payday or quarter end; new senders asking to change payment files.

Detection logic: flag external mail whose subject contains a payment or banking term AND an urgency term, and pay extra attention during payday and quarter-end weeks. This is Rule R6; tune it with an allow-list of verified supplier and bank domains.

Sigma is a YAML text format that describes detection rules in a vendor-neutral way, so one rule can be converted into many SIEM query languages. YAML itself is a simple, indented text format used for rules and settings. SPL (Splunk Search Processing Language) is the search language of the Splunk log platform, one of the most common SIEM backends. Each detection below gives a complete Sigma rule and a matching SPL query, plus mapping, false-positive, and tuning notes. The coverage map first says which rule covers which ranked threat from Section 5.3, and how honest that coverage is.

Every Sigma rule here carries the full set of 13 standard keys: title, id, status, description, references, author, date, logsource, detection, fields, falsepositives, level, and tags. The tags include attack.t1591 and attack.t1591.003, so the rules map cleanly to this technique in any ATT&CK-aware tools. All six rules were written for the sample events shown in Section 5.4.

6

Real-World Case Study

On 2 July 2021, a ransomware crew used the holiday calendar of an entire country without breaking a single door to get it. The Kaseya VSA incident is the clearest public example of tempo exploitation. This section retells it defensively: what happened, when, and what it teaches every team that plans holidays.

Context: Kaseya VSA and REvil

Kaseya made VSA, an RMM (remote monitoring and management: software that lets service providers watch and manage thousands of customer computers from one console). MSPs (managed service providers: companies that run IT for many client firms) used VSA to push updates everywhere at once. REvil was a ransomware-as-a-service group: it built the file-locking software and rented it to affiliates who carried out attacks.

Map to this technique

The attack landed on the Friday before the US July 4 holiday weekend, when many corporate and government IT teams were already working with fewer people than usual. This matches T1591.003 exactly: the date is public knowledge, and the attackers picked it on purpose. CISA's incident alerts and its #StopRansomware guidance both note the holiday-weekend pattern.

Timeline

  1. 2021-07-02, early afternoon US Eastern time

    Attackers exploit zero-days (a flaw with no vendor fix available at the time) in on-premises VSA servers. One was CVE-2021-30116 (a CVE is the public ID of a known software vulnerability), an authentication bypass.

  2. 2021-07-02, the same afternoon

    A fake agent.exe update is pushed through VSA to downstream clients, encrypting their files. About 60 MSPs and 800 to 1,500 victim organizations are hit within hours.

  3. 2021-07-02, evening

    Kaseya disables its cloud VSA service (SaaS: software as a service - software you rent and reach through a browser) and asks every on-premises owner to shut VSA down. Thousands of admins spend the holiday weekend switching things off.

  4. 2021-07-03 to 07-05

    Incident command runs through the July 4 weekend with very small teams at Kaseya, at the MSPs, and at victims. Response queues stretch, and standard approvals wait for Tuesday.

  5. 2021-07-05 and after

    REvil claims responsibility and demands USD 70 million for a universal decryptor (software that can undo their encryption).

  6. 2021-07-13

    REvil's websites and other infrastructure disappear from the internet. They return briefly before vanishing again.

  7. 2021-07-22

    Kaseya states it received a universal decryptor from a third party and starts distributing it to victims through the incident process. Kaseya says it did not pay the ransom.

  8. 2021-11-08

    Ukrainian police, working with international partners, arrest people linked to REvil and seize infrastructure used in the operation. In January 2022, Russian authorities announce further arrests of REvil members.

The attack chain, step by step

1

Tempo recon. The date is chosen for the US holiday weekend. Whether the crew did formal T1591.003 work or simply used common knowledge, the effect is identical: defenders would be scarce.

2

Exploit. Zero-day flaws in on-premises VSA servers allow authentication bypass and code execution on a small set of internet-reachable servers.

3

Deploy. VSA's own update channel distributes a fake agent.exe, turning a trusted management tool into the delivery method.

4

Encryption. Downstream client machines encrypt within hours. The single console gives the attack an enormous multiplier.

5

Panic over the holiday. Help desks are empty, escalation paths are slow, and the shutdown guidance itself adds weekend work. The timing multiplies the damage beyond the encryption.

6

Recovery. A decryptor arrives weeks later, and many victims still rebuild from backups. Trust in the update channel has to be re-earned before VSA servers come back online.

Tools used

Attacker tools
Kaseya VSA zero-day exploits, including CVE-2021-30116 (authentication bypass) and CVE-2021-30118 (arbitrary code execution), a fake agent.exe update pushed through VSA, and REvil ransomware.
Defender tools
Backups, the decryptor built with Emsisoft from the key Kaseya received, the Kaseya incident page and advisories, and CISA guidance AA21-201A.

Detection point

Endpoint defenses caught some of the ransomware pushes, and some MSPs stopped the update mid-flight, which shows detection could work. But the timing itself was the force multiplier: many victims had holiday on-call gaps, so even correct alerts waited hours for a first look. Fixing the staffing gap would have shrunk the damage even with the zero-days unfixed.

Containment, eradication, recovery

In plain words: containment stops the spread, eradication removes the attacker and everything they left behind, and recovery returns normal work.

Containment

Kaseya disabled its SaaS VSA servers and told on-premises owners to power them off. MSPs disconnected management networks, and organizations isolated endpoints that had run the fake update.

Eradication

Eradication means removing the attacker and everything they left behind. VSA servers were rebuilt from clean images, credentials around them were rotated, and teams verified nothing the attackers added had survived before anything came back online.

Recovery

Victims restored from backups where encryption was complete, applied the distributed decryptor where it helped, and returned systems in stages with extra monitoring.

Impact and consequences

Organizations
800 to 1,500 downstream businesses hit through about 60 MSPs.
Everyday reach
About 800 Coop grocery stores in Sweden closed for almost a week after one provider chain was hit, showing how far one link reaches.
Demand
USD 70 million for a universal decryptor.
Duration
Days of outage for most victims, weeks of recovery for many.

Team response and communication

Kaseya published regular statements and an incident page through the weekend. CISA issued alerts during the holiday and a full joint advisory (AA21-201A) in the following weeks. Law-enforcement guidance told victims to report rather than pay. The lesson is communication rhythm: people accept a crisis run with very small teams far more easily when updates arrive on a fixed schedule.

Lessons for defenders

  • Holiday IR readiness is not optional. Rota, authority, and contact trees must work on the weekend the calendar says everyone is away.
  • Fast patching for internet-facing legacy apps. VSA servers sat exposed with known-weak authentication; keep an inventory of what is reachable and patch fast.
  • Freeze periods need extra monitoring, not less. Attackers read freezes as quiet time, so staffing and alerting must rise during them.
  • Your MSP's tempo is attacker input. Know your providers' patch cadence, holidays, and escalation paths, and write them into contracts.
  • Rehearse the weekend command structure. A tabletop exercise run on a simulated Saturday exposes gaps no weekday drill will find.

If a similar incident hit you this Friday

First 2 hours

Identify the management tool under attack and isolate it. Page the provider's emergency line. Open a bridge even if the crew is small, so decisions have a home.

First 24 hours

Map downstream reach: which clients, which stores, which plants. Communicate on a fixed rhythm, even when the update is "no change". Start evidence collection early.

First week

Rebuild from known-good images, rotate credentials, and keep the extra monitoring running after the freeze ends. Hold a short review while memories are fresh.

Mitigation and Defense

You cannot make your calendar secret, and you should not want to. The defense is different: make sure that any hour an attacker picks still has a rested, rehearsed team behind it. ATT&CK lists one mitigation for this technique, M1056 Pre-compromise: everything in this section happens before an attacker can use the timing. The table below covers policy, identity, endpoint, network, cloud, logging, monitoring, backup, and response, then lists the order to do them in and a short version for small teams.

Do this first

  • Map your next three public holiday closures to on-call coverage, and fix any weekend that is thinner than a weekday.
  • Audit externally shared calendars and revoke everything without a named owner and expiry date.
  • Turn on alerting for after-hours password and MFA resets.
  • Add callback verification to the password-reset procedure, and tell every help-desk agent about it.
  • Ship ITSM, productivity audit, identity, and email gateway logs into the SIEM so Rules R2, R3, R5, and R6 have data.
  • Write a one-page weekend command structure: who decides, who approves changes, who talks to the MSP.

Home and small office

Small teams have the same tempo problem with none of the spare staff. Three habits carry most of the value:

  • Do not post closures in real time with addresses. Say "the office is closed this week" without exact hours, street addresses, or camera notes. Share the details only with people who need them.
  • Verify payment changes by callback. Any email that changes bank details gets a phone call to the number you already have, never the number in the email. Payday weeks deserve extra suspicion.
  • Keep a second contact channel for urgent help. Agree now on who to call when the main inbox or phone is down, and write the number somewhere that works offline.
8

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

Every mistake below is real and common; each one quietly hands an attacker a timing advantage. The cards follow one format: the symptom you would see, the root cause, the fix, and the prevention habit that keeps it fixed.

1. Holiday on-call gaps

Symptom
Alerts wait hours for a first look during closures; response times double in December.
Root cause
The rotation was designed around office hours and normal weeks.
Fix
Staff closures at weekday depth, with named backups and a clear escalation path.
Prevention
Publish the holiday rota a quarter ahead and review it in the monthly security meeting.

2. Freezes that mute monitoring

Symptom
New detections wait for the freeze, and coverage quietly drops exactly when attackers like to work.
Root cause
The freeze policy lumps monitoring changes in with risky system changes.
Fix
Split the policy: detection and logging changes stay allowed during freezes.
Prevention
Add a rule to the change policy that security visibility may never decrease during a freeze.

3. Closure posts with too much detail

Symptom
Public posts state exact dates, hours, site names, and notes about running with very few staff together.
Root cause
Marketing treats every announcement as pure customer service.
Fix
Post the minimum: "closed this week" beats "site B unmanned from Friday 17:00".
Prevention
Add a public-footprint check to the communications approval flow.

4. Calendars nobody audited

Symptom
An audit finds shift rota calendars shared with personal accounts or public links.
Root cause
Sharing was convenient once and nobody reviewed it afterwards.
Fix
Revoke, then re-share only with named owners and expiry dates.
Prevention
Run Rule R2 monthly and review the delta.

5. Odd-hours tickets treated as normal

Symptom
Night reset tickets are approved quickly because "that is how night shift works".
Root cause
The help desk optimizes for speed and lacks a verification habit.
Fix
Callback verification for every night reset, using directory numbers only.
Prevention
Measure night reset approvals weekly and coach exceptions.

6. Global timezone false positives never baselined

Symptom
The after-hours rule pages constantly, so analysts mute it within a week.
Root cause
Thresholds were written for the headquarters clock only.
Fix
Baseline hours per office and per team, then re-tune the rule.
Prevention
Review rule noise monthly and adjust per-region thresholds.

7. ITSM data missing from the SIEM

Symptom
Nobody can answer "how many resets happened at 02:00 last month".
Root cause
The help-desk platform was never treated as a security data source.
Fix
Export tickets to the SIEM with timestamps, source IP, subject, and channel.
Prevention
List ITSM in the log-source inventory with an owner and a freshness check.

8. Payday BEC warnings absent

Symptom
Fraud attempts peak in payroll weeks and finance learns the pattern only after a loss.
Root cause
Awareness content ignores the finance calendar.
Fix
Send a short warning with the callback rule before every payroll and quarter-end week.
Prevention
Put the warning dates in the security calendar for the whole year.

9. No weekend tabletop

Symptom
The first real weekend incident reveals missing contacts and unclear authority.
Root cause
Exercises are always scheduled on weekday mornings when everyone is present.
Fix
Run one exercise on a simulated Saturday with the real on-call people.
Prevention
Schedule two weekend tabletops per year and track their findings to closure.

10. Alert thresholds that assume 9-to-5

Symptom
Rules fire on harmless evening activity and sleep through the true quiet-hour abuse.
Root cause
Thresholds were copied from a template built for office-hour traffic.
Fix
Derive thresholds from your own hourly baselines per site.
Prevention
Re-baseline twice a year and after any big hiring or office change.

11. MSP patch windows unknown

Symptom
A provider pushes a major change during your busiest week without notice.
Root cause
Contracts never covered the provider's change calendar.
Fix
Ask for the calendar, review it quarterly, and add notification duties to the contract.
Prevention
Keep a joint tempo calendar with every critical provider.

12. Handover notes that skip the night

Symptom
The day team learns about night events late, or never.
Root cause
Handover happens in chat threads that scroll away.
Fix
A written handover log with a fixed template, read at every shift start.
Prevention
Spot-check handover quality weekly during the daily review.

Best-practice summary

Staffing
Equal response depth on every date the calendar makes special.
Verification
Callback habits for resets and payment changes, without exception.
Visibility
All tempo-relevant logs in the SIEM, with tuned rules watching them.
Rehearsal
Weekend exercises that test the same paths attackers will test.

Habits that keep you sharp

Daily

Review after-hours tickets and resets from the past 24 hours. Confirm every night reset had a callback. Glance at the careers-page request count for spikes.

Weekly

Check new external calendar shares (Rule R2 output). Skim the digital-risk feed for closure mentions. Confirm the on-call rota for the next two weekends is staffed.

Monthly

Re-tune one rule against fresh baselines. Read the public footprint as an outsider. Rotate one upcoming holiday into the tabletop scenario list.
