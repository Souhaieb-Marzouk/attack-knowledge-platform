Overview

This page is about one quiet step that starts many big attacks: learning who a company works with. T1591.002 covers how attackers collect facts about vendors, suppliers, and partners from public sources. In this section we explain the technique in plain words, show where it sits in MITRE ATT&CK, and list the sources attackers read.

What T1591.002 is, in plain language

MITRE ATT&CK describes T1591.002 - Business Relationships as gathering information about the victim's business relationships that can be used during targeting. The information includes second and third-party organizations and domains, such as managed service providers and contractors. Some of them hold connected, and sometimes elevated, network access. The information can also reveal supply chains and shipment paths for the victim's hardware and software. Attackers collect it from online data sets, such as social media and the victim's own website, or by asking people directly through T1598 Phishing for Information. MITRE lists the platform as PRE, which means the whole step happens before the attacker touches any system owned by the victim.

ATT&CK lists one mitigation for this technique: M1056 Pre-compromise. Pre-compromise means actions you take before an attack starts. The ATT&CK advice is to focus on minimizing the amount and sensitivity of data available to external parties. For business relationships this means thinking before you publish: partner names, supplier case studies, portal links, and vendor logos all feed the attacker's map. This idea shapes the mitigation advice in section 7.

You can picture it as drawing a map of entrances. The main entrances of a company are watched by a security team. But every vendor, supplier, and service provider holds a key to a side entrance. A cleaning company has keys to the building. A supplier has an account on the purchasing portal. An IT provider has a tool that reaches into the network. Attackers read public sources to draw this map without sending a single packet to the victim. Security teams call this kind of collection OSINT (open-source intelligence: facts anyone can collect from public sources).

The problem is that all of this is public by design. Companies publish partner lists to win customer trust. Sales teams write case studies about their biggest customers. Human resources posts job ads that name the software and the service providers in use. Each page is harmless alone. Together they form a complete picture of who can reach the company and how.

Why it matters

For many years, defenders concentrated on their own network edge: firewalls, email gateways, and laptops. Attackers adapted. Today attackers often enter through a vendor instead of the main company. If a small supplier holds a working account on your vendor portal, or runs software that connects into your network, the attacker does not need to defeat your defenses. The attacker defeats the supplier's defenses and enters over the trusted connection.

Supply-chain ransomware showed how heavy this risk can be. In 2021, attackers compromised the platform of one managed service provider and pushed ransomware to hundreds of downstream companies in a single action. Each of those companies was attacked through a business relationship, not through its own weakness. This is why T1591.002 sits so early in the attack chain: the map of relationships decides where the whole attack begins. A defender who knows the same map can protect the bridges first.

Tactic: Reconnaissance

T1591.002 belongs to the Reconnaissance tactic (TA0043). This is the first tactic in every attack story. Everything here is research. No malware, no logins, no alerts on your firewalls.

Parent technique

T1591.002 is a child of T1591 - Gather Victim Org Information. The parent collects facts about the organization itself; this child focuses on the organizations around it.

What it feeds later

The finished relationship map feeds two main paths: T1199 Trusted Relationship, entering through an existing partner connection, and T1195 Supply Chain Compromise, attacking the software or updates that flow from a vendor.

Typical public sources attackers read

Each card below is a source type that appears again and again in real intrusions. None of them are secret. All of them are written by the victim's own staff, which is why this technique needs no special tools.

Press releases and annual reports

"We are proud to announce our partnership with..." This news helps the marketing team. It also gives recon facts to attackers at the same time. Annual reports and investor pages name key suppliers, distribution deals, and outsourcing partners, sometimes with contract values.

Partner and brand pages

"Our technology partners", "Certified resellers", "Powered by". These pages list exactly who is allowed to integrate with the company, and often show logos, contact emails, and portal links.

Procurement portals and tenders

Supplier registration pages, public tender awards, and request-for-proposal (RFP) results reveal who sells what to whom. Some governments and large buyers publish awarded contracts with amounts and dates.

Case studies and customer stories

A success story often names both sides of the deal and describes the systems involved: "Company X connected our warehouse platform to their ERP". ERP means enterprise resource planning, the software that runs core business processes. The attacker learns not only the relationship but also the technical details of how the systems connect.

Court records and filings

Lawsuits, contract disputes, lien records, and bankruptcy filings are public. They describe contracts, service levels, and sometimes direct network access in surprising detail.

Job ads naming vendors

"Experience with our Palo Alto firewalls required" or "you will support our ABC MSP-managed environment". Job ads leak the technology stack and the service providers. Attackers collect these ads for years of history.

Note: if you can read it, so can attackers

Every partner page, case study, and job ad is also a recon map. The goal is not to hide your business from customers. The goal is to know which pages form your public partner map, and to protect the doors those pages point to.

The story in words

The list below is the required text version of the animation. Each entry matches one simulation step by title, and each entry ends with a defender view so the story always stays useful for the blue team.

  1. Step 1 - Read the annual report

    The attacker starts with public reporting. Annual reports, investor news, and press releases name suppliers, outsourcing deals, and new partnerships. Filings can add contract details. The attacker collects every name that claims a business connection to the target.

    Defender view: this step happens entirely outside your logs, so there is nothing to detect here yet. The source of the facts is your own communication team, which is why TPRM and communications should agree on what partnership details are published.

  2. Step 2 - Build the partner list

    Next the attacker crawls the company website. Partner, supplier, vendor, and case-study pages reveal the wider ecosystem, often with logos and contact details. The attacker turns these pages into a structured list of named third parties.

    Defender view: fast repeated reads of partner pages show up in CDN (content delivery network: the servers that cache your website worldwide) logs. Look for one address fetching /partners, /suppliers, and /case-studies pages in a short burst. Most crawls are search engines, but bursts that also fetch procurement pages deserve a look.

  3. Step 3 - Rank the weakest vendor

    With the list in hand, the attacker compares vendors. Which one has remote access, a client portal, or a data feed? Which one posts job ads that suggest a small, stretched IT team? The goal is a ranked list with the weakest bridge at the top.

    Defender view: ask yourself which vendor could reach your core network. Someone is asking the same question. Feed the answer into your TPRM program so the highest-access vendors get the strongest controls.

  4. Step 4 - Watch for vendor breach news

    The attacker now collects breach news and threat reports about the vendors on the list. A vendor that just disclosed an incident is a good target, because the attacker may be able to reuse stolen accounts or use an ongoing compromise.

    Defender view: vendor breach feeds and digital-risk tools shorten this step for attackers. Subscribe to the same feeds and add a rule that a vendor incident automatically opens a TPRM review and a credential reset for that vendor's accounts.

  5. Step 5 - Map the access path

    For the top candidate, the attacker maps how the vendor connects to the target. The connection can be a web portal, a VPN (virtual private network: a private, encrypted connection into a network), a managed tool, or a direct network link. The attacker learns this from public documentation, leaked screenshots, and the vendor's own website.

    Defender view: nothing reaches you in this step. The attacker has now mapped the bridge from outside your network. Inventory every connection each vendor holds, because that inventory is what the attacker just built for free.

  6. Step 6 - Stage partner-brand lures

    The attacker copies partner branding onto staging infrastructure. Emails and pages that look like the real partner, but are controlled by the attacker, are prepared for phishing. Trust in the partner brand makes the victim's staff less careful.

    Defender view: look-alike partner domains are strong early signals. Monitor certificate logs and domain registrations for names that imitate your partners, and alert when mail arrives from a partner domain that is not on your allow list.

  7. Step 7 - Probe the vendor portal

    Credential testing begins against the vendor portal and its VPN login page. The attacker tries password reuse, default credentials, and credential-stuffing lists against vendor accounts. This is the first step that touches your systems directly.

    Defender view: new devices, new countries, and failure bursts on vendor accounts belong in your SIEM (security information and event management: the central system that stores and connects security logs). Section 5 gives you ready-made Sigma rules and Splunk SPL for exactly these patterns.

  8. Step 8 - Hand-off to initial access

    The vendor bridge is ready for the access phase (T1199 Trusted Relationship). The recon file closes here, and the intrusion begins through the trusted channel. Everything after this point is no longer reconnaissance.

    Defender view: from here, detections on the target itself take over. Report the recon observations, correlate them with the intrusion alerts, and keep the case linked so the response team knows the entry path.

Good practice

Notice the shape of the story: steps 1 to 6 are invisible to most defenses, and only steps 7 and 8 touch your systems. That is why this page spends so much time on the signals you do have: portal logs, CDN logs, brand monitoring, and TPRM reports.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains how an attacker thinks about business relationships. The core idea is simple. If the target trusts a vendor, then anyone who controls that vendor is trusted too. The smallest company in the chain often holds the easiest way in. Read this as an attacker playbook so you can predict where your own bridges are.

Why attackers map relationships first

Direct attacks against a large company are expensive. The company buys firewalls, runs a SOC, hires penetration testers, and patches its main systems. But every large company is a customer of many small companies, and most small companies cannot afford any of that. The attacker does not need to defeat the big defenses. The attacker needs the small vendor that holds a bridge into the big company.

Trust passes along the chain. If the target trusts vendor A, and vendor A trusts its IT provider, then whoever controls the IT provider can reach the target. Attack chains often cross two or three companies before they arrive. The recon work in T1591.002 is the step that finds these chains. It answers four questions:

  • Who sells to, buys from, or services the target?
  • Which of those third parties holds technical access: portals, VPNs, software links?
  • Which of them has the weakest visible security?
  • Which of them is worth the effort, meaning one bridge reaches many victims?

The last point is what makes this powerful. An MSP that manages 200 small businesses gives the attacker 200 possible targets through one access path. A software vendor whose update server reaches 1,000 customers is worth even more. This is why supply-chain attacks keep growing: the relationship map tells the attacker where one hour of work can reach a thousand companies.

How the mapping is done, conceptually

1

Collect names. The attacker gathers every third party that public pages connect to the target: press releases, partner directories, case studies, procurement portals, court filings, and job ads. Each source adds names the others miss.

2

Expand each vendor. For every vendor the attacker repeats the same research one level down: the vendor's website, its own partner page, its job ads, its breach history. A vendor with a public breach is moved up the list.

3

Rank by access and weakness. Names become a table. One column records access level: portal account, VPN, software agent, or direct link. The other records how strong security looks. It lists old websites, missing MFA on the login page, exposed admin panels, or expired certificates. MFA means multi-factor authentication: a second proof of identity, such as an app code.

4

Choose the bridge. The attacker picks the vendor with the best mix of access and weakness, prepares partner-brand lures or portal attacks, and hands the file to the access phase (T1199).

Prerequisites

The scariest part of this technique is how little it needs:

Skills

Web search and patience. No exploit development, no malware, no special tools. A spreadsheet is enough to hold the map.

Tools

A browser, a note-taking tool, and optionally free OSINT utilities. Paid tools such as company-data platforms or domain-monitoring feeds speed the work up but are never required.

Access

None. The platform is PRE because every source is public. Nothing in this phase touches the victim's systems, which is exactly why it is so hard to detect.

Lab only: ethical exercises

The exercises below are defensive drills for your own organization. Do them only against pages and systems you own or are authorized to review. They show you how much an outsider can learn about your partner ecosystem without touching your network.

Warning: lab environment only

Run these exercises against your own organization, or against a company that gave you written permission. Scanning, crawling, or probing systems you do not own may break laws and contracts even when the intent is harmless.

Exercise 1: build your own partner list

Starting only from your public pages, press releases, and job ads, build the partner list an attacker would build. Then compare it with your internal vendor inventory. The gap between the two lists is your shadow-vendor problem, made visible.

Exercise 2: read your own job ads

Collect the last twelve months of your job ads, including archived copies on job boards. List every product, platform, and service provider they name. Each name tells an attacker which portal, tool, or MSP to target next.

Exercise 3: check partner-brand domains

Take your top ten partners' brand names and search domain registration and certificate logs for look-alike spellings. If you find them registered by someone else, assume lures exist and check your mail gateway for delivery attempts.

Common variations

MSP-focused

The attacker maps which managed service provider runs the target's IT, then targets the MSP's RMM tools, because one compromise reaches many customers. RMM (remote monitoring and management) tools are the software MSPs use to administer client machines, so they are a main target.

ISV and software-update focused

The attacker maps the software the target runs, then attacks the vendor that ships it, especially update infrastructure. ISV means independent software vendor. A trojanized update turns one vendor compromise into thousands of infections downstream.

Payment-fraud focused

The attacker maps the payment chain: who sends invoices to whom, and which partner domains send payment reminders. This feeds business email compromise (BEC: fraud where attackers send email while pretending to be a trusted person or company) rather than malware.

Common attacker mistakes

Recon is research, and research goes stale or incomplete. Defenders can exploit that.

Mistake: missing shadow vendors

The attacker's list comes from public pages, so it misses informal suppliers. Examples are the contractor someone paid by credit card, or the consultant with a personal laptop on the network. These undocumented vendors are often the real weak bridge. The attacker who skips old filings, local news, and procurement records never finds them.

Mistake: trusting stale relationship data

Partnerships end. Companies rename, merge, and get acquired. An attacker working from a three-year-old case study may spend weeks on a bridge that no longer exists. Defenders should keep their own relationship data fresh, because the team with the fresher map is safer.

Danger: this is illegal outside a lab

Using these methods against real systems you do not own is illegal in most countries. Unauthorized access attempts, credential testing against real portals, and phishing real employees can lead to criminal charges, even when the goal is research. Stay inside your own lab, your own organization, or a written, signed engagement.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Detecting T1591.002 is one of the hardest jobs in the SOC, because the attacker is reading pages you published on purpose. Most of the technique happens outside your logs. This section shows the faint artifacts that do exist, where to look for them, and a triage flow you can hand to an analyst on day one.

Why detection is hard

First, the activity is mostly legal. Reading a partner page is not an attack, and search engines crawl those pages every day. Second, the activity is off your perimeter: the annual report is read on the attacker's laptop, not on your network. Third, the volume is tiny. A patient attacker may load fifteen pages over three weeks. That is too little to stand out inside normal web traffic. A SOC that only watches for intrusions will never see this phase.

The realistic goal is therefore not "catch the researcher". The goal is to catch the transitions: the moment recon turns into portal probing, the moment a look-alike partner domain appears, the moment vendor accounts start failing. Those transitions are visible, and they are what our detections in section 5 target.

The faint artifacts

Scraping of partner and supplier pages

CDN and web logs can show one client address reading /partners, /suppliers, /vendors, and /case-studies in sequence. The requests often carry an empty or unusual user agent (the software name a browser sends to a website) and little interest in normal browsing. One burst proves nothing, but repeated bursts from the same range are worth a note.

Look-alike partner-brand domains

Staging lures need domains. Brand-monitoring feeds, CT log (Certificate Transparency: a public list of TLS certificates; TLS is the encryption that protects HTTPS websites) monitoring, and passive DNS (DNS: the domain name system, the internet's name lookup service) can reveal domains that copy your partners' names. They often appear days or weeks before any mail arrives.

Vendor-portal anomalies

The first step that touches your network is probing the vendor portal and its VPN login page. The signs are failed logins in bursts, new devices and countries on vendor accounts, and password spraying across many vendor usernames. These logs are the richest signal you own.

TPRM reports and staff reports

Front-desk calls from a "partner" asking odd questions, vendors reporting fake invoices, and staff forwarding suspicious partner mail all land as tickets. If TPRM tickets and SOC cases are linked, this human sensor network becomes searchable.

Triage flow: from faint signal to action

This six-step flow covers the most common entry point for detection, a look-alike partner-domain alert. The same structure works for portal anomalies: verify, contain, hunt, escalate to TPRM.

1

Trigger: look-alike alert. A brand-monitoring or CT log feed reports a domain that imitates one of your partners, for example the partner name with an extra letter or a hyphen. Open a case and record the domain, registration date, and any certificate details.

2

Verify it is hostile. Check who registered the domain, where it is hosted, and whether it serves a copy of the partner's login page. Harmless reseller sites exist, so look for credential forms, recent registration, and privacy-proxied ownership combined with hosting on bullet-proof providers.

3

Contain what you control. Block the domain and its known IP addresses (IP: the internet address of a device) at the mail gateway, proxy, and DNS resolver. If the domain imitates a portal your staff logs into, force a short awareness note to the teams that use that portal.

4

Notify the partner. Contact the real partner through a known-good channel. They can takedown the domain with their registrar and check their own side for compromise. Keep the notification in the case file.

5

Hunt through your logs. Search mail, proxy, DNS, and portal logs for the look-alike domain and for the partner's real domain used from new infrastructure. You are looking for anyone who already received or clicked a lure, and for portal logins that match the timing.

6

Open a TPRM case and close the loop. Link the case to the vendor record, review what the partner can reach in your environment, and feed findings back into the detection backlog. If portal activity was found, treat it as a possible intrusion and involve incident response.

Good practice: make TPRM and SOC one team

The strongest blue-team answer to T1591.002 is a shared view. It links the vendor inventory from TPRM, the portal logs from IT, and the case history from the SOC, all by vendor name. Attackers exploit the gap between departments. Close the gap and the faint artifacts start to connect.

5.1Regulation Mapping

Reconnaissance techniques like T1591.002 are rarely named directly in laws and standards. What the standards do name is the program that must exist around vendors: know them, assess them, contract with them, monitor them. When auditors ask for evidence of third-party security, the detections and logs in this section are exactly that evidence. Note: one inventory, two audiences

Notice the pattern: every framework asks for a vendor inventory, and this whole page is about an attacker building the same inventory from public pages. If your TPRM inventory is incomplete, the attacker's public-page version may be the only one that exists. Building the internal inventory is both a compliance task and a direct detection and prevention control.

Turning the table into practice works in three steps. First, map each framework to one owner. ISO A.5.19 to A.5.23 goes to the TPRM lead, GV.SC to the risk committee, and 12.8 to the payment compliance officer. Second, for each control, agree on the artifact that proves it: an inventory export, a signed contract, a monitoring dashboard. Third, wire the artifacts to the log sources in this section. The same portal logs that feed rule R1 can also feed the A.5.22 "monitoring of supplier services" evidence pack. One pipeline then serves both compliance and detection, which keeps audits cheap and detections honest.

When an auditor or assessor interviews you, tell the story as a loop. The inventory defines which vendors are watched. The detections watch them. Alert outcomes update the vendor risk tier, and the updated tier changes how intensely you watch. Frameworks do not name this loop, but every one of the seven mappings above expects its parts to exist. If you can walk an assessor through one real vendor from contract to alert history, you have satisfied the spirit of all of them at once.

Collect, analyze, apply

Threat intelligence work has three stages. Collect: pull vendor names, breach news, and actor reports from the sources above into one place, such as a spreadsheet or your SIEM. Analyze: rate each item for confidence and for relevance to your actual vendor list. Apply: turn the confirmed items into briefings, TPRM actions, and detections, as the workflow below shows.

Applying this intel in practice

Intel becomes value through three concrete habits. First, mapping: once a quarter, walk your top twenty vendors against the actor table and ask which actor profile would pick each vendor as a bridge. Second, triggering: wire vendor breach news into your process. An advisory that names one of your vendors should automatically create a TPRM case, force a credential rotation for that vendor's accounts, and raise portal sensitivity for two weeks. Third, briefing: give the SOC a one-page brief per relevant actor, with the vendor-entry pattern and the detection names from section 5.5 that watch for it. This keeps intelligence connected to the rules that actually fire.

From intel to detection in five steps

The following small workflow turns any new intel item, such as an advisory about an abused MSP tool, into a tested detection. It takes an afternoon per item and prevents the common failure where intel is read, agreed with, and then forgotten.

1

Extract the pattern. Write down, in one sentence, what the attacker did with the relationship: which vendor type, which access path, which account type. If you cannot say it in one sentence, you do not understand the case yet.

2

Check your exposure. Search the vendor inventory for that vendor type or product. If nobody uses it, file the note and stop; if somebody does, continue with their accounts and logs in scope.

3

Write the hypothesis. Turn the pattern into a testable statement against your logs: "a vendor account for product X logging in from a new device within seven days of the advisory date".

4

Test and tune. Run the SPL over ninety days. Expect either silence or a handful of explainable events; tune the threshold and the allow lists until the result is quiet but sensitive.

5

Ship and link. Save the rule with Sigma tags attack.t1591 and attack.t1591.002. Link the intel source in the rule description. Then add the rule name to the vendor incident playbook, so responders know it exists.

Good practice: keep an intel debt list

Intel items that deserve a rule but have no log source yet go on a visible "intel debt" list. When the portal team finally ships the missing log source, the debt list tells you which detection to build first. This keeps missed coverage honest instead of forgotten.

5.4Logs and Patterns

Detections need logs. The attacks live in six places: the vendor portal, the B2B (business-to-business) document gateway, the public website edge, brand monitoring, the help desk, and the mail gateway. For each source below we list the fields to collect, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and the detection logic. Each source also comes with a realistic sample event you can use to test your pipeline.

Source 1: Vendor and partner portal logs

The portal where suppliers log in to view purchase orders, upload invoices, or manage their accounts is the single most valuable source. Collect one structured event per authentication action, and enrich it with first-seen logic.

Fields: ts, vendor_account, source_ip, device_id, action, result, plus computed flags new_device and new_country.

Normal

  • Known vendor IPs and device fingerprints, stable over months
  • Logins inside the vendor's usual business hours
  • Success rate high; failures rare and followed by nothing unusual
  • One or two countries per vendor account, never changing

Suspicious

  • A new device or a new country for a vendor account
  • Failed logins followed by a success within minutes, from the same address
  • One address testing many vendor accounts (password spraying)
  • Vendor account logging in at hours that break its own history

Detection logic: alert on success events where new_device or new_country is true (rule R1), and on failure bursts per vendor account (rule R4). Baseline every vendor account for ninety days before turning the rules on, so the flags reflect real habits.

A vendor account that normally logs in from one office IP in one country succeeds from a fresh device abroad at 02:14. On its own it may be travel. Combined with the look-alike alert in source 4, it is an incident.

Source 2: B2B and EDI gateway logs

B2B gateways move structured business documents between companies. EDI (electronic data interchange: the standard format for machine-to-machine business documents such as invoices and purchase orders) traffic is a favorite target. Attackers who have mapped your relationships can imitate normal document flows.

Fields: ts, partner_id, document_type, count, plus recommended direction and channel.

Normal

  • Document volumes follow a weekly business rhythm
  • Document types per partner are stable
  • Partners send from known trading endpoints

Suspicious

  • A sudden spike in one document type for one partner
  • A dormant partner wakes up with high volume
  • New endpoint or new certificate for an old partner ID

Detection logic: compare per-partner daily volume against a rolling baseline; alert when a day exceeds several times the average or when a document type appears for a partner that never used it. Volume changes are also a fraud indicator: invoice floods often accompany payment-diversion attacks.

Partner PID-4471 normally sends about 120 invoices a day. At 08:02 the gateway has already counted 940 inbound invoices. Either their system is broken, or someone is generating documents to hide one forged invoice inside a flood.

Source 3: CDN and WAF web logs

Your public website edge logs every request. CDN means content delivery network: the servers that cache your website worldwide. WAF means web application firewall: a filter in front of web applications. Together they record the bulk reads of partner and supplier pages described in step 2 of the simulation.

Format: Common Log Format (CLF: the classic one-line-per-request web log format) or structured JSON from the edge. Keep c-ip, cs-method, cs-uri-stem, cs(User-Agent), sc-status, and time.

Normal

  • Search engine crawlers with verified reverse DNS
  • Humans browsing partner pages in small numbers with full asset loads
  • Monitoring bots hitting a health endpoint, nothing else

Suspicious

  • One address reading /partners, /suppliers, /vendors, and /case-studies in sequence
  • No interest in images or scripts, only HTML pages
  • Custom or empty user agents, requests at machine speed

Detection logic: rule R3 counts requests to relationship-themed paths per client address over ten minutes. The pattern alone is weak evidence, so treat it as a note that enriches the next alert from the same range.

One address walks every relationship page of the site in under forty seconds, with no assets fetched. A person would load scripts and images and pause. This is a crawler mapping the partner ecosystem.

Source 4: Brand and digital-risk feed

Digital-risk services watch domain registrations, certificate logs, and cloned pages for your brand names. Your partner brands deserve the same watch, because attackers borrow partner trust, not just your own.

Fields: ts, alert_type, domain, target_brand, first_seen, registrar, plus enrichment such as mx_record and page_similarity.

Normal

  • Real resellers and regional distributors
  • News articles and forums mentioning the brand
  • Old registrations that never receive mail

Suspicious

  • Fresh registration imitating a partner name
  • Mail records switched on within days
  • A login page copying the partner portal

Detection logic: rule R2 fires on typosquat and look-alike alerts for partner brands. Keep the partner brand list in your TPRM system and sync it to the monitor, so new partners are covered automatically.

A brand-new domain adds "secure-portal" to a partner's name, has mail enabled, and shows a 91 percent visual similarity to the partner's real portal. This is the staging step of a partner-brand lure.

Source 5: TPRM and help-desk ticket log

People notice things that machines do not. Accounts payable notices a strange invoice, the front desk takes an odd call from a "partner", a vendor reports that someone pretended to be them. When these tickets are logged in a structured way and shared with the SOC, they become searchable records.

Fields: ts, ticket_id, report_type, subject, reported_by, channel, plus a forwarded_to_soc flag.

Normal

  • Invoice questions about real purchase orders
  • Password resets and access requests
  • Delivery and scheduling issues

Suspicious

  • Callers claiming to be a partner but failing basic checks
  • Requests for banking or invoice details
  • Urgency and pressure around payment changes

Detection logic: rule R5 surfaces tickets tagged as suspicious contact that mention invoices, purchase orders, payment, or partners. The value is correlation: a ticket plus a look-alike domain plus a portal anomaly tells one story.

Accounts payable reports a caller claiming to be VendorA, asking for invoice and banking details. Two days after the look-alike domain alert, this is no longer a lone odd call.

Quirks to expect when you first onboard these logs

Every log source above has a surprise waiting in week one. Knowing them in advance saves days of confusion.

Portal logs: shared vendor accounts

Many suppliers share one login across their staff, so device_id changes constantly and new_device floods. Fix it in TPRM: contracts should require per-person accounts for anyone with elevated access, and shared accounts stay on the strict allow list.

Portal logs: clock skew

The portal, the VPN, and the SIEM often disagree about time by minutes. Normalize every timestamp to UTC on ingest, or your R4 fifteen-minute window will both miss bursts and invent them.

Edge logs: crawler look-alikes

AI and analytics crawlers rotate user agents faster than any block list. Verify crawlers by reverse DNS or known ranges, and expect R3's noise to come almost entirely from this group.

Ticket exports: free-text chaos

Help-desk agents tag inconsistently, so report_type is missing on half the tickets. Start with a monthly tag-hygiene report before trusting R5, and make the subject field matching your safety net.

Source 6: Mail gateway logs

The mail gateway is the filter that receives every message from the internet before delivery. When attackers impersonate a partner, their mail passes through this system. It therefore records the exact moment a relationship map turns into a fraud attempt.

Fields: ts, event, sender, recipient, subject, spf, dkim, plus the enrichment fields lookalike_of and verdict. SPF and DKIM are two checks that prove the sender is allowed to send mail for that domain.

Normal

  • Mail from partner domains that pass SPF and DKIM
  • Known marketing platforms that send on behalf of partners
  • Stable volume and stable sender domains per partner

Suspicious

  • A sender domain that imitates a partner brand but is not the real partner domain
  • Subjects about invoices, payments, or bank details from that look-alike domain
  • First-seen sender domains aimed at finance staff

Detection logic: rule R6 fires when the gateway or the SIEM stamps a message with lookalike_of (the real partner domain it imitates) and the subject mentions invoices, payments, or bank details. Keep both conditions together; each one alone creates many false alerts. Also keep the quarantined copies, because blocked attempts still confirm the recon.

Two days after the fake domain first sent mail, it aims a payment-change message at the finance director. This is the moment to block the sender and warn finance. Rule R6 turns exactly this message into an alert.

Deployment order, testing, and maintenance

Rules fail in production for boring reasons: a field renamed, a baseline never built, a threshold copied from another company. The order below avoids most of that pain.

1

Ship in read-only mode first. Deploy R4, then R1, then R5, then R2, then R6, then R3, all as non-alerting searches that only write to an index. Two weeks of read-only data tells you the real volumes before anything pages anyone.

2

Build the baselines. From the read-only period, produce the per-vendor device, country, and failure tables. These become the lookup tables that the new_device, new_country, and threshold logic reads.

3

Test with replay. Replay the sample events from section 5.4, including the failure burst and the look-alike mail, against the rules. Every sample must fire the rule it was designed for, and nothing else should.

4

Enable with owners. Turn alerts on one rule at a time, each with a named owner, a documented triage step, and a target response time. A rule without an owner becomes the next mistake 5.

5

Re-baseline quarterly. Vendor habits drift: new laptops, new countries, new staff. A quarterly re-baseline keeps thresholds honest and doubles as the tuning review that mistake 11 asks for.

For validation at the YAML level, run each rule through the Sigma CLI (command-line tool: a program you drive with typed commands) converter for your target platform before promoting it. This way syntax errors surface in version control instead of in the SOC queue. Keep the six rules in a repository with review, and record every threshold change next to the alert data that motivated it.

Finally, measure the rules like any other control: monthly counts, false-positive rate per rule, and the ratio of alerts that linked to a TPRM case. When R2, R5, and R6 start correlating with each other, or R1 precedes an R4 burst, your detections are doing exactly what the technique chapter of this page predicted. That correlation, not any single alert, is the true detection of T1591.002 in action.

They entered through a small refrigeration and HVAC contractor that had a business relationship with Target. The facts below are defensive and educational, drawn from widely published reporting.

Note: sources and framing

This case study summarizes facts widely reported by KrebsOnSecurity, Bloomberg Businessweek, and a 2014 US Senate Commerce Committee report on the breach. The report's title uses the term kill chain, which means the ordered steps of an attack, from research to data theft. Details below reflect that public record. The purpose is education for defenders, not blame of any individual or company.

The target and the vendor

Target is a major US retailer with more than 1,700 stores and a large payments environment. Fazio Mechanical Services is a small refrigeration and HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) contractor from Minnesota. Fazio had performed refrigeration and HVAC work for Target stores, which made the relationship real and useful: vendors need account access for billing, scheduling, and project documents. Attackers chose this bridge, not Target's main door.

Map to the technique

Everything about this case fits T1591.002. The relationship was discoverable: a company that does refrigeration work for a retailer leaves traces in procurement records, business registries, and trade reporting. The vendor was small, so its defenses were lighter. The vendor held a bridge: credentials that worked on Target's supplier portal. The attacker only needed to map the relationship, phish the vendor, and use the trust channel.

Timeline of the case

  1. Weeks before November 2013

    The attackers phished Fazio Mechanical staff. The phishing emails carried the Citadel credential-stealing malware. It collected credentials from an employee who used the same password at work and on Target's external supplier portal. The weak point was not Target's network; it was password reuse inside a trusted vendor.

  2. 2013-11-15 (date widely reported in the investigation record)

    The stolen Fazio credentials were used to log into Target's supplier portal, a vendor-facing system for billing and project management. Vendor portal access alone does not reach payment systems, but it gave the attackers a foothold inside Target's vendor zone and a starting map of internal names and connections.

  3. Late November 2013

    The attackers moved from the supplier zone into the wider Target network, reaching systems the vendor network should never have touched. This lateral move is the failure of segmentation that turned a portal incident into a network incident.

  4. 2013-11-30 (reported)

    Point-of-sale (POS: the checkout terminals and software in stores) malware from the BlackPOS/Kaptoxa family was installed on store systems around this date. It was built to capture card data from memory during the swipe, at the moment the number is briefly unencrypted.

  5. Early to mid December 2013 (reported 2013-12-02 onward)

    During the busiest shopping weeks of the year, the malware collected card data from stores and staged it for exfiltration. The stolen batch was moved out in batches through existing data flows to systems the attackers controlled.

  6. 2013-12-19

    Target publicly disclosed a breach affecting approximately 40 million payment card accounts. The announcement came in the middle of the holiday season and became national news within hours.

  7. January 2014

    Target disclosed that contact details of roughly 70 million people, separate from the card numbers, had also been taken. The combined scope made this one of the largest retail breaches of its time.

  8. 2014

    Leadership changed: CEO Gregg Steinhafel resigned in May 2014, and CIO Beth Jacob had departed in March 2014. The breach also drove industry-wide changes in payment card security, including faster adoption of chip cards in the United States.

The attack chain as numbered steps

1

Vendor reconnaissance (T1591.002). The attackers identified Fazio Mechanical as a Target vendor through public and business sources, then studied the vendor as the weakest bridge.

2

Vendor phishing. Emails with malware were sent to Fazio staff. At least one employee opened the bait, and the malware captured credentials from the infected machine.

3

Portal credentials. Because the employee reused the same password for Target's supplier portal, the stolen credentials worked directly against Target's vendor-facing system.

4

Move through the vendor zone. From the supplier network the attackers reached the wider Target network. Segmentation between vendor zones and core systems did not hold them back.

5

POS malware. The BlackPOS/Kaptoxa malware was deployed to store point-of-sale systems and configured to dump card track data from memory.

6

Exfiltration. Collected card data was staged inside the network and moved out in batches during December 2013, then sold on criminal markets.

Tools used (as reported)

Public reporting names these tools in the Target chain. The phishing emails carried the Citadel credential-stealing malware. The attackers used the stolen Fazio credentials on Target's supplier portal. They installed BlackPOS/Kaptoxa RAM-scraping malware on the point-of-sale systems. Finally, they used Target's own data flows to move the card data out.

Warning: the detection point that was missed twice

Public reporting, including the Senate committee analysis, states that malware alerts fired during the intrusion. Nobody acted on them in time. In addition, the vendor path itself was never segmented, so the vendor zone offered a direct path to the payment environment. A detection that fires and is not triaged, plus a network design that trusts a supplier too much, turned one small phishing email into a historic breach.

Response after discovery

Containment

Containment means stopping the attack from spreading further. Target engaged forensic investigators, confirmed the malware, and worked to stop the collection and movement of card data. Payment industry partners were notified so stolen cards could be flagged quickly.

Eradication

Eradication means removing the attacker and all their tools. The malware was removed from store systems, affected credentials and access paths were closed, and the entry path through the vendor account was cut off. The vendor-facing environment was then tightened with new security controls.

Recovery

Target rebuilt trust through public communication, offered credit monitoring, accelerated chip-card rollout, and rebuilt its security leadership and program. Recovery took years and far outlasted the technical cleanup.

Impact and consequences

  • Roughly 40 million payment card numbers taken, plus about 70 million contact records
  • Cumulative costs reported at over 200 million US dollars across investigation, litigation, and remediation
  • CEO and CIO departures in 2014, plus lasting brand damage
  • Industry-wide payment security changes, including faster chip-card adoption

Team response and communication

The disclosure on 2013-12-19 came during peak shopping season and shaped how the public saw the crisis. Later reporting showed the gap between the technical detection in late November and the public disclosure in December. That gap became a lesson in its own right. Communication timing must be planned with legal, brand, and security together, and the vendor entry path must be described clearly so the industry learns.

Lessons that connect back to this technique

  • Segment vendor networks. A supplier portal account should never lead, even indirectly, to payment systems.
  • Put MFA (multi-factor authentication: a second proof of identity, such as an app code) on every vendor portal, because stolen passwords are the cheapest attack in this story.
  • Act on every malware alert. The alerts that fired were accurate; the process failed them.
  • TPRM must check vendor security, including how vendors handle credentials and endpoint protection, before and during the relationship.
  • Map your own business relationships before attackers do, and know which vendors can reach which zones.

Mitigation and Defense

You cannot stop an attacker from reading public pages. Mitigation therefore focuses on strengthening the bridges the pages point to. The table below gives the control set by area, with effort and ownership. After the table you get a short list to start with, and special advice for small businesses that are themselves vendors to bigger companies.

Do this first

If you only have one quarter and a small budget, do these in order. Each one removes a whole attack path rather than shaving a percentage off the risk.

  • Turn on MFA for every vendor portal account, starting with vendors that have remote or privileged access.
  • Send vendor portal and VPN logs to your SIEM and enable rules R1 and R4 from section 5.5.
  • Review network segmentation: list which vendor zones can reach payment and core systems, then close every path that has no business reason.
  • Build the shadow-vendor list: compare the TPRM inventory with spending records and the public partner pages, and register what you find.
  • Extend brand monitoring to your top ten partner brands, and wire the alerts to the triage flow in section 4.
  • Write the vendor offboarding checklist, so every departed vendor loses accounts, certificates, and network paths the same week.

Home and small-office version

Small businesses are vendors to someone, and the Target case showed what that means. If you service bigger companies, you hold bridges into them. Do the basics that make you a hard target instead of the weak bridge. Turn on MFA everywhere your tools allow it. Keep work accounts separate from personal ones. Patch the RMM and remote-access tools you use for client work. Then ask your clients which portal accounts you hold, so you can protect them properly.

If you are the big company

Remember that your vendor's security is your security boundary. Put the security expectations from section 5.1 into contracts, and check the evidence instead of collecting questionnaires. Offer your critical vendors a minimum standard and help them reach it. That standard is MFA on the portal you gave them, segmented access, and a named contact for incident calls at night.

Good practice: prevention buys detection time

Every control above also makes the detections sharper. MFA on portals makes R1 events rarer and more meaningful. Segmentation keeps a portal incident small. A clean vendor inventory makes per-vendor thresholds in R4 honest. Defense in depth and good detections reinforce each other.

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

Most vendor-path failures are not exotic. They are ordinary gaps: a list nobody finished, a portal without a second factor, an alert everyone ignored. Each card below names one mistake, how it looks in real life, and how to fix and prevent it.

Mistake 1: The vendor inventory misses shadow vendors

Symptom
An intrusion trace points to a supplier who is not in the TPRM system at all.
Root cause
The inventory only tracks vendors with contracts; credit-card purchases, free tools, and one-off contractors never get registered.
Fix
Reconcile the TPRM list with spending records, network logs, and the public partner pages, then register every third party found.
Prevention
Quarterly reconciliation and a simple rule: any vendor with a portal account, VPN profile, or data feed must exist in the inventory.

Mistake 2: Vendor portals without MFA

Symptom
Portal accounts log in with a password only, and logins appear from new devices without friction.
Root cause
The portal was built for convenience for suppliers, and MFA was postponed as "too hard for vendors".
Fix
Roll out MFA in waves, starting with privileged and remote-access vendor accounts; provide hardware tokens for staff without smartphones.
Prevention
Make MFA a condition of portal access in contracts, and block password-only logins after a grace period.

Mistake 3: A flat network between supplier zones and the core

Symptom
Traffic from the vendor zone reaches payment or management systems without passing a single control point.
Root cause
Vendor links were added one project at a time, and every link quietly kept "temporary" wide access.
Fix
Map vendor flows, deny by default, and route what remains through jump hosts and allow lists.
Prevention
Firewall change reviews must ask "which vendor zone is this for" and expire rules automatically.

Mistake 4: No vendor offboarding

Symptom
Departed vendors still hold working portal accounts, certificates, and VPN profiles years later.
Root cause
Contract end triggers billing changes but not security changes; nobody owns the security side of contract closure.
Fix
Run an offboarding sweep now: disable, rotate, and remove; then make the checklist part of contract closure.
Prevention
Finance triggers the security checklist when a vendor contract ends or a project closes.

Mistake 5: Vendor-account alerts auto-suppressed as background noise

Symptom
R1-style alerts close automatically, and the reason field says "vendor noise".
Root cause
An early flood of false positives led to a broad suppression instead of real tuning.
Fix
Delete the blanket suppression; replace it with per-vendor baselines and travel-based exceptions that expire.
Prevention
Suppression rules need an owner and an expiry date, and monthly review of what they silenced.

Mistake 6: Real vendor travel treated as an attack

Symptom
Every new-country alert for a vendor turns out to be a real trip, so analysts stop trusting the alert.
Root cause
Nobody baselined vendor habits, and travel notices only cover employees, not suppliers.
Fix
Ask top vendors for travel windows or expected regions, store them in the vendor record, and feed them into the detection logic.
Prevention
Onboard every new vendor with a ninety-day baseline and a simple "where do you log in from" question.

Mistake 7: Vendor portal logs missing entirely

Symptom
After an incident, nobody can answer "when did the vendor account last log in and from where".
Root cause
The portal is treated as a business tool, not a security log source, so its logs were never onboarded.
Fix
Onboard portal authentication logs to the SIEM with the fields from section 5.4, then backfill what the portal still holds.
Prevention
Add "sends logs to the SIEM" to the acceptance criteria for any new portal or supplier-facing system.

Mistake 8: TPRM and the SOC never talk

Symptom
The SOC investigates a vendor account with no idea who owns it, and TPRM learns about portal abuse from the news.
Root cause
Two teams, two systems, and no shared list that links them by vendor name.
Fix
Give SOC read access to the vendor inventory, add vendor context to portal alerts, and link TPRM tickets to SOC cases.
Prevention
A standing monthly meeting with one agenda: what each team saw about vendors last month.

Mistake 9: Partner-brand look-alikes ignored

Symptom
Look-alike alerts for partner brands sit unread, because "that domain is not ours".
Root cause
Brand protection is scoped to the company's own name; partner brands fall between teams.
Fix
Extend monitoring to top partner brands and adopt the triage flow from section 4 for those alerts.
Prevention
TPRM maintains the partner brand list and syncs it to the monitor on every vendor change.

Mistake 10: Contract clauses without evidence checks

Symptom
Every vendor signs the security questionnaire, yet audits find no MFA and no logging anywhere.
Root cause
Paperwork replaced verification; a signed promise is treated as a control.
Fix
Sample-test critical vendors: ask for configuration evidence, not answers; run a joint test for the top five.
Prevention
Annual evidence review for high-tier vendors, with consequences written into the contract.

Mistake 11: Alert thresholds never tuned per vendor

Symptom
One global threshold floods the queue during a big vendor's busy season and misses small vendors completely.
Root cause
The rule was tuned once, centrally, and never revisited as the vendor mix changed.
Fix
Move thresholds to a per-vendor lookup table fed by ninety-day history, and review it quarterly.
Prevention
Detection reviews use per-vendor volume charts, so drift is visible before it becomes noise or blindness.

Best-practice habits

Three small routines keep the whole topic alive without a big program. Put them in the team calendar; habits last longer than projects.

Daily

  • Triage R1 and R4 vendor-portal alerts with vendor context attached.
  • Check overnight look-alike domain alerts for partner brands.
  • Review new TPRM tickets tagged as suspicious contact.

Weekly

  • Review per-vendor login volume for spikes and dead accounts.
  • Check whether any vendor breach news matches your inventory.
  • Confirm new vendor accounts from the week all have owners and MFA.

Monthly

  • Reconcile the vendor inventory with spending and portal accounts.
  • Review suppression rules and per-vendor thresholds for drift.
  • Review one vendor end to end: account, access, logs, contract, and offboarding plan.

Response checklist for a vendor-path alert

  • Confirm the vendor identity behind the alert: account, vendor record, and contract owner in TPRM.
  • Pull the full timeline for the account: successes, failures, devices, countries, and the last known-good activity.
  • Contain the account: suspend or force reset, and revoke active sessions and tokens.
  • Check what the account can reach, and watch those paths for movement for the next seventy-two hours.
  • Notify the vendor through a known-good channel and ask them to check their own side.
  • Hunt mail and proxy logs for related look-alike domains and lures aimed at your staff.
  • Open or link the TPRM case, and record lessons for the quarterly vendor review.
