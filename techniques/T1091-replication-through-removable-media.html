T1091 – Replication Through Removable Media

🎯Tactics: Lateral Movement + Initial Access 💻Platforms: Windows 🧩Complexity: Low–Medium 🔍Detection difficulty: Medium–High 📊Version: 1.3

T1091 is the USB technique. Malware on one computer quietly copies itself onto every USB stick or external drive that gets plugged in. When that stick is later used on another computer, the infection starts there too. This is the classic way malware crosses into an air-gapped network — a network with no internet connection at all, used by power plants, military sites, and factories. ATT&CK lists two tactics for this technique. As Lateral Movement, a worm inside one office machine writes itself to each stick it sees, or renames its files to look like normal documents. As Initial Access, the attacker prepares the media before it reaches you: mailed infected drives, corrupted formatting stations, or modified stick firmware. Modern Windows blocks Autorun (the old feature that starts a program on a stick by itself) by default, so today the tricks are malicious shortcut (.lnk) files, double extensions like Report.pdf.exe, and plain user curiosity. The classic real-world case is Stuxnet (2010): public reporting states it traveled into the isolated Natanz enrichment plant in Iran on USB sticks and helped destroy about 1,000 centrifuges. If your organization allows USB media — especially near industrial systems — this technique deserves its own detections.

Initial Access→ Execution→ Replication via Removable Media (T1091)→ Lateral Movement→ Persistence→ Impact (incl. OT)

This technique is easy to confuse with T1200 – Hardware Additions. The difference is simple: T1200 adds a device with a new function, for example a dongle that pretends to be a keyboard. T1091 uses a device that looks completely normal — a storage stick that carries files — and the files themselves do the harm.

Think like the attacker

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

When attackers use this

Attackers reach for T1091 at two very different moments. The first moment is inside an active intrusion. The team already owns one or more office machines and wants to reach a network they cannot touch any other way. The target is usually an air-gapped plant, a government enclave, or a classified segment. They push a worm component to the owned machines and let it wait. Every time an employee plugs in a stick, the malware copies itself across within seconds. This is the Lateral Movement side, and it is how APT28 moved data toward air-gapped machines and how APT30 used SHIPSHAPE, according to the ATT&CK page.

The second moment is the very start of an attack. Here the media itself is the weapon. FIN7 has mailed USB drives to companies; some of them were built to act as virtual keyboards the moment they were inserted. Other groups drop bait sticks in car parks or hand them out at conferences. When a curious employee plugs one in, the attacker gains initial access without a single phishing email. The ATT&CK description also counts mobile phones and even modified USB charging cables as carriers, because a smartphone mounts like a disk when connected to a PC.

How attackers do it, step by step

  1. Own one host. Get a foothold on an internet-connected PC with phishing, a drive-by download, or a valid account. This host becomes the USB watcher.
  2. Install the replication module. The malware starts a watcher that fires when a new volume mounts — a device-arrival notification or a polling loop that checks for drive letters every few seconds.
  3. Stage the payload on the stick. It writes its files fast: the main executable into a hidden folder, shortcut (.lnk) files next to real documents, and an autorun.inf where the OS still allows one.
  4. Disguise everything. Legitimate files get the hidden attribute set; a malicious copy takes their name, so the stick looks exactly the same as before. HIUPAN hides all existing files and leaves one fake installer visible.
  5. Wait for travel. Nothing more happens on the office PC. The stick leaves the building in a pocket or a bag — the employee is the transport, and no exploit was needed to move the malware.
  6. Cross the gap. On the isolated host, Autorun is usually blocked, so the payload is built to trigger anyway: a previewed shortcut, a double extension, or an exploit such as the LNK bug in CVE-2010-2568 that fired when Explorer merely drew the file icon.
  7. Spread inside the isolated network. The new host gets the same watcher, and sticks now move data in both directions — malware in, stolen files out. USBStealer worked exactly this way for APT28.
  8. Stay quiet. The implant hides its files, stops local AV tools where it can, and waits weeks or months. Stolen data leaves the same way the malware came in: on a stick.

Where it works best

🏭Air-gapped OT plants

Energy, water, and manufacturing sites with no internet path. OT (operational technology) means the computers that control physical machines. The stick is often the only bridge in, which is why Stuxnet, Ramsay, and USBferry all targeted these places.

🏛Government and military

Classified enclaves with strict rules about what data may leave the network (egress) but human media flows. Agent.btz hit US military networks in 2008 through exactly this gap.

🏦Finance and hospitality

Card-data environments have tight firewalls but loose USB habits. FIN7 mailed drives straight to employees and let the postman do the delivery.

🧑‍💻Ordinary offices

Raspberry Robin and QakBot ride normal office sticks and shared PCs. One infected laptop in a meeting room can seed a whole floor.

🧳Travel and event bait

Conference giveaways, airport drops, and lost drives. The physical hand-off removes the need for any network contact with the victim.

Why attackers like this technique

  • It crosses air gaps — for an isolated network, a stick is often the only working path in.
  • Isolated networks log less: no proxy, no DNS, no cloud logs exist on the far side.
  • The user does the work: curiosity opens the file, so no exploit is needed at all.
  • Media transfer never appears in network telemetry (the records your network tools produce). The SIEM — the system that collects all security logs — therefore has a hole in its coverage.
  • It is cheap: one infected host passively seeds every stick that touches it, and each stick then infects more hosts.

What attackers need first

  • One compromised, online host with USB ports enabled — the first infected machine (patient zero).
  • A USB-aware worm or stager built for the target operating system.
  • Knowledge of how people really move data between sites — vendor laptops, report folders, backup routines.
  • For mail campaigns: a target list, printer, and postage — FIN7 shows how little else is required.
  • Patience: weeks can pass between planting the worm and the first stick travel.
  • Optional: custom BadUSB firmware for the highest-impact drops, so the device type itself lies.

Tools and example commands

Red teams use this technique in two defensible ways. First, they verify that device-control policy really blocks unapproved sticks. Second, they measure whether employees plug in unknown media. They use harmless canary tokens — fake items that raise an alert when touched (for example a canarytokens.com USB drop token). This runs during an authorized purple-team exercise, where attackers and defenders test together. The commands below are read-only checks that defenders and testers can run on hosts they own. They prove what a tester would see first: which devices a host remembers, and whether Autorun is really disabled.

Ethics and legality — read this first

USB replication is how real worms such as Stuxnet destroyed physical equipment. Test removable-media techniques only inside your own lab or with written authorization from the asset owner. Plugging unknown drives into systems you do not own, or mailing bait media to anyone, can break computer misuse laws and employment rules even when the intent is friendly. Log every test device you plant, brief the local team, and remove it after the exercise.

Think like the defender

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

What defenders can see

Removable media leaves a surprisingly loud trail on the host, even though nothing crosses the network. Windows announces every new storage device. The Kernel-PnP channel logs the device being configured and started (events 400/410). The Security log records event 6416 when a new external device is recognized. And the USBSTOR registry key keeps a permanent history of every mass-storage device the host has ever seen, including serial numbers. If your hosts forward only firewall and sign-in logs, you are blind to all of this — so the first fix is logging, not detection.

The second half of the trail is the file system. Sysmon — a free Microsoft add-on that records detailed activity on each Windows host — shows event 11 for every file created on a mounted volume. Look for the tell-tale patterns: autorun.inf appearing on E:\, shortcut files next to documents, and executables named like reports. Sysmon event 15 goes further and hashes alternate data streams — hidden extra data attached to a file — which catches the Zone.Identifier tricks worms use. Process events (Sysmon 1, Security 4688) then show anything that runs from the media. Plant hosts also need file-integrity monitoring (FIM — a tool that warns when protected files change), because on the far side of an air gap there are no network logs at all.

Where to look

  • Sysmon/Operational — events 1, 11 and 15, filtered on drive letters E:\/F:\ or on Device\HarddiskVolume paths.
  • Windows Security — 6416 (new device), 4663 (file writes), 4688 (process creation with media paths).
  • Registry HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Enum\USBSTOR on every host — pull it in EDR live response (EDR — endpoint security software that records what happens on each computer) or with a scheduled audit script.
  • Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-PnP/Configuration — events 400/410/420 for the full device lifecycle.
  • Forwarded Security 6416 and Kernel-PnP 400/410 events from plant terminals — media events still get logged even when the host has no network path.
  • FIM baselines on OT data folders — recipe files, PLC project files, HMI (operator touch panel) configurations.
  • EDR device-control dashboards — the blocked-device queue shows who tries which stick.

Why this matters to the SOC

One stick can undo every network control you own. The infection does not need a port, a VPN account, or a credential — it needs a pocket. For a SOC this changes three things. Blast radius (how far the damage spreads) jumps from one host to a whole segment, because the worm replicates without any network traffic. Data can leave the company without touching a single monitored exit point (egress), which defeats network-only DLP — data loss prevention tools that watch data moving over the network. And in OT environments the end state can be physical damage, not just encrypted files — Stuxnet proved that a USB-carried worm can break machines.

There is good news: USB events are a cheap early warning. A device-control violation — an unknown serial number that got allowed, a new VID/PID pair — usually shows up days or weeks before any malware does. Treating device arrival events as first-class security data gives you a leading indicator instead of a post-mortem.

Where it fits in the attack chain

T1091 usually sits mid-chain. Before it: T1566 phishing or T1078 valid accounts deliver the first implant to an internet-connected host. Next to it: T1200 hardware additions is its physical-access cousin — that technique adds a device with a new function (a keyboard emulator that types commands), while T1091 uses a normal-looking storage device whose files do the harm. After it: T1059 command interpreters run the dropped payload, T1547 boot autostart or new accounts give persistence inside the gap, and T1052 exfiltration over a physical medium describes the outbound half of the same stick. In OT incidents the chain ends at manipulation-of-control techniques — the Stuxnet lesson in Section 6.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

USB threats never went out of fashion. A small set of families keeps using sticks to reach machines that no other attack can touch. This section shows where to get fresh intel and who is known to use this technique.

Where threat intel comes from

🌍OSINT sources

Vendor blogs (ESET, Kaspersky Securelist, SentinelOne, Trend Micro) and advisories from CISA and national CERTs. Also abuse.ch for URL and payload feeds, AlienVault OTX pulses tagged with T1091, and VirusTotal for stick-borne sample families.

🧭ATT&CK-based intel

The official T1091 page lists the software and groups to track: Stuxnet (S0603), Agent.btz (S0092), Flame (S0143), PlugX (S0013), USBStealer (S0136), and Raspberry Robin (S1130). It also lists QakBot (S0650), APT28 (G0007), FIN7 (G0046) and more.

📡Commercial and community feeds

EDR vendors publish USB-borne campaign reports and hashes. ISACs for your sector (energy, water, finance) share OT-focused media warnings and STIX bundles you can import.

🏢Internal telemetry

Your own USBSTOR inventories, device-control block queues, helpdesk tickets about broken sticks, and offline AV findings from OT machines. This is often the best intel, because it describes your real media habits.

The intel cycle for this technique

  1. Define priorities. Decide which media flows matter most: plant transfers, contractor laptops, executive travel. These become your top hunt hypotheses.
  2. Collect. Pull device-arrival events, Sysmon file creates on media, and OT FIM diffs into one place; subscribe to vendor feeds that track USB worms.
  3. Process. Parse and normalize: USBSTOR strings into vendor/serial pairs, drive letters, mount points, file names into a common schema.
  4. Analyze. Correlate device arrival with later writes and executions per host. Look for chains, not single events — the same pattern 5.5 encodes.
  5. Produce. Turn findings into new Sigma selections (new bait file names, new VID/PID pairs, new hidden-folder names) and hunt queries.
  6. Feedback. Track false positives from each rule, tune allow-lists, and report coverage numbers to the detection engineering backlog.

  • Pivot from campaign reports to hunts: pull the file names, autorun.inf strings and hashes from vendor write-ups into 30-day searches over your Sysmon 11 data.

  • Share sanitized findings back to the community — USB worms cross organizations on physical media, so your neighbors benefit most.

Threat #6 — USB exfiltration (inverse direction)

5.5 Sigma Rules & Splunk SPL

Every threat from 5.3 and 5.4 gets exactly one Sigma rule and one matching SPL query. Copy them into your detection repo, test them with the purple-team methods from Section 3, then tune the drive letters and allow-lists to your own environment.

Sigma Rule #1 — Executable Staging to Removable Drive (matches Threat #1)
sigma #1 — usb_worm_file_staging.yml
title: Executable or LNK Staging to Removable Drive
id: 3f2c8a41-9b7d-4e62-a1c5-8d0f6b94e712
status: stable
description: Detects a process writing .exe or .lnk payloads to a removable drive letter. This is the classic USB worm replication pattern (Agent.btz, QakBot, Raspberry Robin) mapped to T1091.
references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1091/
    - http://blog.threatexpert.com/2008/11/agentbtz-threat-that-hit-pentagon.html
author: Detection Guide Team
date: 2026-01-15
tags:
    - attack.lateral_movement
    - attack.initial_access
    - attack.t1091
logsource:
    product: windows
    service: sysmon
detection:
    selection_write:
        EventID: 11
        TargetFilename|startswith:
            - 'E:\'
            - 'F:\'
        TargetFilename|endswith:
            - '.exe'
            - '.lnk'
    filter_legit:
        Image|endswith:
            - '\explorer.exe'
            - '\cmd.exe'
            - '\robocopy.exe'
            - '\onedrive.exe'
    condition: selection_write and not filter_legit
falsepositives:
    - Approved field-kit scripts that copy signed tools to USB for engineers
    - Admins imaging kiosks or point-of-sale terminals from approved media
level: high
Splunk SPL #1
spl — usb_worm_file_staging
index=sysmon EventCode=11 (TargetFilename="E:\\*" OR TargetFilename="F:\\*")
| regex TargetFilename="\\.(exe|lnk)$"
| search Image!="*\\explorer.exe" AND Image!="*\\cmd.exe" AND Image!="*\\robocopy.exe" AND Image!="*\\onedrive.exe"
| stats count dc(TargetFilename) as staged_files values(TargetFilename) as files by Computer, Image
| sort - staged_files

Catches a worm staging payloads onto any inserted stick. Main false-positive source is managed imaging or backup jobs, which the filter list removes; extend it with your approved copy tools.

Real-World Case Study

The worm that broke the air gap

In June 2010, a small antivirus company in Belarus, VirusBlokAda, found strange code on a customer’s computer in Iran. The code used a Windows feature nobody had ever abused this way, and it carried no visible purpose. Within weeks, Microsoft, Symantec, and Kaspersky had all confirmed it: a new worm called Stuxnet was spreading without any internet connection to its victims. It used an unprecedented four zero-day vulnerabilities, it knew how to talk to industrial controllers, and it was looking for one very specific factory.

Stuxnet became the textbook case for T1091. Analysts believe it was built by a state actor to sabotage uranium enrichment centrifuges at the Natanz facility in Iran — a plant that is air-gapped from the internet. The worm could not arrive over the network, because there was no network path. It arrived the way data always arrives in such a place: on a USB stick carried in by someone inside. Once it infected one internet-facing machine anywhere, it copied itself onto every stick that machine touched, waiting for one to travel.

How the attack chain worked

  1. Seed. The worm infected internet-connected machines outside the plant (supply chain, offices, contractor laptops).
  2. Ride. It copied itself to every inserted USB stick — the T1091 moment that made it famous.
  3. Cross. A stick entered Natanz; the LNK zero-day (CVE-2010-2568) executed the payload on folder preview, and autorun.inf covered older hosts.
  4. Spread. Inside the plant network it used network shares, a print-spooler zero-day, and more sticks to reach the control segment.
  5. Sabotage. It reprogrammed Siemens Step7 PLCs and their frequency drives, while returning normal-looking sensor values.
  6. Exfiltrate. It collected details about each new victim and sent them to its operators over the internet from machines outside the gap, and between infected machines through its peer-to-peer links.

How it was detected

Not by a monitoring center — by accident. Antivirus heuristics on machines outside the target, in an unrelated country, flagged unusual behavior and enormous replication rates. The worm also generated massive network traffic on infected networks (its peer-to-peer update protocol), which researchers noticed. Only later did teams realize the copies had ridden USB sticks across the air gap. The lesson was hard: a detection that existed (AV heuristics) worked only where the worm was visible, and the plant — where it mattered most — had no visibility at all.

Containment

  1. Identify and isolate infected hosts; halt any exchange of removable media between sites.
  2. Apply Microsoft patch MS10-046 for the LNK vulnerability, including to offline and OT-adjacent machines.
  3. Block Autorun/AutoPlay organization-wide and inventory every USB device via USBSTOR and 6416 events.
  4. Run removal tools on infected systems; verify the worm was gone from shares, print queues, and Step7 project folders.

Recovery

  1. Audit and restore PLC and drive configurations; re-validate every frequency-drive setting against engineering baselines.
  2. Replace or re-commission damaged centrifuge control equipment as inspection confirmed failures.
  3. Introduce a formal media-transfer process: dedicated, vetted sticks, offline AV scanning kiosks, and a media register.
  4. Stand up monitoring that works inside the gap: host logs, FIM on control-system files, and periodic offline scans.

Lessons learned

  • An air gap is a network control, not a security guarantee — media crosses it by design, so media must be controlled.
  • USB policy (M1034/M1042) and offline patching discipline (MS10-046) are as important in OT as firewalls.
  • Devices need monitoring inside the gap: host logs, FIM on control files, and periodic offline AV scans are mandatory.
  • Exploit protection at the endpoint (M1040-style ASR rules) blocks classes of bugs, not just one CVE.
  • Detection can be accidental — proactive hunts on USB and PLC artifacts are better than waiting for luck.
  • Telemetry that says everything is fine can be forged; independent integrity checks are the real control.
Why this case is the T1091 case

Stuxnet is the reason this technique exists in ATT&CK. Every stage of it has a matching detection pattern in Sections 5.4 and 5.5: stick infection (T1), LNK exploitation (T2), the air-gap bridge (T4), and the renamed-file trick (T5). Had those rules existed in 2010, each infected stick would have raised a signal long before a centrifuge broke.

Layered defenses that work

Mitigation & Defense

Defense in depth

No single control stops a USB worm: you need policy for the stick, hardening for the host, and monitoring for the moment both fail.

Official ATT&CK mitigations for T1091

The ATT&CK page lists three official mitigations: M1034 Limit Hardware Installation (limit USB devices and removable media), M1042 Disable or Remove Feature or Program (disable Autorun; restrict removable media by policy), and M1040 Behavior Prevention on Endpoint (ASR rules that block unsigned executables from running from USB drives). Supporting controls below add user training and AV depth, but the official three come first.

Enterprise defenses

🔐Device control & allow-listing (M1034)

Enforce USB allow-lists with endpoint DLP or EDR device control (Defender for Endpoint, DeviceLock, Seprol): only approved serials mount, others are read-only or blocked. Log every block — that queue is your early-warning feed.

⚙Kill Autorun and AutoPlay (M1042)

Set GPO NoDriveTypeAutoRun to 0xFF and deny write-removable access where possible. Windows 7+ blocks autorun.inf execution by default, but legacy hosts and kiosks stay risky until you verify the policy landed.

🛡Behavior prevention (M1040)

Enable Attack Surface Reduction rules such as blocking untrusted and unsigned processes from USB drives. Defender ASR and equivalent EDR exploit-prevention stop the payload class, not just one CVE.

🦠Offline AV scanning for OT

Any media entering a plant passes a dedicated scan kiosk with current signatures. Update the kiosk through a controlled one-way path. Antivirus/antimalware (M1049) remains a real layer even where clouds cannot reach.

🎓User training (M1017)

Teach the found-drive rule: never plug in unknown sticks; hand them to IT. Run sanctioned drop tests with canary tokens and report the results as metrics, not blame.

🏭OT/ICS media hardening

Air-gapped hosts get physical port lockdown, signed-file-only transfer processes, and a media register. Use dedicated transfer stations or data diodes instead of direct stick-to-HMI moves.

📊Monitoring & detection

Ship Sysmon, Security and Kernel-PnP logs from every host — including OT jump machines. Deploy the 5.5 rules, baseline media events per site, and alert on chains (mount → write → execute).

🤝Third-party media rules

Contractor laptops surrender media at the door; vendors receive your controlled sticks, not their own. Put the media-handling clause in contracts and audit it like PCI 9.4 expects.

Home user defenses

  • Never plug in a stick you found, received in the mail, or got at a conference — hand it to IT or destroy it.
  • Keep Windows fully updated: the famous LNK trick has been patched since MS10-046 in 2010.
  • Keep antivirus on and current; let it scan media on mount.
  • Do not double-click files on a stick; open documents from inside the real application instead.
  • Disable AutoPlay in Settings if it is not already off.
  • Encrypt any stick that carries personal or work files, and treat it like a house key.
  • Keep backups that are not connected to your PC — ransomware still arrives on media.

Best practices for day-to-day SOC work

  1. Baseline media behavior per site. Know which hosts see sticks daily; everything outside the baseline is your hunt list.
  2. Hunt with hypotheses weekly. For example: hosts where a 6416 was followed by 4688 from media in 15 minutes (rule 8) but no ticket exists.
  3. Purple-test the Sigma set. Use the Section 3 lab commands plus canary sticks to prove rules 1–8 fire; record the MTTD (mean time to detect — how long the alert takes).
  4. Keep the feedback loop. Every false positive from 5.5 should end as a filter or lookup update within the sprint, not left in the backlog forever.
  5. Document the USB runbook. Who pulls the stick, who images the host, who calls the OT engineer — decide before the Stuxnet-style alert arrives.
  6. Report one metric upward. Media policy violations per month, with the blocked-device trend, keeps USB risk visible to management.
  7. Review OT exceptions quarterly. Every scan-kiosk bypass and port exception needs a named owner and an expiry date.
Keep it simple

USB detection is one of the cheapest wins in security: the events are free, the rules are written above, and the volume is low. Turn on the logs this week, tune the rules this month, and the next Stuxnet-shaped stick will meet a SOC that is watching.
