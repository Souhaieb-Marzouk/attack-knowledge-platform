MITRE ATT&CK · Resource Development · Sub-technique

T1588.007 Artificial Intelligence

Before an attack starts, the attacker needs help. Today that help can come from generative AI: models that write text, images, voices, and code on demand. This guide explains how adversaries obtain access to AI tools, how groups already use them for phishing, deepfakes, and malware support, and what defenders can log, detect, and block. All content is educational and defensive.

Overview

What this technique is, why it matters to defenders, and where it sits in the attack chain.

What it is

T1588.007 covers the way attackers get access to generative AI tools to help their attacks. Generative AI is software that creates new text, images, audio, video, or code from instructions. A large language model (LLM) is the text kind: an AI model trained on huge amounts of text that writes human-like answers. A prompt is the instruction text you give such a model.

The attacker does not need to build anything here. They only need access: a public chat account, an application programming interface (API) key (a secret string that lets a program call a cloud service), or a copy of an open-weight model that runs on their own hardware. The tools then help with reconnaissance, social engineering, credential theft, and malicious content development.

Why it matters

AI removes two old defenses: cost and skill. A phishing mail in any language now takes seconds, not hours. Voice cloning (AI copying a person's voice from short audio samples) makes phone fraud cheap. A deepfake (fake image, audio, or video that looks or sounds like a real person) can replace a whole pretext. Malware code help lowers the bar for low-skill groups.

For defenders this is hard: prompting a public AI chat leaves no trace in your logs. You detect the outputs that touch your organization: clean but unusual mail, fresh sender domains, sensitive data pasted into AI sites, and fraud that survives your filters. ATT&CK added this sub-technique in 2024, and its list of documented users is growing with every version.

Where it fits

This technique lives in the Resource Development tactic (TA0042), the pre-compromise phase before any contact with the target. It is the seventh child of T1588 (Obtain Capabilities) and the newest of the family. Related ATT&CK entries include T1682 (Query Public AI Services), T1683 (Generate Content), and T1656 (Impersonation).

After the AI help is used, the results flow into the attack chain: phishing (T1566), impersonation, obfuscated scripts (T1027), and intrusion tools. Detection engineers treat T1588.007 as a quality multiplier: it rarely triggers an alert on its own. It makes every other step cheaper, faster, and more convincing.

    The same story in words

    This written version covers exactly the same eight steps as the animation. Read it if you prefer text, use it with a screen reader, or quote it in a training session.

    Step 1

    Collecting public material about the target

    The actor studies open-source intelligence (OSINT, information from public sources): earnings videos, podcast interviews, and conference talks of the target company. This builds the raw material for later voice cloning and deepfake video. Nothing has touched the victim, so the defender sees nothing.

    Step 2

    Obtaining access to AI services

    The actor gets AI access through anonymous free accounts, or with an API key bought from a criminal resale market, or with a stolen subscription. None of these accounts belong to the victim, so there is still nothing in victim telemetry to alert on. Only the AI provider may hold weak signals.

    Step 3

    Crafting lures and deepfake media

    The actor uses the AI services to draft spear-phishing text that mimics the finance team's tone, and to build deepfake audio and video of executives from the collected material. The work happens entirely on attacker-controlled accounts, so the victim still sees nothing.

    Step 4

    Registering a lookalike sender domain

    The actor registers a domain that resembles the company and points it at their mail setup. This step leaves a public trace: certificate transparency logs and domain registration feeds can show the new name within minutes. This is the first real defender visibility in the chain.

    Step 5

    Sending AI-written lures

    The mail gateway receives messages from the fresh domain. The text is clean and well written, so classic spam filters find little to score. A weak signal appears: a new sender, a young domain, and an unusual request pattern. One signal alone is rarely enough to block.

    Step 6

    The deepfake video meeting

    The actor joins a video call as a deepfaked executive and asks for an urgent payment to a "new supplier account". The meeting itself writes no useful security log. The last line of defense is a human process control: calling the executive back on a known number before any money moves.

    Step 7

    The transfer is executed

    A finance employee approves the payments. Identity and fraud systems can now show signals: a beneficiary appears for the first time, the approval falls outside the employee's normal pattern, and account activity looks abnormal. These are after-the-fact signals, not early warnings.

    Step 8

    SOC and fraud team respond

    The SOC, the fraud team, and the treasury join forces: freeze the payments, call the bank, start callback verification, and review AI usage rules. The team records which signals existed at each step and closes the gaps: domain monitoring, payment controls, and AI governance.

    How to read the animation

    Red flows are attacker actions. Amber flows carry the harmful content itself, such as lure mail or deepfake media. Blue elements belong to the defender side. The panel next to the canvas lists what a security operations center (SOC) could see at that exact moment, including the steps where the honest answer is nothing.

    What is realistic here

    This story is a composite of public reporting on AI-assisted fraud and phishing. The tools, names, and timings are simplified for teaching. The important part is the shape: AI access is cheap and invisible, the best early signals sit in public data such as domains, and the strongest control at the payment moment is a human process, not a log rule.

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    Why attackers reach for AI, how they get access in principle, and where the process leaks traces.

    ?Ethics and scope

    This section explains attacker behavior so defenders can detect it. It stays at the level of concepts and public reporting. It contains no working prompts for abuse, no impersonation scripts, and no fraud instructions. Any hands-on practice belongs in an isolated lab you own, with fake data, and clearly labeled as lab-only.

    An attacker chooses the cheapest path to a convincing attack. Writing flawless lure text in a foreign language used to need a native speaker. Cloning a voice used to need special equipment and skill. Today a free AI chat does the first task in seconds, and a voice service does the second for a few dollars. Public reporting keeps adding examples: the ATT&CK entry for this technique already lists state-linked groups, financial crime crews, and espionage campaigns.

    AI also buys scale and variety. One operator can produce hundreds of unique, well-written mails instead of one copied template, which defeats rules that look for repeated text. The same services can draft fake recruiter profiles with AI images, as the Contagious Interview group reportedly did, or build whole conversational pathways for voice phishing, as reported for ShinyHunters using a commercial voice agent service.

    Access channels

    Free tiers and anonymous accounts

    Most AI vendors offer free or cheap tiers, and sign-up needs little identity. This is the lowest bar: an anonymous account with a disposable mail address is enough for text generation. The cost to the attacker is close to zero, and no victim-side system is involved at all.

    Stolen and resold accounts and API keys

    Criminal markets resell AI subscriptions, prepaid credits, and API keys, often harvested from stealer malware. A bought API key lets a program call the model at scale. This matters for defenders: keys stolen from your staff or customers put your names on hostile AI traffic.

    Abuse of a corporate tenant

    When attackers phish a valid user, they may reach the company's own AI services: an enterprise chat assistant, an API account, or a coding agent. ATT&CK documents campaigns where adversaries obtained access to enterprise AI coding tools to support intrusions. Your own logs then become the evidence.

    Open-weight and local models

    Open-weight models are AI models whose files anyone can download and run on their own hardware. They remove accounts, keys, and provider logging from the picture entirely. Public reporting notes this lowers the entry cost for abuse, and endpoint agents can still see the unusual model files and processes.

    Conditions and preparation

    The AI access itself is simple, but useful attacks still need preparation. The actor needs real target material, because AI quality depends on good input: public speeches for voice cloning, real mail threads for tone, and real org charts for plausible names. Staff pages and conference bios on your public website are a common source. The actor also needs delivery infrastructure, such as fresh sender domains and phone or meeting channels. Groups with few skills use AI to copy known attack patterns; capable groups use it to make each message unique and to speed up payload development. Either way, the AI step leaves most of its traces in the attacker's world, not yours.

    iLab-only practice example

    A safe classroom exercise looks like this: in an offline lab, generate a benign text with any public AI chat, then compare it with three mails your own team wrote, and list which features would change in mail gateway scores, sender reputation, and user report rates. You are studying the defensive effect of AI-written text without creating any harmful content. Label the exercise and the lab network clearly.

    Variations and common mistakes attackers make

    VariationWhat changesTrace it leaves
    Public chat with anonymous accountFree and fast, limited volumeAlmost nothing on the victim side
    API key at scaleProgrammatic, high volume, costs moneyResold keys, stolen keys, provider-side abuse signals
    Corporate AI account misuseUses your tenant and your dataCloud audit logs, new keys, odd upload volumes
    Local open-weight modelNo provider, no key, needs hardwareModel files and processes on the attacker's endpoint

    Mistakes that expose AI-assisted attacks

    AI text is clean, so unusual perfection itself can be a weak marker when the sender is new. Deepfake video still shows small defects on close look, such as odd lighting or lip timing. Recycled voice samples repeat artifacts across calls. Stolen API keys keep a spending pattern that fraud teams at providers can flag. And LLM-written code carries style quirks: researchers linked one wiper family to AI generation partly from its nonsensical comments. Each mistake is a pivot point for the defender.

    4

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    What the technique looks like from the inside, which artifacts survive, and a short triage flow your team can follow.

    Start with an honest fact: when the attacker prompts a public AI chat from an anonymous account, no alert fires in your security information and event management (SIEM) system, and none will. MITRE states this openly. Its official detection strategy for this technique (DET0842) says most of the activity takes place outside the visibility of the target organization. Good teams therefore work on two layers. The first layer is process: strong payment and identity controls that do not depend on spotting fake content. The second layer is telemetry: detect the moments when AI access touches systems you do log, such as your cloud, your mail flow, your proxy, your identity provider, or your endpoints.

    Artifacts the technique leaves behind

    Cloud and identity artifacts

    Corporate AI usage appears in cloud audit logs: model invocations from new IP ranges or networks (ASNs), fresh API keys, dormant identities that suddenly call AI services, and bulk uploads to AI endpoints. The ASN is the network operator behind an IP address; a new ASN for an AI call is a useful signal. Identity provider audit logs add the helpdesk side: password resets and security-info re-registrations.

    Mail and voice artifacts

    AI-written lures arrive from senders you have never seen, on domains that are days old. The text is clean, so content filters score it low. Voice fraud shows up as helpdesk or finance calls with unusual requests. The artifacts are metadata: domain age, sender history, request pattern, call records.

    Network and endpoint artifacts

    Proxy and DLP logs catch staff pasting sensitive data into public AI chats, visible as large POST requests to AI domains. Endpoints can meet AI-assisted malware: scripts with odd style, encoded commands, or fake coding-task payloads delivered by fake recruiters. Local AI models leave large model files and new processes. Webserver logs show scrapers pulling your public pages.

    Where to look

    Why this matters for the attack chain

    Catching the AI step early collapses the rest of the chain. A lookalike domain blocked before first mail means no lure arrives. An abused API key disabled quickly means no bulk generation from your tenant. A payment or reset callback rule defeats even a perfect deepfake, because the control tests the request, not the media. That is why this pre-compromise technique has real detection value: it tells you which artifacts your downstream detections should expect, and where AI quality will defeat naive content rules.

    Short triage flow

    1. Receive or find the marker. A domain alert, a new-sender mail pattern, a CloudTrail anomaly, a DLP hit, or a scraper burst gives you the first artifact.
    2. Enrich it. Check domain age and registration, sender history, the identity's past AI usage, and the file's download lineage. Note the ASN behind any new IP.
    3. Check exposure. Search your own telemetry for the marker over the last 90 days: proxy, mail, cloud, identity, EDR. One query per source, saved as a scheduled hunt.
    4. Validate hits. Decide benign or malicious with full context: user, role, process ancestry, beneficiary, timing. A legitimate developer pasting code to an AI site looks different from bulk uploads from a service account.
    5. Escalate or document. Real hits become an incident, and fraud cases run in parallel with the fraud team. No hits become an intelligence note with the next review date, so the hunt stays alive.

    ✓Good practice

    Turn every credible public report on AI abuse into one scheduled hunt plus one saved query, and record the owner and the review date. Detection coverage for T1588.007 is built from metadata and process controls, because the AI step itself usually writes no log line in your world.

    iWhat ATT&CK officially says

    MITRE lists one official mitigation for this technique: Pre-compromise [M1056], because the activity happens on accounts and hardware that the target organization does not own. The official detection strategy is DET0842 — Detection of Artificial Intelligence, with one main analytic, AN1974. We quote it honestly: "Much of this activity will take place outside the visibility of the target organization, making detection of this behavior difficult. Detection efforts may be focused on behaviors relating to the potential use of generative artificial intelligence (i.e. Phishing, Phishing for Information)."

    In practice this means your detections watch the edges where AI output touches your systems: mail metadata, cloud audit logs, proxy and DLP traffic, endpoint process trees, PBX and identity records, and your own webserver logs. Read the official T1588.007 page for the full strategy text. The rest of section 5 turns these official hints into concrete rules.

    5.1Regulation mapping

    An AI-focused capability threat matters to auditors because AI-assisted fraud and AI-built payloads sit behind many reportable incidents, and because regulators now also ask how you govern AI itself. The table below maps the technique to common frameworks. Use it to justify detection engineering work in risk language, and to know which evidence an auditor may ask for.

    FrameworkControlWhy it appliesExpected evidence
    GDPR Art. 32, Art. 5(1)(f) Requires security measures against unlawful access and deception. AI-generated phishing and deepfake fraud are now common entry paths to personal data. Mail control records, fraud incident tickets, staff awareness training records
    ISO/IEC 27001:2022 A.5.7, A.8.7, A.8.16, A.5.19 Covers threat intelligence, malware protection, monitoring activities, and supplier security. AI services are suppliers whose use must be governed and logged. AI usage policy, approved-tools list, monitoring runbooks, Statement of Applicability entries
    SOC 2 CC3.2, CC6.1, CC7.2 Requires analysis of threats, access control, and monitoring for anomalies. Stolen AI API keys and abused cloud identities are access-control risks. Risk register entries for AI abuse, API key inventory, alert triage records
    PCI DSS 4.0 5.2, 12.3.1, 12.6.3 Cardholder environments need malware defenses, targeted risk analysis, and staff awareness of social engineering, which deepfakes directly attack. Anti-malware records, payment-change process documents, phishing awareness materials
    NIST CSF 2.0 GV.SC-07, ID.RA-05, DE.CM-09 Links supply chain risk and monitoring. AI tools are part of the modern supply chain, both as services and as model assets on endpoints. Supplier and AI service inventory, risk mappings, monitoring coverage lists
    NIST AI RMF 1.0 MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE functions The dedicated framework for AI risk. It asks organizations to map how AI systems are used, measure misuse, and manage incidents, including malicious use by outsiders. AI inventory, misuse scenarios, AI incident playbooks, governance review minutes

    iAuditor-friendly framing

    When an auditor asks how you address a pre-compromise technique, answer with the downstream controls: AI usage governance, payment and reset callback verification, mail controls for new senders, DLP coverage for AI endpoints, and a documented intelligence process. Keep one page that lists which T1588.007 threat each control touches. That page becomes your evidence pack.

    Every item you ingest needs a confidence level, or your alerts will argue with each other. Use three levels and write them into the detection metadata.

    4. Measure quarterly: which intelligence items produced detections, which detections fired, and which feeds never produced anything useful. Drop weak feeds, keep strong ones.

    The six threats below are the realistic ways this technique turns into risk for an organization. They are ranked and categorized by platform. The same six threats are used in 5.4 and 5.5, so the section stays practical.

    ThreatRankCategoryOne-line summary
    TH-1HighCloud · IdentityAnomalous AI platform API usage in the corporate cloud, from new keys and new infrastructure
    TH-2HighIdentity · EmailAI-generated phishing mail and voice-phishing pathways aimed at finance and helpdesk teams
    TH-3MediumNetwork · DLPSensitive data pasted into public AI web chats through the corporate proxy
    TH-4MediumEndpoint · WindowsAI-assisted malware and fake recruiter payloads reaching endpoints
    TH-5HighIdentity · VoiceVoice-clone or deepfake fraud attempt against finance staff or the IT helpdesk

    High TH-1 anomalous AI platform API usage in corporate cloud

    Description. An attacker uses AI services through your cloud tenant: model invocations from new IP ranges or networks, brand-new API keys, a dormant identity that suddenly calls AI models, or bulk uploads to AI endpoints. Preconditions. A phished cloud identity, a stolen key, or a service account with weak governance. Impact. Free compute for attack preparation, reconnaissance over your own data, and a persistence path through new keys. Justification. Directly documented in ATT&CK campaigns where adversaries obtained AI access inside tenant infrastructure, and reliably visible in cloud audit logs, so High rank with good detection odds.

    High TH-2 AI phishing mail and voice-phishing pathways

    Description. AI-written mail targets finance and helpdesk staff: fraudulent payment changes, urgent credential requests, or recruiter pretexts. Commercial voice-agent services can run scripted voice-phishing pathways, as documented for ShinyHunters. Preconditions. Public material about your staff, plus a fresh sender domain or a phone channel. Impact. Payment fraud, credential theft, and unauthorized access through human approval. Justification. The most common documented outcome of this technique, and it defeats content filters, which forces metadata and process detection, so High.

    Medium TH-3 sensitive data pasted into public AI chats

    Description. Employees paste contracts, source code, or customer data into public AI web chats. Attackers do not need this traffic, but it feeds them: leaked data improves their pretexts and reconnaissance, and it shows your AI governance gap. Preconditions. Uncontrolled proxy access to AI sites. Impact. Data exposure, loss of trade secrets, regulatory findings. Justification. Very common but indirect for attackers, so Medium; detectable from proxy and DLP logs with medium false-positive risk.

    Medium TH-4 AI-assisted malware and recruiter payloads

    Description. AI-generated or AI-refined scripts, and fake coding-task payloads from fake recruiters, reach endpoints: encoded PowerShell stages, odd-style wiper or loader scripts, or project archives from a "coding interview". Preconditions. A user opens an attachment or clones a fake repository. Impact. Endpoint compromise, credential theft, wiper or ransomware follow-up. Justification. Documented for NIMBUS MANTICORE, APT28's LAMEHUG, and Contagious Interview; detection rests on interpreter behavior, so Medium with a workable endpoint rule.

    High TH-5 voice-clone fraud against finance or IT helpdesk

    Description. The attacker clones an executive's voice from public audio and calls the finance team or the IT helpdesk. The request is an urgent payment or a password reset and MFA re-registration for a "locked-out" manager. Preconditions. Public voice samples, known names and roles, and a helpdesk that resets identities on a phone request. Impact. Payment fraud, or account takeover through a reset your own staff performed in good faith. Justification. This is where voice clones pay off: a trusted helpdesk reset bypasses phishing controls entirely. Detectable in identity provider audit logs and PBX records, and defeated by callback process controls, so High.

    Low TH-6 AI scraper harvesting public pages and staff profiles

    Description. Automated scrapers, often AI-driven, bulk-download your public website: team pages, press releases, conference bios, and PDFs. The structured data feeds the reconnaissance and prompt-building behind TH-2 and TH-5. Preconditions. Nothing; your public site is open by design. Impact. Indirect: better target lists and pretexts for the attacker, plus content theft. Justification. The harm is indirect and the traffic can look legitimate, but it is visible in webserver logs through user agent and rate anomalies, so Low with real detection value.

    5.4Logs and patterns

    For each threat: where to look, which fields matter, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and one realistic event-shaped example. Field names follow the most common logging formats; adjust them to your stack.

    TH-1 — anomalous AI platform API usage in corporate cloud

    Log sources
    AWS CloudTrail (for example eventSource bedrock.amazonaws.com); Azure and Google Cloud audit logs; SaaS AI admin logs
    Key fields
    eventSource, eventName, userIdentity.type, userIdentity.userName, sourceIPAddress, userAgent, aws_account_id
    Normal behavior
    Model calls from documented applications, stable source ranges, keys created by approved automation only
    Suspicious behavior
    Model invocations from new IPs or ASNs, a dormant identity suddenly using AI, new access keys outside onboarding, bulk uploads to AI endpoints
    Detection logic
    AI model invocation or new IAM key creation where the source address is outside corporate ranges (the ASN, the network operator behind an IP, helps here)

    The service account in the example had not called a model in over a year, and the source address sits in the reserved documentation range 198.51.100.0/24, which stands in for an unknown hosting network in teaching material.

    TH-2 — AI phishing mail and voice-phishing pathways

    Log sources
    Microsoft 365 message trace and tenant mail logs; mail gateway verdicts; voice platform records; helpdesk ticketing
    Key fields
    SenderAddress, RecipientAddress, Subject, Directionality, SpamConfidenceLevel, domain_age_days (enrichment)
    Normal behavior
    Mail from known partner domains with history; spam scores driven by links and attachments; requests follow documented processes
    Suspicious behavior
    First-seen sender on a young or risky top level domain, flawless text, urgency plus a payment or credential change, scripted calls from voice-agent platforms
    Detection logic
    Inbound mail from a new sender whose domain is on a risky top level domain or younger than 30 days, enriched with domain age data

    !Process control: deepfake callback verification

    No log rule detects a deepfake voice call, because the call itself writes no security log your SIEM reads. The control that works is a process: for any payment change or credential reset requested by phone, video, or mail, call the requester back on a number you already have on file, never a number from the request. Treat this rule as mandatory process, and log the callback result in the ticket. This is a defensive process control, not a log detection, and it is the last line that defeated several reported deepfake fraud attempts.

    TH-3 — sensitive data pasted into public AI web chats

    Log sources
    Proxy logs (POST requests); data loss prevention (DLP) verdicts; SSL inspection metadata where lawful
    Key fields
    cs-method, c-uri-domain, c-uri, cs-bytes (request body size), src_ip, user, dlp_category (enrichment)
    Normal behavior
    Small approved usage of enterprise AI tools; short prompts; documented developer accounts
    Suspicious behavior
    Large POST bodies to public AI chat domains from unusual users, repeated uploads, DLP category hits such as financial or source code data
    Detection logic
    Proxy POST to a known AI chat domain where the request body is large (20 KB or more) or a DLP integration tagged the content

    TH-4 — AI-assisted malware and recruiter payloads on endpoints

    Log sources
    Sysmon Event ID 1 (process create); EDR process telemetry; browser download history; mail attachment records
    Key fields
    Image, CommandLine, ParentImage, User, Hashes
    Normal behavior
    Script interpreters started by management tools, deployment systems, or admins with plain, reviewed commands
    Suspicious behavior
    Encoded command flags, base64 download-and-run chains, or interpreters spawned directly by a browser or mail client after an external download
    Detection logic
    Script interpreter with an encoded or download-style command, whose parent process is a browser or mail client (recent external download lineage)

    In the example a PowerShell stage (encoded with the -enc flag, which hides the command as base64 text) starts directly under the browser, shortly after the user downloaded a "coding interview task" archive from a recruiter contact. That parent-child pair is the download lineage the detection uses.

    TH-5 — voice-clone fraud against finance or IT helpdesk

    Log sources
    Identity provider audit logs (Microsoft Entra ID audit); IT helpdesk ticketing; PBX call detail records
    Key fields
    category, activityDisplayName, result, initiatedBy.user.userPrincipalName, targetResources.userPrincipalName; PBX: caller_id, callee_extension, duration_seconds
    Normal behavior
    Password resets and security-info re-registrations from the standard helpdesk service account, during ticketed working hours
    Suspicious behavior
    A reset or MFA re-registration on a finance or executive account performed outside the standard service account, or shortly after an inbound call, with no ticket
    Detection logic
    Successful password reset or security-info re-registration not performed by the standard helpdesk service accounts; pair the hit with PBX call records in the ticket

    In the example a helpdesk analyst reset the password of the chief financial officer account minutes after an inbound call, with no linked ticket. The event itself is normal admin work. It only becomes evidence when you join it with the PBX record and find no callback was logged. The client address 10.20.6.12 is a private example range.

    !Process control: callback verification for identity resets

    A password reset or MFA re-registration requested by phone is exactly what a voice clone needs. Require helpdesk staff to call the requester back on the number stored in the staff directory, never the number that called them, and record the callback in the ticket before any reset. This is the same defensive process control as the payment callback in TH-2, applied to identities, and it defeats the reset even when the voice is perfect.

    TH-6 — AI scraper harvesting public pages and staff profiles

    Log sources
    Webserver access logs (Apache or nginx); WAF logs; CDN logs; bot management reports
    Key fields
    client address (src), method, path (c-uri), status, user agent (c-useragent), bytes; request rate per source
    Normal behavior
    Declared search engine crawlers that fetch robots.txt first; low rates on staff pages; browsers with full header sets
    Suspicious behavior
    One source fetching dozens of team or people pages per minute, generic client libraries or empty user agents, no robots.txt request
    Detection logic
    GET requests to staff or profile paths with a script user agent, aggregated per source; alert when a source exceeds 30 such requests in 5 minutes

    The same source pulled more than 60 staff and profile pages in four minutes and never requested robots.txt, the file that tells crawlers what they may fetch. The python-requests user agent is a generic script library, not a declared crawler. The source address uses the reserved documentation range 203.0.113.0/24 for teaching.

    5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    One Sigma rule and one matching Splunk query per threat. The Sigma logic and the SPL logic match by design. Replace index names, watchlist contents, and enrichment lookups with your own values before deploying, and record the mapping from rule to threat in your detection inventory.

    TH-1 — Sigma: AI model usage or new keys from unknown infrastructure

    SPL for TH-1. Assumes CloudTrail data lands in index=aws with sourcetype=aws:cloudtrail; extend the corporate-range filter with your real ranges (for example 172.16.0.0/12) before deploying.

    To cover the dormant-identity case, compare each userIdentity.userName against a first-seen table of AI usage (for example with a weekly summary index): an identity with no model calls in 90 days that starts invoking models deserves review even from a corporate range.

    TH-2 — Sigma: phishing from young or risky sender domain

    The custom field domain_age_days must come from an enrichment join of your domain registration or passive DNS data onto the message trace records; a value of 30 or less counts as young. If you do not enrich, keep only the TLD selection. Assumes Microsoft 365 message trace data in index=o365 with sourcetype=o365:message_trace plus a lookup domain_intel carrying the age field.

    TH-3 — Sigma: large upload or DLP hit to public AI chat

    The field dlp_category comes from a DLP integration that tags proxy transactions; it is not part of the raw proxy record. Sigma maps cs-method and cs-bytes to cs_method and bytes_in in Splunk stream:http data, and c-uri-domain is extracted from the URL. Assumes proxy data in index=proxy with sourcetype=stream:http.

    TH-4 — Sigma: encoded script stage after external download

    SPL for TH-4. Assumes Sysmon data lands in index=sysmon with sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational; adjust to your environment.

    TH-5 — Sigma: identity reset outside standard helpdesk accounts

    The two excluded principals stand in for your real helpdesk service accounts; keep this list short and owned. For a sharper rule, join the target user against your VIP or finance group, and pair each hit with the PBX call record of the previous hour. Assumes Entra ID audit data lands in index=azure with sourcetype=azure:aad:audit from the Splunk Add-on for Microsoft Cloud Services; flatten the JSON field paths to match your extraction.

    TH-6 — Sigma: scripted harvester on staff profile pages

    Sigma webserver field names map to Splunk access_combined fields: cs-method to method, c-uri to uri_path, c-useragent to useragent, and src_ip to clientip. The aggregation condition counts matching requests per source over the 5 minute timeframe; the SPL mirrors that with a bucket of 5 minutes and count > 30.

    Tuning guidance

    Start every rule in search-only mode for two weeks and review hits with the data owner. For TH-1 add your real corporate and VPN ranges to the filter instead of lowering severity. For TH-2 keep the top level domain list short and local, and join a first-seen sender table. For TH-3 agree an approved-tools list with legal and development so allowlisted enterprise AI domains can be excluded. For TH-4 review the top encoded commands your deployment tools use and allowlist them explicitly. For TH-5 own the helpdesk allowlist and join hits with PBX records before you page anyone. For TH-6 allowlist approved scanners and crawlers, and keep the path list local to your site structure.

    Validation in the lab

    Test each rule in a lab before production. For TH-1 replay a trimmed CloudTrail InvokeModel event against your search head and confirm the rule fires. For TH-2 send a test mail from a documentation-safe example domain to a lab mailbox. For TH-3 POST a large harmless text file to a lab endpoint and confirm the size logic. For TH-4 run a harmless encoded Write-Output command from a lab browser and confirm the fields. For TH-5 replay a trimmed Entra audit reset event for a lab test user and confirm the filter logic. For TH-6 loop a harmless curl fetch of your lab site's team pages more than 30 times and confirm the count condition. Keep the test steps in the rule ticket so the next engineer can re-run them.

    Real-World Case Study

    The 2024 Arup deepfake video call fraud in Hong Kong shows the AI capability of this technique at work in a single fraud case, with no malware involved at all.

    This case is the most famous criminal (non-state) use of the capability this technique describes. A finance employee joined a video meeting with people who looked and sounded like the company's chief financial officer and several colleagues. All of them were deepfakes. The employee then sent about 25 million dollars in company funds. All facts below come from public reporting by press and from statements the company later gave to media; details that were never confirmed publicly are marked as such. The primary public reference is the February 2024 press coverage of the Hong Kong police statements, including CNN's report of 4 February 2024.

    Context

    Arup is a large engineering and consulting firm with offices worldwide, including Hong Kong. Press reporting in February 2024 described how, in late January of that year, an employee in the Hong Kong finance office received an email that appeared to come from the firm's chief financial officer in the United Kingdom, proposing a confidential transaction. The employee was initially suspicious. The turning point came with a video conference: the "CFO" and several colleagues joined the call, and their faces and voices looked real. Public reporting stated the personas appeared to be recreated from public footage, such as video presentations available online. Trusting the meeting, the employee followed the payment instructions. The firm publicly confirmed the incident in May 2024.

    Mid-January 2024

    The first lure email

    Per press reporting, an employee in the Hong Kong finance office received an email that appeared to come from the UK chief financial officer about a confidential transaction. The message raised suspicion, which shows the human instinct worked at first.

    Late January 2024

    The deepfake video conference

    The employee joined a video call with multiple participants who looked and sounded like the CFO and known colleagues. Press reporting described the personas as appearing to be recreated from public footage. No security log recorded anything unusual, because a video meeting is just normal traffic.

    25–31 January 2024

    Fifteen transfers

    Following the meeting's instructions, the employee executed 15 transfers totaling about 25 million United States dollars (about 200 million Hong Kong dollars) to five local bank accounts, according to police statements quoted in press.

    2024-01-27

    First police report

    Press reporting gives 27 January as the date the case first reached the Hong Kong police. Note that other reports place the transfers between 25 and 31 January, so public accounts differ on the exact order of events. The date is included here as press-reported.

    2024-02-04

    Police describe the method publicly

    Hong Kong police briefed the press on the case, and the multi-persona deepfake method became public knowledge worldwide. The victim company was not named at this stage. This early, detailed description turned one fraud into a global warning.

    Early February 2024

    Discovery inside the company

    The fraud came to light when the employee checked with headquarters and learned no such transaction existed. Public accounts differ on the exact day and the order against the police report, so the timing here is approximate and press-reported.

    May 2024

    The company confirms

    The engineering firm Arup publicly confirmed it was the victim of the deepfake fraud, in statements to media. The company said it had strengthened its financial processes and warned other organizations to raise awareness.

    Later in 2024

    Arrests reported

    Later in 2024, press reporting described arrests in Hong Kong connected to the case, including people linked to the accounts that had received the funds. This shows the money laundering side of the operation running on local contacts.

    The attack chain in T1588.007 terms

    Detection points across the chain

    Each step had at least one moment where a prepared defender or a prepared process could have caught it. The table collects them in one view. It also shows the hard limit of this technique: the strongest signals sit in metadata and in human process, because the deepfake content itself scores clean.

    Chain stepEarliest available signalWhere a defender could have seen it
    Lure mailFirst-seen sender on a fresh domain with clean textMail gateway metadata rules (TH-2): new sender plus young domain review before delivery
    Deepfake meetingNo useful security log; pressure and secrecy in the requestProcess control: out-of-band callback on a known number before any payment instruction is accepted

    Containment, eradication, and recovery

    Containment. In a payment fraud case, containment means stopping the money and the channel. The remaining transfers are halted with treasury, the receiving banks are asked to freeze and recall funds, the lure sender domain and addresses are blocked at the mail gateway, and the employee's account and sessions are secured in case the attacker kept a contact path. No endpoint was compromised, so there was no host isolation to do.

    Eradication. There was no malware to remove, so eradication is about removing what made the attack possible. Public reporting does not describe mailbox-level access, but a careful response would still check the employee's mailbox for forwarding or inbox rules and review who else received the lure. The process gap is then closed: payment changes for new beneficiaries now need verification and multi-person approval, and video requests for money are verified through a known contact.

    Recovery. Recovery had two parts. Financially, recovery depended on the fraud side of the house: freezing the remaining accounts, contacting the receiving banks, and working with the Hong Kong police, which later led to reported arrests; most of the lost amount was not publicly reported as recovered. Operationally, the company recovered trust by confirming the incident, strengthening its financial processes, and warning others. That is typical for this technique: the controls that work are human and procedural, supported by small technical signals.

    Impact and consequences

    The direct loss was about 25 million dollars across 15 transfers. The wider impact reached every finance team that read the story: organizations worldwide revisited how video instructions for payments are verified. The case also fixed a public belief: deepfakes are good enough for multi-person video meetings, not just short voice clips.

    Team response and communication

    A case like this needs the whole team, not one desk. Threat intelligence (CTI) tracks the deepfake fraud pattern and shares indicators. The SOC joins mail and identity signals into one picture. Incident response (IR) leads containment and the verification steps. IT secures accounts and mail rules. Legal handles the police process and bank claims. Communications (comms) manages statements, and finance owns treasury, the payment freeze, and the process changes. Two communication choices mattered here: the police described the method early and in detail, and the victim confirmed its role in May 2024 instead of staying anonymous. Honest, early communication is part of the defense.

    Tools involved

    Attacker side: press reporting did not name the exact AI tools used, so the toolset is unknown; the method required AI or manual deepfake video and voice generation, a video conferencing platform, and disposable mail infrastructure. Defender side: the controls that matter are mail gateway metadata checks, treasury and fraud monitoring, payment and callback processes, and a fraud-plus-cyber incident playbook in the SIEM. The tools are ordinary; the difference was made by process and speed.

    Lessons learned

    • Verify unusual money or identity requests with an out-of-band callback on a number you already have on file, never a number or link from the request itself.
    • Require a payment-change process with extra checks for new beneficiaries, regardless of who appears to ask.
    • Use multi-person authorization for large transfers, so one deceived employee cannot move everything.
    • Train deepfake awareness regularly with live-style scenarios, not as a one-off video, and include video meetings.
    • Treat early suspicion as an action trigger: the employee doubted the first mail, and that doubt should have started verification.
    • Build one playbook that joins fraud response and cyber response, because this attack had no malware but needed both teams.
    • Watch your public data too: staff pages and videos feed the personas, so know what your website gives away.

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop an attacker from prompting an AI service, but you can make the outputs fail. Enterprise controls, home advice, and a prioritized action list.

    ATT&CK is honest about this technique: the one official mitigation is Pre-compromise [M1056], and its description says the technique cannot be easily mitigated with preventive controls, because it happens on accounts and hardware you do not own. Defense therefore has two goals. First, make AI-generated content fail at the point of action, with process controls that test the request instead of the media. Second, govern the AI access you do own, so your cloud, keys, and endpoints cannot be used as free AI infrastructure.

    !Common trap

    Do not rely on content filters to catch AI-written text. Modern AI output has no spelling errors and no repeated template, which is exactly what classic filters score on. Detect on metadata (sender, domain age, first-seen identity) and stop the action with process controls (callback, multi-person approval). Also do not ban AI without logging: a silent ban drives usage into shadow channels, meaning unapproved tools and personal accounts where you see nothing.

    Key facts

    Technique
    T1588.007 — Artificial Intelligence (Resource Development, TA0042, platform PRE)
    Core idea
    Attackers obtain access to generative AI tools, such as large language models, to aid reconnaissance, social engineering, credential theft, and malicious content development
    Status
    Newest sub-technique of T1588 (added 2024); documented usage list keeps growing
    Official guidance

    Response checklist

    • Freeze first: for payment fraud, ask treasury to halt pending transfers and call the receiving bank at once; for identity fraud, disable the reset and secure the account.
    • Pivot the marker (domain, sender, key, host, caller number) across 90 days of mail, proxy, cloud, identity, and EDR logs.
    • Enrich: domain age and registration for senders, identity history for cloud usage, PBX records for voice claims, download lineage for endpoints.
    • Confirm scope: which users, mailboxes, keys, identities, and hosts were touched; note first and last seen.
    • Contain: block sender domains, disable abused keys, force credential resets, isolate affected hosts.
    • Eradicate and verify: remove payloads, confirm no persistence, and require callback verification for any related request.
    • Report and prevent: record the case, update detections and the approved-tools list, share sanitized indicators with your ISAC.
