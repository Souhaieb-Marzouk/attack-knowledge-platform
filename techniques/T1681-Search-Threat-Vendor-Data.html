Search Threat Vendor Data T1681

Attackers read threat intelligence reports too. They search vendor data to learn what is published about their own campaigns, then change infrastructure and behavior fast. This page shows how it works and how defenders can stay ahead when the attacker reads their own press coverage.

Overview

This section explains the technique in plain words, why it weakens common defenses, and what this guide will teach you.

What is T1681?

T1681, called Search Threat Vendor Data, describes attackers who read threat intelligence the way you do. Threat vendors are companies that publish reports about attacks. Their data includes behavior descriptions, attack timelines, and atomic indicators, which are simple facts like file hashes, IP addresses, and domain names linked to an attack.

Attackers search this data for two reasons. First, they look for their own campaigns: what did the vendor see, which indicators are public, how close is the attribution, which means naming the group. Second, they look at campaigns by other groups that target the same industries, to copy what works and avoid what got caught. ATT&CK notes that some actors have replaced indicators mentioned in a vendor blog post in under a week, and some have searched for their own domains in vendor data and then taken those domains down to avoid seizure.

Why it matters

Many defenses lean on indicator lists. If an attacker can see the same list you can, they can rotate everything on it faster than you refresh it. A blocklist built from last month's report can be dead on arrival. This technique turns your intelligence work into their evasion work, so your detection must be about behavior, not just names and numbers.

Where it fits in the attack chain

It sits in Reconnaissance (TA0043) but serves later stages. Before an operation, the actor checks what is known and burns old infrastructure. During operations, they watch for publication that could interrupt them. After publication, they rebuild. Related techniques: Search Closed Sources (T1597), Acquire Infrastructure (T1583), and Obtain Capabilities (T1588).

Written version of the simulation (same 8 steps)

  1. Publication. A threat vendor publishes a report on a ransomware affiliate. It names two command and control (C2) domains, which are servers that infected machines contact for orders. Defender view: the report reaches your feed; you import the indicators.
  2. Attacker notices. The attacker watches vendor RSS feeds and search results. Within hours they know the report is out. Defender view: nothing visible yet; this all happens outside your estate.
  3. Self-search. The attacker searches vendor portals and passive DNS databases for their own domains and IPs, checking exactly what is exposed. Defender view: nothing visible, unless an insider does the same from your network.
  4. Rotation. The attacker registers fresh lookalike domains and moves C2 to new servers. Public reporting notes actors do this in under a week. Defender view: certificate transparency logs start showing the new names.
  5. Takedown. The attacker files abuse reports against their own exposed domain to sink or delete it, removing evidence and preventing seizure. Defender view: your tracked indicator starts returning NXDOMAIN, which means the name no longer resolves.
  6. Stale blocklist. A defender org still blocks only the old domains. The malware now beacons to the new C2 and passes the old blocklist untouched. Defender view: a dangerous false sense of coverage.
  7. ISAC update. The sector sharing group (ISAC) pushes the updated indicator set plus a behavioral note: look for DNS beaconing to young domains. Defender view: intel cycle catches up; hours matter.
  8. Behavioral catch. The SOC hunts by behavior, not by the old list: a workstation with a rare beacon pattern to a five-day-old domain is found and contained. Defender view: behavior beats indicators; the report reset failed.
3

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains why attackers read their own coverage and how they act on it. All examples are conceptual and for safe, authorized lab work only.

When and why attackers use it

Reading vendor data is cheap, legal-looking, and highly valuable. Before an operation, it tells the actor which of their tools are burned and which victims are already named in disclosures. After an operation, it tells them what to replace. Some actors also monitor reporting on rivals to steal working ideas and avoid their mistakes.

Where it applies

It applies at every maturity level. Low-skill actors read free blogs and social posts. Funded groups buy closed-source platforms and analyst access. The technique also pairs with infrastructure work: every published domain or hash becomes a to-do item for rotation or takedown.

What an attacker needs (prerequisites)

  • Free or paid access to vendor blogs, portals, and feeds.
  • A way to track their own artifacts: domain lists, hashes, infrastructure notes.
  • Ready replacement infrastructure, often pre-registered domains.
  • Patience to verify which published indicators still work.

How it is performed (conceptual steps)

  1. Subscribe to vendor RSS, mailing lists, and portal alerts; automate new-report checks.
  2. Search each report for own artifacts: domains, IPs, hashes, tool names, victim names.
  3. Query passive DNS, sandbox, and scan databases to see what else leaked.
  4. Rank what must change now: active C2 first, retired artifacts last.
  5. Rotate or take down exposed items; file abuse reports against own domains to remove evidence.
  6. Adjust tactics that the report described, so future operations look different.
lab-only-exercise.txt — measure your own exposure cycle (lab only)
# Lab exercise for defenders: time your own intel-to-detection loop.
# Use ONLY infrastructure you own, such as a lab domain you registered.

1. Register a harmless lab domain (example: mylab-detect-test.example).
2. Note the exact date your team could theoretically see it in a feed.
3. Set up certificate transparency alerts for the domain pattern.
4. Measure: how long between a change on your side and an alert on theirs?
5. Write down the gap. That gap is what an attacker exploits every week.
⚠
Danger — ethics and law

Filing false abuse reports, running takedown fraud, or probing vendor platforms with stolen access is illegal. This guide describes these behaviors so you can detect their effects, not to teach them.

Common variations

  • RSS and alert farming: automated watches on dozens of vendor sources.
  • Portal mining: searching sandbox and passive DNS databases for own artifacts.
  • Victim mining: reading breach disclosures to pick targets that are busy with response.
  • Self-takedown: abusing reporting systems to erase their own exposed evidence.

Mistakes attackers make

  • Rotating domains in patterns that certificate transparency still reveals.
  • Reusing infrastructure notes across operations that get reported separately.
  • Searching vendor portals from networks tied to their other work.
  • Trusting that a takedown removes every copy of the evidence.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

You cannot see the attacker reading the report. But the report itself, its fallout, and your own data quality are all observable. This section shows where to look and how to triage.

How defenders can see it

Watch the effects. Vendor reports arrive (ingestion logs). Published indicators die fast (DNS and CT monitoring). New lookalike infrastructure appears (certificate transparency). Insider misuse shows up in proxy logs. Each effect is a small, checkable signal. MITRE's official note (detection strategy DET0866) says the same in other words: most of this activity sits outside your visibility with a high false-positive rate, so focus on the effects you can verify.

Why it matters to you

If your detection is a list of atomic indicators, an attacker who reads that list can defeat it on schedule. Knowing this technique exists forces a better question in every design review: "What happens when the attacker reads this rule?" If the answer is "nothing happens", the rule is behavior-based.

Where to look (log inventory)

  • Threat feed ingestion logs: added, aged-out, and replaced indicators per feed.
  • Certificate transparency (CT) monitor: new certificates for your brand and for known actor patterns.
  • DNS / sinkhole logs: resolution status of tracked malicious domains.
  • Proxy logs: who inside your network reaches vendor portals and with what queries.
  • Web/WAF logs: recon bursts correlated with publication dates.
  • ISAC messages: sector-level alerts that often arrive before public reports.

Short triage flow

  1. A vendor report lands. Diff it against yesterday's set: what is new, what vanished?
  2. Check whether any named artifact is present in your last 90 days of logs.
  3. Start CT and DNS watches on the named pattern, not just the exact values.
  4. Re-check every automated block that used the old indicators.
  5. Hunt behaviors the report described, independent of the indicator list.
  6. Share your findings with your ISAC; watch for a probe spike in the next days.

Known actors and documented behavior

ATT&CK lists two procedure examples for T1681. Contagious Interview (G1052) registered accounts with threat-intelligence vendor services to check for reporting about their own infrastructure and to evaluate new potential infrastructure. UNC3886 (G1048) replaced indicators mentioned in open-source threat intelligence publications, sometimes in under a week. ATT&CK also records actors searching for their own domain names in vendor data and then taking those domains down, likely to avoid seizure or further investigation. Treat the pattern as the norm: assume every published report reaches its subject within days.

How to apply intel here

  1. Collect: ingest feeds with timestamps; log every update as an event.
  2. Analyze: diff publications; rate actor response speed; mark confidence per claim.
  3. Apply: trigger hunts after each report; refresh blocks; share back via ISAC.

Good practice

Treat a major vendor report like an event in your SIEM, not a file. When a report lands, an automatic checklist should run: diff indicators, search history, start watches, and schedule a hunt.

5.4

Logs and Patterns

For each threat: sources, key fields, normal versus suspicious, and the detection idea. Realistic event examples follow.

Threat 1 — Rapid indicator rotation

Sources: TI feed ingestion logs, CT monitor, DNS logs. Fields: indicator, feed, first_seen, last_seen, action, cert_nt, qname.

Normal: feeds add and age out a handful of indicators per day. Suspicious: a full indicator set from one actor vanishes within days of publication, and young lookalike domains appear.

Detection logic: alert on churn rate per feed and per actor set, plus CT matches for the naming pattern.

Threat 2 — Self-takedown of exposed infrastructure

Sources: DNS monitoring, sinkhole logs, whois history services.

Fields: qname, rcode, sinkhole_ip, first_seen, registrar.

Normal: tracked malicious domains stay resolvable for weeks. Suspicious: several tracked domains flip to NXDOMAIN or sinkhole right after publication.

Detection logic: status-change rule on your tracked-indicator watchlist.

Threat 3 — Victim mining from disclosures

Sources: TI feed content, web/WAF logs, mail gateway.

Fields: feed article title, org names, src_ip, uri_path, time.

Normal: baseline recon level on your public estate. Suspicious: crawl or probe spike starting days after a report names your sector or organization.

Detection logic: correlate publication dates with recon-volume change points.

Threat 4 — TTP adaptation

Sources: EDR telemetry, network analytics, detection coverage matrix.

Fields: technique IDs, detection names, hit counts over time.

Normal: steady hit rates for covered behaviors. Suspicious: a documented TTP family goes quiet right after a detailed report, then a lookalike appears.

Detection logic: coverage review triggered by every major report, not by incidents.

Threat 5 — Insider access to vendor portals

Sources: proxy logs, SSO logs.

Fields: user, src_ip, url_domain, url path and query, time.

Normal: a small analyst group uses TI platforms in work hours. Suspicious: non-analysts, night hours, queries for company or peer domains, export patterns.

Detection logic: allowlist of TI-platform users; alert on everyone else.

Threat 6 — Publication-window exploitation

Sources: TI ingestion logs, WAF logs, mail gateway.

Fields: publication timestamps, alert volume, campaign start times.

Normal: no strong link between vendor publishing days and attack starts. Suspicious: repeated pattern of operations starting right after quiet weeks.

Detection logic: raise monitoring posture for 7 days after major reports.

ti_feed.json — indicator churn in your ingestion pipeline (Threat 1)
{"time":"2026-10-13T06:15:00Z","feed":"vendorX_c2","action":"bulk_ageout",
"count":38,"reason":"stale","actors":["tracked-affiliate-Y"],
"note":"all 38 indicators first_seen 2026-08-30"}

How to read it: the vendor aged out the entire old set and added a new set registered days after the report. action and count give you the churn rate; the note field shows the registration gap that matches the "under a week" behavior.

certstream.json — young certificate matching the actor pattern (Threat 1)
{"time":"2026-10-13T09:02:11Z","update_type":"NewEntry",
"leaf_cert":{"subject":"CN=portal-cdn-sync.com",
"san":["portal-cdn-sync.com","mail-verify.portal-cdn-sync.com"],
"not_before":"2026-10-13T00:00:00Z"},
"known_pattern":"*-cdn-sync[.]com (vendor report, 2026-10-10)"}

How to read it: the certificate transparency feed shows a new certificate whose name matches the pattern the vendor published three days earlier. not_before proves the infrastructure is young. This is exactly the rotation signal, visible to anyone who watches CT logs.

dns.log — tracked indicator domains going dark (Threat 2)
2026-10-14 02:11:04 qname=sync-hub-eu.com rcode=NXDOMAIN resolver=10.10.1.53 client=monitor
2026-10-14 02:11:09 qname=cdn-sync-eu.com rcode=NXDOMAIN resolver=10.10.1.53 client=monitor

How to read it: two tracked C2 names stop resolving (NXDOMAIN) and one now answers with a sinkhole address, a server operated by defenders or registrars to absorb malicious traffic. After publication, this flip pattern is the self-takedown fingerprint.

How to read it: the user is a temporary contractor outside the analyst allowlist, the hour is off-hours, and the query string targets your own domain on a vendor platform. Any one field alone is weak; together they demand a look.

Rule pair 1 — Threat feed indicator churn spike (Threat 1)

sigma/ti_2026_01_feed_churn_spike.yml
title: Threat Feed Indicator Churn Spike
id: 91a4c2d7-3e58-4f61-9b2a-8c4d5e6f7a11
status: stable
description: |
  Detects an unusually large add or age-out event in threat feed
  ingestion. Actor-driven rotation after publication often shows up as a
  bulk age-out followed by a bulk add for the same actor within days.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1681/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-28
logsource:
  category: network
  product: threat_feed
detection:
  selection_bulk:
    action:
      - add
      - bulk_ageout
    count|gte: 20
  condition: selection_bulk
fields:
  - feed
  - action
  - count
  - actors
  - note
falsepositives:
  - Vendor-side maintenance that re-baselines a feed
  - New feed onboarding with a large initial import
level: high
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1681
splunk — matching SPL (assumes a JSON threat-feed pipeline)
index=intel sourcetype=ti_feed:json (action="add" OR action="bulk_ageout") count>=20
| stats count AS bulk_updates sum(count) AS moved_indicators values(actors) AS actors BY feed
| sort - moved_indicators

Field assumptions: your TIP or ingestion script emits feed, action, count, actors, and note per update. This is a custom-but-common pipeline; the rule is worthless without event-ized feed updates.

False positives and tuning: onboarding a new feed trips this once; suppress with an expiry. Track events for two weeks, then set the count threshold near three times your median bulk size.

Rule pair 2 — New certificate matching a published pattern (Threat 1)

sigma/ti_2026_02_ct_pattern_match.yml
title: New Certificate Matching Published Actor Pattern
id: a2b5d3e8-4f69-4a72-8c3b-9d5e6f7a8b22
status: test
description: |
  Detects certificate transparency entries whose names match a wildcard
  pattern published in vendor reporting or your internal infrastructure
  notes. Attackers rotate domains, but CT logs still expose the names.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1681/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-28
logsource:
  category: network
  product: certstream
detection:
  selection_new:
    update_type: NewEntry
  selection_pattern:
    leaf_cert.subject|endswith:
      - '-cdn-sync.com'
      - '-hub-sync.com'
  condition: selection_new and selection_pattern
fields:
  - leaf_cert.subject
  - leaf_cert.san
  - not_before
falsepositives:
  - Legitimate companies whose real product names match the pattern
level: high
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1681
  - attack.t1583.001
splunk — matching SPL (certstream JSON feed)
index=intel sourcetype=certstream:json update_type="NewEntry"
    (leaf_cert.subject IN ("*-cdn-sync.com","*-hub-sync.com"))
| table _time leaf_cert.subject leaf_cert.san{} not_before
| rename leaf_cert.subject AS cert_subject, leaf_cert.san{} AS san
| lookup actor_patterns pattern AS cert_subject OUTPUT actor_name
| eval actor_name=coalesce(actor_name, "(new pattern)")

Field assumptions: certstream:json exposes update_type, leaf_cert.subject, leaf_cert.san, and not_before. The actor_patterns lookup only enriches the output with actor names; the rule matches on the pattern itself. Maintain the lookup with your intel team.

False positives and tuning: keep the pattern list tight; short generic tokens will match real businesses. Require a second hit from the same pattern family before paging, and always check not_before age.

      - REFUSED

splunk — matching SPL (Zeek/Corelight DNS feed)
index=dns sourcetype=corelight_dns client="monitor"
    qname IN ("*-cdn-sync.com","*-hub-sync.com") (rcode="NXDOMAIN" OR rcode="REFUSED" OR answer="198.51.100.7")
| bin _time span=1h
| stats dc(qname) AS changed_domains values(qname) AS qnames values(rcode) AS rcodes BY _time
| where changed_domains >= 3
| sort - _time

Field assumptions: a monitor host queries every watchlist domain every 15 minutes, so status changes are deliberate events. 198.51.100.7 stands for your known sinkhole address; replace it with your value in both the Sigma rule and the SPL.

False positives and tuning: registrar errors cause single flips; require three or more in an hour. Keep the watchlist and the published-report list in the same lookup to avoid drift.

title: Detection Coverage Drop After Major Vendor Report

status: test
description: |

date: 2026-09-28

  category: network
  product: detection_metrics
detection:
  selection_scope:
    metric: family_hit_count
  selection_drop:
    drop_pct|gte: 80
  condition: selection_scope and selection_drop
fields:
  - family
  - actor_link
  - week
  - hits_this_week
  - hits_last_week
  - drop_pct
falsepositives:
  - Genuine end of a campaign
  - Detection engine maintenance windows
level: low

  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1681
splunk — matching SPL (detection metrics export)
index=metrics sourcetype=det_cover:json metric="family_hit_count"
| bin _time span=7d
| stats sum(hits) AS hits BY _time family actor_link
| streamstats window=1 current=f last(hits) AS prev_hits BY family
| where isnotnull(prev_hits)
| eval drop_pct=round(100*(prev_hits-hits)/max(prev_hits,1),1)
| where drop_pct >= 80
| table _time family actor_link prev_hits hits drop_pct

Field assumptions: you export weekly hit counts per detection family (most SOAR or metrics tooling can). actor_link ties the family to the tracked actor from the vendor report. The Sigma rule expects a ready-made drop_pct field; the SPL shows how to compute it from weekly hits if your export does not include it, for example in a scheduled search that writes the result back to the metrics index.

False positives and tuning: campaigns do end; treat this as a review trigger, not an incident. Pair it with the report calendar so the review asks: did the report explain the drop?

Real-World Case Study

A composite case shows what happens when an actor resets infrastructure faster than a defender refreshes a blocklist, and how behavioral detection ends the story.

✎
About this case study

This is a composite case built from documented behaviors, including the ATT&CK-noted pattern of actors replacing published indicators within a week and taking down their own exposed domains. Names and numbers are invented.

Context

Organization: "Beacon Regional Health", a 1,400-staff hospital network (fictional). It blocks malware C2 addresses with a curated indicator list, refreshed monthly by hand. Threat actor: "Crimson Vault", a ransomware affiliate tracked by vendors for months.

Timeline

  • Day 0 — Report published. A vendor names two Crimson Vault C2 domains and a loader hash. Beacon imports them into its blocklist the same day.
  • Day 0–1 — Attacker reads. Crimson Vault's watch script flags the report. The operator queries a passive DNS portal for their own domains and sees the coverage.
  • Day 2–4 — Rotation. The operator registers four new lookalike domains, moves C2, and stops using the old names. The loader hash changes after a small rebuild.
  • Day 5 — Self-takedown. The operator files abuse reports against their own exposed domains. Two go NXDOMAIN; one resolves to a sinkhole.
  • Day 9 — Quiet return. Crimson Vault scans the sector again. Beacon's blocklist still contains only the dead names.
  • Day 11 — Access attempt. A phishing mail with the new loader reaches three Beacon staff. The mail gateway, tuned on the old hash, passes it.
  • Day 12 — Behavioral catch. Beacon's DNS analytics flag a workstation beaconing every 60 seconds to a domain registered six days earlier. The new Sigma-style DNS rule, deployed after the ISAC alert, fires.
  • Day 12–18 — Response. The host is isolated, the loader is found and blocked, credentials are reset, and the blocklist process is automated with feed-churn monitoring.

Attack chain

  1. Monitor vendor reporting for own indicators (T1681).
  2. Rotate C2 infrastructure and loader artifacts (T1583 support).
  3. Take down own exposed domains to erase evidence.
  4. Re-enter the sector with fresh artifacts during the report-free window.
  5. Deliver new loader via phishing; prepare ransomware deployment.

Detection point

Three chances existed. First, the feed churn and CT entries on days 2–5, missed because ingestion was not event-ized. Second, the dead-indicator signal on day 5, missed because nobody watched the watchlist. Third, the behavioral DNS beacon on day 12, which fired and ended the intrusion before encryption. The lesson is not that intel failed; it is that static use of intel fails.

Containment

Isolated the workstation at 09:40, disabled its local admin token, and blocked the young C2 domain at the resolver for all sites within 20 minutes.

Eradication

Removed the loader and its persistence, reset the user's credentials and tokens, and re-imaged the host. The old-hash blocklist entries were retired with an audit note.

Recovery

Returned the host to service, automated blocklist refresh from the vendor feed, deployed the feed-churn and CT rules from section 5.5, and joined the health ISAC channel.

Impact and consequences

No encryption occurred and no patient data was touched. Costs: about 90 staff-hours, one re-image, and an emergency blocklist automation sprint. The near miss changed how Beacon funds intelligence work.

Team response and communication

The SOC lead briefed the CISO within two hours and sent a hospital-wide notice the same day, framed as "controls worked; we are upgrading them". The intel analyst wrote a one-page post-mortem that the ISAC published anonymously, which helped two peer hospitals fix the same gap.

Lessons learned

  • Atomic indicators age in days; every list needs an aging and refresh policy.
  • Feed updates must become SIEM events, or churn signals are invisible.
  • Watch your watchlist: resolution status changes are free detection.
  • Certificate transparency exposes rotated infrastructure quickly.
  • Behavioral analytics carry you when names change; fund them first.
  • Share outcomes through an ISAC; your lessons protect your peers and raise sector baselines.

Mitigation and Defense

Intelligence, network, identity, and response controls that keep working even when the attacker reads everything you read.

MITRE lists M1056 Pre-compromise as the mitigation class. Applied here, it means: assume the attacker reads what you read, and design intelligence and detection so that being read does not equal being beaten.

Intelligence operations

  • Age-out policy for atomic indicators with owner and review date.
  • Event-ize every feed update into the SIEM.
  • Post-report checklist: diff, history search, hunts, watch refresh.

Network and DNS

  • Sinkhole and watch your tracked-indicator domains.
  • Monitor certificate transparency for your brand and actor patterns.
  • Flag beacons to young domains from managed endpoints.

Detection engineering

  • Pair every indicator rule with a behavior rule for the same TTP.
  • Track detection-family hit counts weekly.
  • Test: "if the attacker rotates names today, what still fires?"

Identity and access

  • Restrict vendor portal accounts to named analysts.
  • Alert on off-hours TI platform access.
  • Review contractor access to intel tooling quarterly.

Logging and monitoring

  • Centralize proxy, DNS, web, and feed logs with time sync.
  • Keep 90+ days searchable for report-to-history searches.
  • Raise posture for 7 days after major public reports.

Response and sharing

  • Playbook: "new vendor report on an actor in our sector".
  • Join and contribute to your ISAC.
  • After incidents, share anonymized lessons back.

Home and small office

  • Use security software that updates protections automatically; that is your feed freshness policy.
  • Do not rely on one "bad list" you found online; enable the behavioral shield in your antivirus and keep it on.
  • Register a business domain watch (free CT alert services exist) if your name is easy to imitate.
  • If your sector has a sharing group, join it; small teams benefit the most.
