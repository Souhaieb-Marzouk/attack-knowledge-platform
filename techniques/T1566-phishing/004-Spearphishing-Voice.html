T1566.004 Spearphishing Voice

Spearphishing Voice — usually called vishing — is a targeted attack that arrives as a phone call. The attacker pretends to be IT support, a bank, or an executive and uses urgency to reach the goal: a password, a one-time code, an MFA approval, or the installation of remote-access software. This guide explains the attack and shows how to detect and stop it.

    Overview

    Voice attacks skip mail filters entirely. This section explains what vishing is, why a ten-minute call can beat a million-dollar security stack, and where the technique sits in the attack chain.

    What is this technique?

    In a spearphishing voice attack, the attacker calls the target and plays a role: IT help desk, bank fraud department, Microsoft support, or a senior executive. The caller ID may look internal or local, and the story creates pressure — a fraud attempt in progress, an expired password, an executive waiting for a file.

    The goal is concrete. Usually it is one of four things: the victim reads out an MFA one-time code, approves a push notification, confirms information the attacker needs to pass identity verification elsewhere, or installs remote-access software so the attacker can control the machine directly. In the most damaging cases the call goes to the IT help desk itself, tricking staff into resetting MFA for a “locked-out executive.”

    Why it matters

    Email defenses have improved, so attackers moved to a channel with almost no logging and no filtering: the phone. Voice adds tools email never had — live conversation, tone, immediate answers to objections — and caller ID is trivially spoofed, so “it came from our number” proves nothing.

    The technique defeats even phishing-resistant habits when aimed at people who help others: help desk agents are trained to be useful, and a confident caller with the right details can turn that helpfulness into an MFA reset. This is exactly how several major intrusions of recent years began.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Spearphishing Voice is an Initial Access technique. After a successful call, attackers commonly:

    • Log in with reset credentials or approved MFA (T1078)
    • Enroll their own MFA device (T1098.005)
    • Control the endpoint through a remote tool (T1219)
    • Harvest credentials from the session (T1555)
    • Deploy ransomware or extort the organization directly

    The same attack, in words

    1Research the target

    The attacker collects names, job titles, phone numbers, and recent news from professional networks, breach dumps, and the company website. They pick a target whose role makes the story plausible — an accountant for payroll, an assistant for executive mail — and write a short script with answers for likely objections.

    2Spoof the caller ID

    Using a VoIP service, the attacker sets the displayed number to the company’s real help desk line or a local number. When the phone rings and shows an internal number, the victim’s guard drops. The SOC often has no record that anything unusual happened, because caller ID is not verified end to end.

    3Run the story

    “Hi, this is Tom from IT. We’re seeing failed logins on your account — someone is trying to break in. I need to verify you quickly.” Urgency plus authority plus a helpful reason. The victim, wanting to be safe, cooperates. Skilled callers sound busy, use internal jargon, and never argue.

    4Harvest the MFA event

    The attacker triggers a real login or password reset. The victim’s phone shows a push notification or a code arrives by SMS. “Yes, that’s us confirming — please approve it / read me the code.” The victim approves or reads it aloud. The identity provider logs a perfectly normal successful authentication — performed by the attacker.

    5Install the remote tool

    For deeper access, the caller walks the victim to a website from the call and asks them to install a “support tool.” A remote-access program such as AnyDesk or a ScreenConnect-style agent lands on the workstation, started by the user, with administrative rights granted by the user.

    6Take over the machine

    The attacker connects to the running remote tool and now sees the victim’s screen. They browse files, dump saved credentials, and read the victim’s screen for internal names and systems. The endpoint starts talking to the attacker’s relay infrastructure.

    7Make the access permanent

    The attacker calls the help desk — the same trick, one level up — and asks for an MFA reset for an “executive with a broken phone,” or simply enrolls a new MFA device on the compromised account. Once their own factor is registered, the stolen access survives password changes.

    8Defender responds

    The SOC joins the signals: MFA approval during a call window, a remote tool installed from a link, a help desk reset without proper verification. They isolate the host, revoke tokens and MFA factors, block the remote-tool relays, and change the help desk procedure so every reset requires verification the attacker cannot fake in a call.

    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    Voice attacks are performed by people, not malware. Understanding the caller’s playbook — research, spoofing, scripting, and escalation — shows defenders exactly where to build friction. All content here is for defense and education.

    Ethics and law

    This section describes attacker behavior so defenders can recognize it. Impersonating organizations on phone calls, spoofing caller ID, and obtaining access by deception are crimes in most jurisdictions. Everything here must stay inside authorized lab and awareness exercises.

    When and why attackers choose the phone

    The telephone has three properties email lacks. First, no telemetry: most organizations log mail, web, and identity, but not who called whom and why. Second, live persuasion: the caller can answer objections in real time, adjust the story, and apply pressure exactly when the victim hesitates. Third, a human door: help desks exist to solve access problems quickly, and a confident caller with the right background details can turn that helpfulness into an MFA reset. When mail-based phishing gets harder, vishing returns as the cheap, reliable alternative.

    How the attack is performed

    Preparation comes first: names, roles, direct numbers, tool names, and recent events, gathered from professional networks and breach data. The caller then uses a VoIP service that allows arbitrary caller ID, often choosing the company’s own help desk number. During the call they blend authority (“IT”, “fraud department”), urgency (“account is being attacked right now”), and a helpful frame (“I just need to verify you”). The ask is kept small: approve a prompt, read a code, visit one site, install one tool. Skilled operators chain calls: the first call harvests details, the second, citing them, unlocks the help desk.

    Prerequisites and conditions

    • Target profile: a person whose role makes the story fit — finance, assistants, or the help desk itself.
    • Phone reach: a direct number or a directory guess; VoIP setup with spoofed caller ID costs little.
    • Background details: enough internal knowledge (tool names, ticket style, manager names) to pass casual verification.
    • A concrete ask: MFA approval, code, reset, or tool installation — defined before the phone rings.
    • Follow-through ready: stolen session or tool must be used fast, while the story is still fresh.

    Practical lab exercise (lab-only, safe)

    You can rehearse the detection side without calling anyone: build the identity and endpoint telemetry a vishing attack would produce, then test your alerts against it.

    The lab value is the correlation, not the events: every field name you confirm here makes your production Sigma and SPL correct the first time. For awareness training, use your approved phishing-simulation platform’s voice module or scripted role-play with consent forms — never real calls to real staff.

    Common variations

    • Help desk level-up: same script aimed at IT staff to reset MFA for a “locked-out” VIP.
    • Battery-drain pressure: repeated MFA pushes, then a call offering to “stop the alerts.”
    • Voicemail drop: urgent callback requests that start the call on the attacker’s terms.
    • Callback phishing (Luna Moth style): a fake invoice or subscription email says “call this number,” blending mail and voice; the voice call then walks the victim to a fake page or remote tool.
    • AI voice cloning: a short executive voice sample cloned to add authority to the script.
    • Two-team operations: one caller extracts data, a second uses it minutes later while trust is warm.

    Common attacker mistakes

    • Details that fail a callback: wrong manager, wrong tool, wrong recent event.
    • Refusing any verification step, which trained staff will notice.
    • Asking for everything in one call instead of chaining small asks.
    • Relay infrastructure that your proxy can see and block by domain.
    • Reusing the same voice and script across targets, which awareness teams learn to spot.

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    The call itself is nearly invisible, but everything the caller achieves leaves records: MFA approvals, factor enrollments, tool installs, relay connections, and help desk tickets. The SOC’s job is to make those records joinable.

    How defenders can see this attack

    Vishing is detected after the voice channel, in the systems the caller was actually aiming at. A successful attack produces a signature sequence: an identity event (approval or reset), an endpoint event (remote tool), and a process event (help desk ticket). Each alone can be innocent; the sequence is the attack. This is why phone-channel incidents are won or lost on log coverage and correlation, not on per-user suspicion.

    Artifacts the attack leaves

    • Identity: MFA approvals at odd hours, new factor enrollments, password or MFA resets, first logins from new devices.
    • Endpoint: installation and first run of remote-access software, new services (Event ID 7045), relay-domain connections.
    • Help desk: a ticket with the reset, often missing verification notes; caller recordings where retention exists.
    • Phone: call detail records on your trunks — timestamps, duration, numbers — even if spoofed at the far end.
    • Human: the victim’s memory of the call, which fast reporting can still turn into evidence.

    Where to look, in order

    1. Identity logs: approvals, enrollments, resets, and their timestamps.
    2. Endpoint telemetry: remote-tool processes, services, and network connections.
    3. Help desk ticketing: who requested what, with which verification evidence.
    4. Phone system records: correlate call windows with identity events.
    5. Proxy logs: relay domains and the download source the victim was sent to.

    Why it matters for the SOC

    Vishing converts the most trusted humans in the company — help desk staff — into an attack tool, and it reliably precedes the most expensive outcomes: full identity takeover and ransomware. The cost of missing it is measured in days of downtime. The cost of catching it early is one revoked token and one coaching conversation. That asymmetry is the business case for the detections in this guide.

    Short triage flow

    1. Anchor on the identity event: which MFA approval, enrollment, or reset started this? Get user, time, and source.
    2. Check the endpoint: any remote tool installed or connected around that time? Which relay domain?
    3. Review the help desk ticket: was verification performed and recorded? Who approved the reset?
    4. Contain fast: disable the account, revoke sessions and tokens, remove attacker MFA factors, isolate the host.
    5. Close the gap: interview the involved staff supportively, correct the verification procedure, and add the missed detection.

    Common triage trap

    Do not treat the victim as the failure. Shame slows reporting, and slow reporting extends attacker dwell time. Praise the report, fix the procedure, and focus the postmortem on the verification gap the caller exploited.

    5.2Threat intelligence

    For vishing, intelligence tracks crews, scripts, and the remote tools they prefer. Sources stay the same as for other techniques; the lens changes to who calls, what they say, and what they ask for.

    MITRE ATT&CK

    T1566.004 anchors the model; pair with T1098.005 (device registration persistence), T1219 (remote access software), and T1621 (MFA request generation). Confidence: High.

    Vendor reporting

    Microsoft, Mandiant, and CrowdStrike publish vishing crew tradecraft: scripts, spoofing setups, and target sectors. Confidence: High for behavior, Medium for attribution.

    OSINT

    Recorded scam calls, help desk verification discussions, and remote-tool abuse reports show live scripts. Confidence: Medium–High for tactics, Low for scale.

    Commercial feeds

    Telephony reputation and spoofing analytics flag high-risk inbound calls before they connect. Confidence: Medium; useful as a signal, not a block.

    ISACs

    Sector groups circulate active scripts and target roles (“payroll vishing wave this week”). Confidence: High for sector relevance.

    Internal telemetry

    Your help desk tickets, MFA enrollment logs, and staff reports show which scripts already reach your people. Confidence: High; review monthly.

    5.4Logs and patterns

    Vishing detection lives in identity logs, endpoint telemetry, help desk records, and phone metadata. The examples below are representative but realistic; confirm field names against your own vendors before coding.

    Threat 1 — Help desk MFA/credential reset via social engineering

    Sources: identity provider audit logs (Okta system log, Entra audit), help desk ticketing system, SIEM correlation.

    Key fields: Okta eventtype (user.mfa.factor.reset_all, user.mfa.factor.activate), Entra operationName (security-info registration), ticket requester, verification_method, operator ID, timestamps.

    Normal: resets happen in business hours, from the corporate network, with a ticket that records verification (video, manager confirmation, callback to a known number).

    Suspicious: resets without recorded verification; VIP or executive accounts reset outside hours; the same operator performing several resets in a short window; reset followed within minutes by sign-in from a new device.

    Detection logic: alert on every MFA factor enrollment and reset for privileged users, and correlate any reset with the next successful sign-in: a new device within 60 minutes is an incident, not a coincidence.

    Field notes: client.device: Unknown plus a foreign IP at 21:14 local time is the pattern; the target entry shows whose factor was changed — here an executive, not the “actor” field’s nominal user, which is exactly what attacker-driven enrollments look like when performed through a taken-over session.

    Threat 2 — Remote-access tool installation by voice lure

    Sources: Windows system event log (service installation), Sysmon process events, EDR alerts, proxy logs for relay domains.

    Key fields: Event ID 7045 ServiceName, ServiceFileName; process Image, ParentImage; proxy cs_host for relay domains.

    Normal: approved remote tools from the managed software catalog, installed by deployment tooling, connecting to named corporate relays.

    Suspicious: user-initiated installs of first-seen remote tools; services created outside change windows; connections to relay domains not on the corporate allowlist.

    Detection logic: alert on service creation or first execution of any remote-access software not in the approved list, and join to the user’s recent identity events.

    Field notes: StartType: auto start means the tool survives reboots; ImagePath under the user profile — not Program Files — indicates a user-initiated install rather than managed deployment; Origin: user session distinguishes this from software distribution. Any of these three alone is worth a look; together they describe a vishing aftermath.

    Threat 3 — MFA approval harvested during the call

    Sources: identity provider sign-in logs, MFA provider logs, SIEM.

    Key fields: approval timestamp, triggering authentication attempt, geolocation and ASN of both, approval latency.

    Normal: approvals follow the user’s own authentication attempt within seconds, from the same device context.

    Suspicious: approval granted while the user is visibly on a call (PBX correlation); approval following an authentication attempt from a different country; repeated push approvals at unusual hours.

    Detection logic: flag approvals whose authentication attempt came from a new ASN or country, and any approval for an account that receives a factor enrollment within the next hour.

    Threat 4 — Executive voice fraud and deepfake BEC

    Sources: PBX/call recordings (where lawful and retained), finance workflow systems, DLP, mail logs for follow-up messages.

    Key fields: caller ID and trunk data, request amount and beneficiary in payment systems, approval chains bypassed.

    Normal: payment changes and urgent transfers go through dual approval on known contact details.

    Suspicious: urgent transfer requests that refuse callbacks, pressure staff to skip dual approval, or arrive alongside a cloned-voice call “confirming” an email.

    Detection logic: procedural: require out-of-band confirmation on a known number for every payment change; log refusals of callbacks as a reportable indicator to security.

    Field notes: the CDR shows a UK-terminated trunk presenting a US internal number — a spoof signature. The second line is the punchline of the whole technique: the identity event lands two minutes after the call ends. Correlating CDR windows with identity events is the highest-value vishing detection a SOC can build.

    Threat 5 — Personal-account vishing spilling into work

    Sources: MDM/UEM signals, MTD alerts, identity logs (password reuse indicators), staff self-reports.

    Key fields: device risk score, personal app installs, reused-password telemetry from breached-data checks.

    Normal: corporate MFA on corporate apps only; unique passwords verified by periodic breach-data checks.

    Suspicious: a personal bank-scam report from an employee whose work account shares the same password or phone number used for MFA.

    Detection logic: procedural: when staff report personal scams, offer a no-fault credential check on matching work secrets and, where supported, re-enroll MFA into a phishing-resistant factor.

    Threat 6 — Reconnaissance and voicemail drops

    Sources: PBX call logs, voicemail system logs, staff reports.

    Key fields: short-duration inbound calls, repeated missed calls from rotating numbers, voicemail topics.

    Normal: low volume of genuine short calls; standard voicemail traffic.

    Suspicious: bursts of very short inbound calls across many extensions, callback requests claiming “IT security,” numbers that rotate daily.

    Detection logic: aggregate short-call patterns by day and number reputation; treat clusters as campaign indicators and brief the help desk with the numbers.

    Coverage tip

    Threats 1–3 generate buildable alerts this week; threat 4 is mostly procedural but catastrophic when missed; threats 5–6 enrich context and feed awareness. Start with the identity correlation, then grow.

    5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    Each rule covers one ranked threat. Sigma YAML is vendor-neutral; the SPL mirrors the logic and states its field assumptions. Adapt indexes and sourcetypes to your environment.

    Rule 1 — Remote access tool process started (Threat 2)

    Field assumptions: Sysmon process events; on EDR platforms map to their process telemetry. approved_remote_tools is a lookup your IT team maintains. Tuning: page on user-session starts for unapproved tools; auto-close for known deployments. False positives: legitimate support sessions — require a ticket reference in the allowlist entry.

    Rule 2 — Remote access tool service installed (Threat 2, persistence)

    Field assumptions: Windows Security channel events via WinEventLog:System (service control manager writes 7045 there). Tuning: join with the approved-tools lookup; alert at high for user-profile paths. False positives: IT deployments — filter by ImagePath under managed directories.

    Rule 3 — Okta MFA factor enrollment or reset (Threat 1/3)

    Field assumptions: Okta system log via Okta:IMX. The query reads one merged stream of factor changes and successful sign-ins, sorted per user: streamstats carries the latest factor-change time forward, and the where keeps only sign-ins that happen within 60 minutes after a factor change — the reset-then-login sequence this technique produces. Tuning: page at high for privileged roles, review queue for standard users within business hours. False positives: new-phone days — resolve by device compliance status.

    Rule 4 — Microsoft Entra security info registration (Threat 1, Entra variant)

    Field assumptions: Entra audit logs via the Azure Monitor pipeline (azure:aad:audit); operationName is parsed with spath so add-on differences do not break the query. Tuning: suppress planned rollouts by change window; correlate with the user’s next sign-in for instant triage. False positives: device upgrades; verify IP and compliance state.

    Rule 5 — Connection to remote-access relay domains (Threat 2/6)

    Field assumptions: Sysmon Event ID 3 network events require DNS name resolution enabled (or use destination IPs with a domain lookup); the proxy join enriches with the user identity. Tuning: medium severity for approved-tool relays, high for first-seen hosts. False positives: IT support usage — the allowlist entry must name the owning team.

    Validation checklist for all five rules

    Generate the lab telemetry from Section 3, replay it through each rule, run the Sigma YAML through your pipeline (sigma-cli check), measure two weeks of precision before paging, and record owner, runbook, and expected volume in the detection catalog.

    Real-World Case Study

    In September 2023, MGM Resorts International — one of the largest hospitality companies in the world — suffered a cyberattack that reportedly began with a single phone call to its IT help desk. The case is public, extensively reported, and the clearest demonstration of T1566.004’s power.

    Context

    The attackers, tracked as Scattered Spider (UNC3944) and working with the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware operation, researched an MGM employee through public sources, then called the IT help desk while impersonating that employee. They provided enough personal details to pass verification and convinced the help desk to reset the employee’s MFA enrollment. With a fresh MFA factor in hand, the attackers logged into the corporate network. Reported, widely cited accounts describe the decisive call as lasting about ten minutes.

    Timeline

    • Before September 8, 2023The crew researches target employees, gathers identity details usable for help desk verification, and prepares remote-access and intrusion tooling.
    • ~September 8, 2023The vishing call succeeds; help desk resets MFA; attackers authenticate as the employee and begin moving through identity and network systems, including administrator privileges.
    • September 10–11, 2023Attackers reach server and virtualization infrastructure; ransomware deploys. MGM takes systems offline — hotel keys, casino floors, reservations, and websites are disrupted nationwide.
    • September 11–14, 2023MGM confirms the cyberattack in an SEC filing; manual operations continue while teams investigate and restore.
    • Mid-to-late September 2023Systems are progressively restored; MGM later reports roughly 100 million dollars of impact in the quarter, largely from disruption and response costs.
    • AftermathThe same crew attacks other hospitality and retail targets; the incident becomes the reference case for help desk verification hardening.

    Attack chain mapped to ATT&CK

    • Research: employee identity details from public and leaked sources.
    • Delivery: T1566.004 — voice call to the help desk impersonating an employee.
    • Persistence: T1098.005 — MFA reset and re-enrollment under attacker control.
    • Valid accounts: T1078 — access with the employee’s reset identity; privilege escalation to admin roles.
    • Impact: T1486 — ransomware deployment affecting core business systems.

    Detection point

    The decisive moments were procedural, not technical: a reset performed with verification the attacker could satisfy, and identity events (reset, enrollment, new sign-in) that were not joined into an alert at the time. The strongest technical signals existed — new MFA factor for an executive-tier identity, new device sign-ins, unusual access to virtualization management — but arrived as separate quiet logs instead of one loud correlation.

    Containment

    MGM took large parts of its environment offline to stop the spread — a costly but effective decision. Accounts were locked, sessions revoked, and the identity layer reset while teams mapped attacker access.

    Eradication and recovery

    Systems were rebuilt and restored in stages over days; operations ran manually during recovery. Third-party forensics supported scoping; the company rebuilt trust with guests through continuous public updates.

    Impact

    Roughly 100 million dollars of quarterly impact, legal and regulatory follow-up, and national headlines. The attack began with a phone call, not a zero-day — which is the core lesson.

    Team response and communication

    MGM’s SEC disclosure, guest communication, and staged recovery showed mature crisis management. Across the industry, the case triggered immediate procedure reviews: identity re-verification for resets, dual-control for privileged recovery, and better monitoring of identity events. In the same weeks the same crew hit other hospitality and retail targets with very different outcomes depending on verification strength and segmentation — a rare, clear comparison of defenses under the same threat.

    Lessons learned

    • The help desk is a security boundary. It must verify like one: callback on a known number, video or manager confirmation for high-impact resets, and tickets that record the evidence.
    • MFA is a lifecycle, not a checkbox. Enrollment and reset events need the same monitoring as logins — they are the attacker’s persistence step.
    • Correlation beats collection. The signals existed; nobody joined them in minutes. Build the reset-plus-sign-in correlation before you need it.
    • Privileged access needs dual control. Admin and virtualization systems should require two people and two factors for sensitive actions.
    • Practice the phone channel. Awareness programs that only test email leave the human door unguarded.
    7

    Mitigation and Defense

    Voice attacks are stopped by procedure as much as by technology: verify humans strongly, watch identity changes, and make remote tools a policy decision instead of a user habit.

    Identity and help desk

    • Write a verification SOP: callback on a known number, video confirmation or manager sign-off for MFA resets, and recorded evidence in every ticket.
    • Dual-control resets for privileged and executive accounts.
    • Alert on every MFA enrollment and reset; treat new-factor-plus-new-sign-in as an incident.
    • Prefer phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/passkeys) and number matching.

    Endpoint and network

    • Allowlist remote tools: block user-installed AnyDesk, TeamViewer, RustDesk, ScreenConnect, and similar by policy.
    • Alert on service installation (7045) for remote software and on relay-domain connections.
    • Keep EDR coverage on every endpoint, including servers used by administrators.
    • Block first-seen remote-tool relays at the proxy until reviewed.

    Telephony and process

    • Log call detail records and retain them for correlation; flag high spoof scores on your trunks.
    • Publish an internal rule: IT never asks for codes; hang up and call back on the official number.
    • Run vishing simulations against the help desk with consent and coaching, not blame.
    • Add voice fraud procedures for finance: dual approval and callback for payment changes.

    Monitoring and response

    • Ship identity, endpoint, and help desk logs to the SIEM with the Section 5 detections live.
    • Write a vishing playbook: contain identity first, then endpoint, then interview supportively.
    • Tabletop the scenario quarterly: “executive locked out, reset requested by phone.”

    Home and small-office advice

    The same psychology targets everyone. Let unknown calls go to voicemail — real institutions accept callbacks on their official numbers, which you dial yourself. Never read a one-time code to anyone, including “the bank” and “Microsoft support”; no legitimate service asks for it. If a caller says your account is under attack, hang up and call the institution on the number from your card or official app. Do not install remote-support software for strangers, no matter how professional the caller sounds. Use unique passwords and passkeys, keep the operating system updated, and if you realize mid-call that something is wrong, it is always acceptable to say: “I will call you back” and end the conversation.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    Twelve failure modes teams meet with voice-channel attacks. Each card gives symptom, root cause, fix, and prevention.

    1. Help desk resets without verification

    Symptom
    Any confident caller can get an MFA reset.
    Root cause
    Speed metrics outweigh verification steps.
    Fix
    Mandatory callback on a known number plus recorded evidence per reset.
    Prevention
    Mystery-caller tests with the SOP, scored quarterly.

    2. Trusting caller ID

    Symptom
    Staff accept internal-looking numbers as proof.
    Root cause
    Nobody explained spoofing is trivial.
    Fix
    Training plus PBX flags for off-trunk calls.
    Prevention
    Vishing module in onboarding for all staff.

    3. No identity-event monitoring

    Symptom
    Reset and enrollment logs are never alerted on.
    Root cause
    Detection focus stays on logins only.
    Fix
    Deploy Rules 3–4 and the reset-plus-signin correlation.
    Prevention
    Coverage matrix includes IA lifecycle events.

    4. Remote tools allowed everywhere

    Symptom
    Any user can install AnyDesk unchallenged.
    Root cause
    No application policy for remote software.
    Fix
    Allowlist + EDR rules + 7045 alerts.
    Prevention
    Software policy review each quarter.

    5. MFA enrollment alerts ignored

    Symptom
    Enrollment events land in a quiet queue nobody reads.
    Root cause
    Considered routine noise.
    Fix
    Severity by role: page for privileged users.
    Prevention
    Weekly review of queue health and volumes.

    6. No VIP protection tier

    Symptom
    Executives follow the same weak verification as everyone.
    Root cause
    No role-based identity policy.
    Fix
    Dual-control resets, hardware keys, and stricter alerts for VIPs.
    Prevention
    Privileged access review including identity lifecycle.

    7. Awareness tests email only

    Symptom
    Staff spot fake mails but fall for calls.
    Root cause
    Program never rehearsed voice scenarios.
    Fix
    Add vishing simulations and help desk role-play.
    Prevention
    Annual curriculum covering all channels.

    8. No callback culture

    Symptom
    Urgent requests bypass verification because “the boss is waiting.”
    Root cause
    Callback seen as rude or slow.
    Fix
    Leadership-endorsed rule: urgency never waives verification.
    Prevention
    Executives model callback behavior publicly.

    9. Phone logs not retained

    Symptom
    Cannot correlate the call window with identity events.
    Root cause
    CDRs discarded or never ingested.
    Fix
    Retain CDRs to the SIEM; add spoof-score flags.
    Prevention
    Telephony data in the logging standard.

    10. No deepfake playbook

    Symptom
    Cloned-voice request freezes the process.
    Root cause
    Finance never rehearsed voice fraud.
    Fix
    Dual approval + callback for every payment change; report refusals to security.
    Prevention
    Finance-specific awareness with voice examples.

    11. Response ignores the human

    Symptom
    Victims hide mistakes; reporting comes days late.
    Root cause
    Blame-centered incident handling.
    Fix
    No-fault interviews, fast thanks for reports.
    Prevention
    Measure report latency as a health KPI.

    12. Ownership gap between teams

    Symptom
    Telephony, identity, and help desk teams each own a piece; nobody owns the attack.
    Root cause
    Channel-based instead of threat-based ownership.
    Fix
    Name one vishing-detection owner; run a joint playbook.
    Prevention
    Threat-based detection catalog with named owners.

    Cheat Sheet and Quick Reference

    The essentials on one screen — for incidents, for the help desk, and for building detections.

    Key facts

    Technique
    T1566.004 — Spearphishing Voice (sub-technique of T1566)
    Tactic
    Initial Access
    Delivery
    Phone call or voicemail with spoofed caller ID and urgency script
    Prime target
    MFA approvals, help desk resets, remote tool installs
    Strongest signals
    Factor enrollment/reset + new-device sign-in; unapproved remote tool + relay connection
    Best counter
    Help desk verification SOP + phishing-resistant MFA + remote-tool allowlist

    Top detections

    • Endpoint: remote access tool execution (Rule 1) and service install (Rule 2)
    • Identity: Okta factor change (Rule 3), Entra security info registration (Rule 4)
    • Network: relay-domain connection (Rule 5)
    • Correlation: reset/enrollment followed by new-device sign-in within 60 minutes
    • Correlation: MFA approval inside a flagged call window

    Response checklist

    • Anchor on the identity event: who, when, from where; pull the help desk ticket and its verification evidence.
    • Disable the account, revoke all sessions and tokens, remove attacker MFA factors.
    • Isolate any endpoint that ran a remote tool; capture triage data before cleanup.
    • Block the relay domains and the download source at proxy and DNS.
    • Search for the same tactics across other users: recent resets, enrollments, tool installs.
    • Reset credentials from a trusted device and re-enroll MFA with strong verification.
    • Interview involved staff supportively; capture the caller’s script and number.
    • Notify stakeholders per policy; involve legal and communications early if data was touched.
    • Fix the exploited procedure the same week; verify the fix with a test scenario.
    • Postmortem: add or tune one detection and update the awareness briefing with the real script.
