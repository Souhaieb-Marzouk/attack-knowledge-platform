T1566.003 Spearphishing via Service

Spearphishing via Service is a targeted attack delivered through a service the attacker does not own: a professional network, a messaging app, personal webmail, a file-sharing platform, or a bulk-mail service. Because the message comes from a platform the victim already trusts, it passes identity checks and feels completely normal. This guide explains how that abuse works and shows you how to detect it.

    1

    Overview

    This section explains what “via Service” means in practice, why attackers rent trust instead of building it, and where this technique sits in the attack chain.

    What is this technique?

    In a spearphishing via service attack, the attacker does not use enterprise email to reach you. They use a third-party service instead: social media, personal webmail, messaging apps, file-sharing platforms, or other services that the company does not control. MITRE describes exactly this: messages sent “through various social media services, personal webmail, and other non-enterprise controlled services,” which usually have weaker security policies than a company mailbox.

    The technique has two common shapes. Shape A — the social persona: the attacker creates a fake profile on a professional network, contacts the target with a believable reason (often a job offer), builds a conversation over days, and then sends a malicious link or file — sometimes to a personal webmail account the target also opens on their work computer, which skips company mail controls entirely. Shape B — the service notification: the attacker signs up to a legitimate file-sharing, e-signature, or bulk-mail platform and makes the platform itself deliver the lure, so the email comes from the service’s real domain with correct SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. In both shapes the trust was borrowed or rented, not forged, which is what makes this technique so hard to filter.

    Why it matters

    Most email defenses are built around sender reputation. A message from a well-known SaaS domain with perfect authentication usually sails through. Content filters hesitate to punish a whole service because thousands of legitimate users depend on it every day.

    For the victim, the message arrives in the same shape as their daily work: a shared file, a document to sign, a comment on a project. The story is believable because the platform itself is believable. Attackers know this and pick services their targets already use, which is why this technique shows up in both criminal and state-linked operations.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Like its siblings, this is an Initial Access technique. After a successful lure, attackers commonly continue with:

    • User execution of a downloaded document (T1204.002)
    • Malicious macro or script execution (T1059 family)
    • Command and control from the infected host (T1071)
    • Credential capture on a linked fake page (T1555, T1539)
    • Abuse of the compromised user’s mail and identity (T1078.004)

    The same attack, in words

    1Create the account

    The attacker signs up to a legitimate file-sharing service with a disposable mailbox and a name shaped like the target’s vendor or colleague. Some services need only an email address; the free tier is usually enough. The SOC has no reason to notice anything, because this activity happens entirely on the provider’s side.

    2Build the lure

    The attacker uploads a document named to match real business, for example Invoice_Q3_review.docm, and creates a share link. Password protection is left off and public access is on, so the file is reachable with one click.

    3The service emails the victim

    The platform sends its standard notification: “M. Weber shared a document with you”. It comes from the provider’s real domain with valid SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. The mail gateway sees a trusted, high-volume sender and passes it through.

    4The victim opens the notification

    The message lands in the victim’s mailbox among other real service notifications. There is no attachment to scan — only a button that points at the service’s own site. The victim clicks through to view the document.

    5Download from a trusted host

    The file downloads from the sanctioned SaaS domain. Proxy logs show a perfectly normal download from an approved category. The saved file carries a Mark of the Web, but nothing has alerted yet, because the delivery host is legitimate.

    6The payload executes

    The victim opens the document and enables editing. The embedded macro runs and spawns a script interpreter, then reaches out to the attacker’s command-and-control server. The endpoint is now the first place a strong signal exists.

    7Attacker takes control

    The attacker drives the implant interactively: harvesting saved credentials, enumerating shares, and moving deeper. The share link stays live for the next victims, and the SaaS account keeps working for further campaigns.

    8Defender responds

    The SOC joins the chain — mail from a sanctioned service, download, hostile process tree — isolates the host, revokes the share link through the provider, reports the abusing account, and ships a new detection for documents that spawn script interpreters.

    Variant in words — the social-persona shape (Shape A)

    A1: A fake recruiter contacts the target on a professional network about a real-sounding job. A2: Over several days they build rapport and move the chat to personal webmail or a messaging app. A3: They send “the job description” as a link to a hosted archive or a OneDrive/Drive document — the same payload path as Shape B, but delivered through channels the company barely logs. A4: The victim opens it on the work computer; from here the detection story is identical: download, execution, process tree. Real operations built this way include Operation Dream Job (Lazarus) and Contagious Interview, both listed in Section 5.2.

    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    To defend against service abuse, you need to understand why attackers choose it and how the pieces fit together. This section explains the attacker’s logic and includes a safe, lab-only exercise. All content is for defense and education.

    Ethics and law

    This section describes attacker behavior so defenders can recognize it. Abusing real services against real people without written authorization is illegal and violates every platform’s terms. All commands here are lab-only and must run in your own test environment.

    When and why attackers abuse a service

    Sending phish directly means fighting filters: new sending domains, warmed IP pools, and constant rotation. Abusing a service outsources that work. The platform already has perfect sender reputation, a signed domain, and a story the victim knows: “someone shared a file with you.” Content filters are also reluctant to punish a whole provider, so the malicious part hides behind the service’s business model. For targeted attacks, the service can even lend personalization: the sharing persona, file name, and comment fields are all attacker-controlled.

    How the attack is performed

    The attacker registers on the platform, shapes the persona to match the target’s world, and prepares the payload: a macro document on a file-sharing site, a signature request with a crafted template, or a survey form that proxies to a credential page. Then the service itself delivers the notification. If the platform inspects links, the attacker adds a hop: the notification stays clean and points to the service, while the service page points onward to attacker infrastructure.

    Favorite channels and service types

    Attackers pick platforms by ubiquity and by how much attacker-controlled text they allow into the conversation.

    • Professional and social networks: fake recruiter or colleague profiles start the chat (LinkedIn, X, Facebook); the lure link arrives inside the platform’s own message notification, so even the email about the message looks genuine.
    • Messaging apps: WhatsApp, Telegram, and chat platforms carry the same persona plays directly to the phone, outside corporate logging.
    • Personal webmail: the conversation moves from social media to a Gmail or other personal inbox that the target also opens on the work computer, bypassing company mail policy.
    • File-sharing and cloud storage: the classic “document shared with you” with a macro payload behind the download.
    • E-signature platforms: “review and sign” flows for finance and HR targets; templates can embed credential-harvesting steps.
    • Mass-mail services: legitimate newsletters hijacked from a compromised tenant to reach thousands at once.
    • Code and collaboration platforms: comments, gists, and commit pages that host lure text on trusted domains.
    • Support and chat portals: a message thread that arrives by email from a help desk the company actually uses.

    Prerequisites and conditions

    • A service account: disposable mailbox, sometimes a paid plan for custom branding or larger sends.
    • Target research: real colleague and vendor names, file naming habits, and current projects, gathered from professional networks and public data.
    • A payload or destination: a weaponized document, an ISO, or a credential page one hop from the service.
    • Timing: business hours in the target’s time zone, ideally during a period when the fake context is plausible (invoice cycles, contract renewals).

    Practical lab exercise (lab-only, safe)

    You can reproduce the shape of this attack locally to build and test detections: a “service notification” mail that contains only a link to a local file host, plus a fake document flow, all inside your own lab.

    Then practice the detection side: hunt the download in your proxy or web logs by user and filename, and write the Sigma and SPL logic you would want in production for sanctioned-domain downloads. The lab gives you ground truth for every field name.

    Common variations

    • Link-in-link: the service page contains a second link that jumps off-platform to the real payload.
    • Tenant impersonation: the sharing persona mimics a real vendor domain in the display name only.
    • Comment delivery: the lure text arrives as a platform comment or mention notification.
    • Mass-mail hijack: a compromised marketing tenant sends to a stolen list with embedded hops.
    • QR handoff: the notification carries a QR code so the file opens on a personal phone, outside corporate logging.

    Common attacker mistakes

    • Sharing persona does not match the target’s real contacts, and mail preview text is generic.
    • The same document hash or infrastructure gets reused across campaigns.
    • Public share links stay live after the campaign, letting responders confirm and report abuse.
    • The lure file needs macros, so MotW and protected-view warnings appear on the way.
    • Volume spikes on one SaaS account, which providers detect and suspend.

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    The message looks legitimate at every stage before the click, so defenders must watch the edges of the trusted flow: who shared, what was downloaded, and what executed afterward.

    How defenders can see this attack

    No single control catches this cleanly, because every individual step looks ordinary. The winning approach is a chain of joined signals: a notification from a sanctioned service with an unknown external sharer, a first-time download of a document-like file, and then an Office process spawning a script interpreter. Mail telemetry explains the why, endpoint telemetry proves the what.

    Artifacts the attack leaves

    • Mail layer: a genuine service notification whose sharing user is external, unknown, or first-seen for that recipient.
    • Social layer: platform notification mails (professional networks, messaging services) whose embedded message text carries attacker-controlled links; fake-persona profiles that no vendor list knows.
    • Proxy layer: a download from the sanctioned domain with a business-like filename and a document or archive content type.
    • Endpoint layer: a file with Mark of the Web, then a hostile process tree: Office or the browser spawning wscript, cmd, mshta, or curl.
    • SaaS audit layer (when enabled): the share event, the sharing account, viewers, and download counts on the provider side.
    • Identity layer: sign-ins from the same user shortly after, if credentials were also harvested.

    Where to look, in order

    1. Mail security logs: extract the sharing user and the platform tenant details.
    2. Proxy or SWG logs: the download from the sanctioned domain, by user and filename.
    3. Endpoint telemetry: file creation, MotW, then process tree within minutes.
    4. SaaS provider audit or abuse report: confirm and kill the share link.
    5. Identity logs: any follow-on sign-in or consent for the affected user.

    Why it matters for the SOC

    This technique defeats the reflex “known sender, therefore safe” that both filters and users have learned. If your only mail detections are reputation-based, an entire attack class passes silently. It also means incidents can start from your vendors’ platforms, so the investigation must reach outside your tenant. Teams that add service-abuse detections typically see their first catch within weeks, because these campaigns are common and loud once you know how to look.

    Short triage flow

    1. Identify the platform and sharer: which service, which sharing account, is it known to the recipient or vendor list?
    2. Confirm the download: did the user retrieve the file? Record filename, size, hash if available.
    3. Check execution: did any Office or browser process spawn a script host or connect outbound within 30 minutes of the download?
    4. Contain by depth: no execution → delete file, block link, report abuse. Execution confirmed → isolate host, kill processes, revoke sessions, reset credentials.
    5. Close the loop: report the abusing account to the provider, add the file hash and infrastructure to blocklists, and update the detection with what you learned.

    Common triage trap

    Do not close the case when the download looks clean. For this technique, the download is expected to be clean — the malicious step happens at execution time. Always check the process tree for at least 30 minutes after the download before you stand down.

    For this technique, intelligence has one special task: tracking which platforms are being abused this month and how the abuse looks. The same source mix applies, but the questions change: not only who is attacking, but through which trusted door they are walking.

    Vendor reporting

    OSINT

    5.4Logs and patterns

    Each threat maps to concrete sources. The event examples below are representative but realistic; confirm exact field names against your own vendor data before coding detections.

    Threat 1 — Weaponized document via sanctioned file share

    Sources: mail security logs, proxy/SWG logs, endpoint process and file events (Sysmon/EDR).

    Key fields: mail sender_domain and shared_by; proxy cs_host, cs_uri_path, rs_content_type; endpoint Image, ParentImage, TargetFilename, MotW zone identifier.

    Normal: colleagues and known vendors share documents; downloads from the sanctioned domain are followed by Office protected view, not script execution.

    Suspicious: external or first-seen sharer; download immediately followed by an Office process spawning wscript.exe, cmd.exe, or mshta.exe; ISO or IMG files appearing in temp directories.

    Detection logic: join the sanctioned-domain download to any process event within 30 minutes where the browser or Office process is the parent of a script interpreter, and alert regardless of the file’s static verdict.

    Field notes: ParentImage ending in WINWORD.EXE with child wscript.exe is the core anomaly; the ParentCommandLine ties the execution back to the downloaded file; IntegrityLevel: Medium shows the code ran with the user’s rights, so containment means host isolation and credential reset, not just malware removal.

    Threat 2 — Social-network persona with personal-webmail follow-through

    Sources: proxy/SWG logs (social platforms and personal webmail categories), DNS logs, mail security logs (platform notification mails such as LinkedIn message alerts), DLP or tenant restrictions for personal webmail, identity logs if credentials were typed.

    Key fields: proxy cs_host (linkedin.com, mail.google.com, chat domains), cs_user, cs_uri_path, referrer chain; mail sender_domain of notification mails; DLP policy_id for personal webmail.

    Normal: recruiters and colleagues really do contact staff on professional networks; personal webmail use exists in many offices and is usually low-volume and workday-shaped.

    Suspicious: a new professional-network contact whose conversation moves to personal webmail or a messaging app; the same user then reaching a file host or a young domain; notification mails whose message text contains links to domains that are not the platform itself.

    Detection logic: correlate social-platform sessions with personal-webmail use and a download or login on a non-corporate domain within the same user and day; treat the combination — not any single request — as the alert.

    Field notes: each line alone is harmless — social networks and webmail are normal workday traffic. The chain is the signal: new profile view, personal webmail minutes later, then a first-seen archive download from an uncategorized young domain. Join the three by cs_user and time window; that is exactly the correlation the Sigma/SPL rules in Section 5.5 automate for the download half.

    Threat 3 — Credential page behind a service link

    Sources: proxy logs, DNS logs, identity provider sign-in logs, mail security logs.

    Key fields: proxy cs_host, referer chain; IdP isInteractive, ipAddress, clientAppUsed; mail shared_by.

    Normal: users reach login pages directly or through your federated flow; referrers are your own portals.

    Suspicious: a session that touched a sanctioned service then a young or uncategorized domain with login paths; a sign-in shortly after with new device or ASN.

    Detection logic: flag browsing chains that hop from a sanctioned SaaS domain to a young login-style domain, then check that user’s identity telemetry for session-replay patterns.

    Threat 4 — Compromised SaaS tenant mass-mailing your users

    Sources: mail security logs, threat intelligence reports, SaaS provider status and abuse channels.

    Key fields: sender_domain (real platform), tenant_id or customer_id if exposed, recipient count, delivery verdict, embedded URL chain.

    Normal: steady volumes from known tenants; recipients mostly subscribed users.

    Suspicious: a known platform tenant suddenly mailing many of your users who never interacted with it; identical links across all recipients; template text mismatched with the tenant’s usual brand.

    Detection logic: alert when one external tenant on a trusted platform targets an unusual share of your users within an hour, and auto-extract the shared URL for sandboxing.

    Field notes: authentication is flawless because the sender is real; the telling fields are shared_by (external, unknown to the recipient) and shared_by_domain_age_days. Even without this enrichment, the recipient’s first-ever contact with that sharer is a strong signal your SIEM can compute from history.

    Threat 5 — E-signature template abuse

    Sources: mail security logs, e-signature platform audit (if federated or via API), finance application logs, DLP.

    Key fields: envelope creator, signer list, template name, embedded link targets, completion events.

    Normal: envelopes created by known internal senders or long-term vendors; signers match contract participants.

    Suspicious: envelopes from first-seen external creators that name your executives or vendors; links inside the signing flow pointing off-platform; sign requests that precede a payment-detail change.

    Detection logic: alert on first-seen external envelope creators combined with finance or executive signers, and require out-of-band verification before processing any linked payment change.

    Threat 6 — Support and chat portal lure

    Sources: mail security logs (ticket notifications), support platform audit, endpoint telemetry.

    Key fields: ticket ID, requester email, organization field, attachment/link list, agent responses.

    Normal: tickets from known requesters about known assets; notification templates stable.

    Suspicious: first-seen requesters impersonating vendors; links inside the transcript to external hosts; requests to run tools or visit pages “for support”.

    Detection logic: treat support notifications as untrusted content: extract every link, detonate, and alert when the requester organization does not match your vendor list.

    Threat 7 — Recon and link analytics abuse

    Sources: mail security logs, proxy logs, SaaS audit (view counts), DNS logs.

    Key fields: tracking pixel domains, tiny proxy responses (sc_bytes very small), repeated service link opens, viewer counts on shared files.

    Normal: internal shares with a handful of known viewers.

    Suspicious: external-first-seen viewers on any internal share; identical tracking pixels across many users; service links opened from datacenter IPs.

    Detection logic: aggregate opens and pixels by campaign identifier; alert on clusters, since single events are meaningless but clusters reveal active targeting.

    Coverage tip

    Threats 1 and 2 give the highest-value alerts and both are buildable today; threat 2 is a correlation habit as much as a rule. Threat 4 needs tenant-aware mail parsing, and threats 5–7 improve your picture but rarely page anyone on their own.

    5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    The rules below cover the ranked threats. Sigma logic is vendor-neutral; the SPL mirrors it and states its field assumptions. Adapt indexes and sourcetypes to your environment.

    Rule 1 — Office application spawning a script interpreter (Threats 1–2)

    Field assumptions: Sysmon data via XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational; for EDR products, map Image/ParentImage to their process fields. Tuning: allowlist known add-in workflows by hash or path; alert immediately at high — volume is normally near zero. False positives: legacy line-of-business scripts; document each one in the rule comment instead of disabling the rule.

    Rule 2 — Executable-like download from sanctioned file host (Threats 1–2)

    Field assumptions: Blue Coat style proxy fields; for Zscaler or Squid map host, URI, content type, and user accordingly. Tuning: add an allowlist for known software delivery paths; join results with Rule 1 events on user and time. False positives: legitimate admin workflows that fetch installers — scope them by group.

    Rule 3 — Trusted-platform notification with first-seen external sender (Threats 2 and 4)

    Field assumptions: your gateway must parse the sharing user or platform contact into shared_by and an external flag; if it does not, extract it from the mail body with a regex on the platform’s template. For professional networks, the platform message itself is not in the mail — the alert gives you the first-contact signal to correlate with proxy sessions (Threat 2). Tuning: keep a vendor allowlist per recipient group; start at medium severity and raise after measuring precision. False positives: legitimate new contacts — the alert is designed for review, not auto-blocking.

    Rule 4 — Public share link created on monitored SaaS (Threat 7 / abuse prep)

    Field assumptions: provider audit logs ingested (example: Dropbox audit via API add-on). If your tenant lacks audit export, enable it — without it you are blind to abuse on your own brand. Tuning: this rule is a low-noise early-warning source; route to a review queue. False positives: intentional public campaigns; mark them in a lookup.

    Rule 5 — LNK or script dropped into user temp (Threat 1 follow-on)

    Field assumptions: Sysmon file creation events (Event ID 11); EDR equivalents map to file-write telemetry. Tuning: pair with the download alert from Rule 2 on the same host; escalate to high when the writer process is Office or a mounted ISO path. False positives: software installers — filter by writer image allowlist.

    Validation checklist for all five rules

    Replay the Section 3 lab telemetry through each rule, run the Sigma YAML through your pipeline (sigma-cli check), track precision for two weeks before paging, and record owner, runbook, and expected volume in the detection catalog.

    Real-World Case Study

    In May 2021, Microsoft reported a spearphishing campaign by the group it tracks as Nobelium (also known as APT29 or Cozy Bear) that abused a legitimate mass-mail platform. The technique matches T1566.003 exactly: the attackers never spoofed a sender — they made a real service send the mail for them.

    Context

    The attackers gained access to the Constant Contact account used by USAID, the US development agency. Using that account, they sent emails to roughly 3,000 recipients across about 150 organizations, including government bodies and NGOs. The messages arrived from Constant Contact’s genuine infrastructure with valid authentication, and they carried USAID branding — a story (“new USAID-directed document”) that was credible for the diplomatic and policy audiences targeted. Embedded links led to attacker-hosted files: an ISO disk image containing a malicious shortcut that chained into Cobalt Strike beacons.

    Timeline

    • Before May 25, 2021Attackers compromise the USAID Constant Contact account, preparing a trusted sending channel with real branding.
    • May 25, 2021Campaign launch: about 3,000 mails go out to roughly 150 organizations; each mail links to hosted ISO files with malicious shortcuts.
    • May 25–27, 2021Microsoft’s Microsoft Threat Intelligence Center detects the campaign, tracks it as Nobelium, and begins notification work.
    • May 27–28, 2021Microsoft publicly discloses the campaign with indicators; targeting organizations can hunt for the ISOs, shortcuts, and beacon infrastructure.
    • Following daysRecipients block infrastructure, report findings, and the abused sending path is shut down; Microsoft notes prior Nobelium activity including supply-chain and brute-force campaigns.

    Attack chain mapped to ATT&CK

    • Platform abuse: T1566.003 — legitimate mass-mail service sends the lure.
    • Delivery: links to hosted ISO disk images rather than attachments.
    • User execution: T1204.002 — victim mounts the ISO and runs the shortcut.
    • Staging: shortcut loads a malicious DLL — T1574.002 hijack pattern.
    • Command and control: T1071 — Cobalt Strike beacon traffic to attacker infrastructure.

    Detection point

    The campaign was caught by intelligence-driven monitoring: Microsoft detected the beacon infrastructure and mail patterns, then notified the community. For the receiving organizations, the technical signals existed in layers — unexpected mass-mail from a vendor’s marketing tenant, downloads of ISO files, and shortcut files executing from mounted images. The case shows why sharing and fast disclosure matter: most victims avoided compromise because someone published the pattern in days.

    Containment

    Recipients that engaged blocked the hosting infrastructure, quarantined similar mails, and warned users not to open unexpected ISO files. Microsoft coordinated notifications to targeted organizations.

    Eradication and recovery

    Organizations hunted for the ISOs, shortcut names, and beacon domains from the disclosure; the sending account access was cut and the platform abuse reported. No widespread breach resulted, largely because of rapid warning.

    Impact

    Reach was large, success was limited: the campaign’s power was the trusted channel, and its weakness was fast publication. The incident became a reference example of platform abuse for state-linked intrusion operations.

    Team response and communication

    The effective response combined the platform owner (account termination), the discoverer (Microsoft, publishing indicators), and the targets (blocking and hunting). Internally, teams that succeeded had a simple rule: treat unexpected content from known brands as untrusted until proven, and give users a fast, blame-free reporting path.

    Lessons learned

    • Sender authentication proves the platform, not the person. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC all passed; the attack rode a real tenant.
    • Vendor accounts are part of your attack surface. A marketing platform account of a public agency became a delivery channel for intrusions.
    • Hosted links beat attachments for evasion. Detection must cover the download and execution layers, not just the mail.
    • Fast, specific disclosure is a control. Days of lead time from publication determined outcomes.
    • Mount-and-run formats still work. ISO/shortcut chains bypassed many filters, so process-tree detections matter.
    7

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot block the platform — your business depends on it. Defense therefore focuses on governing the trust: watching what rides on it, hardening the endpoint, and making abuse reportable.

    Email and SaaS governance

    • Build a sanctioned service register: which platforms may contact your users, from which tenants.
    • Extract and inspect sharing identities in service notifications; compare against the register.
    • Enable and ingest SaaS audit logs for your own tenants; demand abuse-report contacts from vendors.
    • Detonate or sandbox downloads from sanctioned domains, not just unknown ones.

    Endpoint hardening

    • Block macros from the internet (MotW policy) and keep Office protected view enforced.
    • Block or alert on mounted disk images executing shortcuts; prefer blocking ISO mounts for typical users.
    • Keep EDR behavioral rules for Office spawning script hosts; test them quarterly.
    • Use application control for standard user profiles where feasible.

    Identity and network

    • Enforce phishing-resistant MFA and conditional access for the accounts attackers want.
    • Watch for consent and session abuse after any service-lure click (see T1566.002 guide patterns).
    • Keep full-fidelity proxy and DNS logging per user for download context.

    People and process

    • Train specifically on service notifications: check who shared, not just who sent.
    • Train on social-persona lures: verify recruiter identities out-of-band and treat “job description” files from personal webmail as hostile.
    • Provide a one-click report button that works for platform mail.
    • Define a vendor abuse playbook: who reports to the platform, who informs the vendor owner.

    Home and small-office advice

    The same trust logic applies at home. When a mail says someone shared a file, open the service’s app or website and check the “shared with you” list there instead of clicking the mail button. Do not mount disk images or enable macros from unexpected shares, even if the platform is familiar. Keep Microsoft Office and your OS updated, since current versions block macros from the internet by default. Use strong unique passwords and passkeys where offered. If you opened something suspicious, disconnect the device, run a full antivirus scan, change passwords from another device, and report to the platform.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    These are the twelve failure modes teams meet most often with service-abuse phishing. Each card gives symptom, root cause, fix, and prevention.

    1. Allowlisting whole service domains

    Symptom
    Every lure from the platform is delivered without inspection.
    Root cause
    Filter policy treats the domain as trusted for content too.
    Fix
    Trust authentication, still inspect URLs, sharers, and downloads.
    Prevention
    Policy review separates sender trust from content trust.

    2. No SaaS audit logging

    Symptom
    Cannot answer who created or opened a malicious share.
    Root cause
    Audit export never enabled or never ingested.
    Fix
    Enable audit APIs for all sanctioned platforms; ingest to SIEM.
    Prevention
    SaaS register includes a logging column, checked quarterly.

    3. Blocking service domains outright

    Symptom
    Business complains; security quietly re-opens the block.
    Root cause
    Shortcut response after an incident.
    Fix
    Scoped rules: inspect sharers and payloads, keep the platform working.
    Prevention
    Response playbook offers graduated actions, not all-or-nothing.

    4. Ignoring Mark of the Web

    Symptom
    Downloads skip macro and MotW-based controls silently.
    Root cause
    MotW stripped by tooling or policy, or not logged.
    Fix
    Preserve and log MotW; alert on MotW files executing script hosts.
    Prevention
    Endpoint baseline includes MotW policy verification.

    5. Testing only raw attachments

    Symptom
    Detections tuned on direct-attach malware miss service delivery.
    Root cause
    Test corpus outdated.
    Fix
    Add hosted-link and mounted-image cases to the lab pipeline.
    Prevention
    Validation checklist covers every ranked threat.

    6. No process-tree analytics

    Symptom
    Only static file alerts; behavioral stage invisible.
    Root cause
    EDR rules focus on hashes and verdicts.
    Fix
    Deploy Rule 1 (Office spawning script host) and test quarterly.
    Prevention
    Detection review includes parent-child logic each cycle.

    7. Alert fatigue on sanctioned downloads

    Symptom
    Download alert muted within days.
    Root cause
    No join with execution or sharer context.
    Fix
    Score downloads: external sharer + script/ISO + user first-seen.
    Prevention
    Weekly precision review with measured signal ratio.

    8. Vendor relationships without contacts

    Symptom
    Abusive share stays live for days.
    Root cause
    No abuse-report path or account manager engaged.
    Fix
    Register abuse contacts per platform; practice takedown request.
    Prevention
    Vendor security reviews require abuse SLAs.

    9. Missing owner for SaaS detections

    Symptom
    Rules break when platform changes templates; nobody notices.
    Root cause
    Detections owned by nobody.
    Fix
    Named owner, review date, and test case per rule.
    Prevention
    Detection catalog review in team ritual.

    10. Ignoring internal share abuse

    Symptom
    Attackers reuse your own tenant to lure colleagues.
    Root cause
    Detections watch inbound only.
    Fix
    Alert on unusual external-facing shares from internal users (Rule 4).
    Prevention
    Quarterly share-permission review.

    11. No support-channel scrutiny

    Symptom
    Lures ride ticket notifications unnoticed.
    Root cause
    Support mail treated as internal-only.
    Fix
    Extract and detonate every link in ticket notifications.
    Prevention
    Include support channels in the mail-security scope matrix.

    12. Reports without feedback

    Symptom
    Users stop reporting service lures; data dries up.
    Root cause
    No response loop to reporters.
    Fix
    Auto-thank, publish outcomes, thank vendors publicly when useful.
    Prevention
    Report rate tracked as a KPI.
    Operating habits
    CadenceBest practice
    DailyReview service-notification alerts and endpoint process-tree alerts; confirm every execution result is investigated.
    WeeklyPrecision review of Rules 1–5; update the abused-platform watchlist from reports and your mail data.
    WeeklyTest one lab lure end to end (delivery, download, execution) to keep the pipeline honest.
    MonthlyReview public share links and external sharing on your own tenants; refresh vendor abuse contacts.

    Response checklist

    • Identify the platform, sharing account, and recipients from the mail record.
    • Determine who downloaded the file, when, and from which host.
    • Check endpoint telemetry 30 minutes around the download: process tree, script hosts, outbound connections.

    • Revoke the share link via the provider and report the abusing account.
    • Reset credentials for affected users and revoke sessions if identity signals exist.
    • Add file hashes, domains, and infrastructure to blocklists.
    • Search the environment for the same sharer, file, and infrastructure across all users.
    • Notify affected users and vendor owners; document timeline and decisions.
    • Postmortem: add or tune one detection and one awareness example from the case.
