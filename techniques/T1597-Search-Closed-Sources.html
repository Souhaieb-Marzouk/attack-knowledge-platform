1Overview

What this technique is, why attackers like it, and where it sits in the attack chain.

What is T1597?

Search Closed Sources means an adversary buys or licenses information about a target from private, paid databases instead of stealing it. The data is sold through legal marketplaces, so the buyer often breaks no law by purchasing it. Typical products include private threat reports, leak databases, passive DNS records, WHOIS history, and employee data sets.

The word closed is the key idea. Open sources such as search engines are free and public (ATT&CK covers those under T1593 and T1596). Closed sources cost money, and that small barrier filters out most casual researchers. The buyer gets curated, hard-to-find data with very little effort.

Why it matters to defenders

This technique happens entirely on someone else's platform. No malware touches your endpoints, no scan hits your firewall, and no login appears in your VPN logs. That means most security tools stay silent, and many teams wrongly conclude "nothing is happening".

In reality, a well-prepared attacker who bought the right data can skip weeks of noisy scanning and arrive at your door with valid employee credentials and a map of forgotten assets. Detecting the downstream use of that data, and watching the few indirect signals that do exist, is the defensive job here.

Where it fits in the attack chain

1 · Recon→ 2 · Weaponize→ 3 · Deliver→ 4 · Exploit→ 5 · Install→ 6 · C2→ 7 · Act

T1597 is a PRE technique: it happens before initial access. It feeds every later step. Bought credentials support password attacks (T1110), a bought asset map guides exploitation of public-facing apps (T1190), and bought personal data fuels phishing (T1566).

Written version of the simulation (text alternative)

STEP 1

Choose a target

A recon operator picks a mid-size company as the next victim. Before any scanning, they list what they need: employee passwords, an external asset map, and context about the company's sector.

STEP 2

Open a vendor account

The operator buys a subscription to a closed threat intel portal using a shell company identity. The portal logs the signup, but the target company cannot see any of this.

STEP 3

Search closed reports

Inside the portal, the operator searches the company name and sector. Two private analyst reports mention the target: one describes a recent acquisition, another lists ransomware groups active in that industry.

STEP 4

Buy technical data

The operator pays a data broker API for passive DNS, WHOIS history, and certificate records for the target's main domains. The query takes seconds and costs a few dollars.

STEP 5

Map the attack surface

The returned data reveals old subdomains, an expired certificate, and a legacy VPN portal that still resolves. The operator now holds an asset map the company itself may not have.

STEP 6

Buy employee data

From a credential broker, the operator buys leaked staff emails and password hashes from an infostealer log. Four employees reused their corporate password. A breach monitor would have flagged this weeks ago.

STEP 7

Pick the quiet entry

The operator compares the map and the credentials, then chooses the legacy VPN portal: reachable, less monitored, and one reused password away from a foothold. No attack has started yet.

STEP 8

The SOC's blind spot

The SOC sees no packets and no logins, because everything happened on vendor platforms. Only indirect signals exist: a vendor "your brand was searched" notice and a breach-monitor alert that is still waiting in an unowned queue.

3Red Team / Hacker Perspective

How professional operators and red teams think about bought intelligence, presented for defense and education only.

?

When and why attackers use it

Buying data is fast, quiet, and cheap. Active scanning (T1595) can take days and lights up every sensor the target owns. A paid lookup takes minutes and appears nowhere in the target's logs. Attackers also get quality: curated vendor reports explain which exploits work in the target's sector, and leak databases provide passwords that may still be valid.

It is also low-risk. Purchasing a report about a company is legal in most countries. An investigator reviewing a suspect's activity would find ordinary-looking purchases, not intrusion tools. This is why mature intrusion sets invest in data collection before touching the network.

≡

How it is performed

The workflow is very ordinary and commercial: register an account on a paid platform, pay for credits or a license, then query. Operators use reseller accounts, shell companies, or stolen payment cards to hide who is buying. The two sub-techniques split the market:

  • T1597.001 — Threat Intel Vendors: private reports, vendor portals, custom analyst research.
  • T1597.002 — Purchase Technical Data: passive DNS, WHOIS history, certificate data, subdomain lists, IP reputation history.

A third gray market exists: criminal shops that sell infostealer logs and breach dumps. Buying there is illegal in most jurisdictions, and defenders should know it happens anyway.

Prerequisites and conditions

Money or access

Vendor subscriptions run from tens to thousands of dollars. Brokers selling logs price per record. Criminals sometimes skip payment by compromising an existing analyst account.

A cover identity

Reputable vendors screen buyers, so operators register front companies or use resellers. Less careful actors simply use crypto payments where accepted.

Patience for OPSEC

Good operators avoid searching the exact target name, because some vendors alert monitored brands. They search suppliers, subsidiaries, or old names instead.

Common variations and mistakes

Variations
  • Ordering a custom report from an analyst to get target-specific answers (T1597.001).
  • Buying historic data: old DNS and expired certificates reveal assets that were forgotten.
  • Subscribing to data-broker APIs that sell aggregated person records for phishing prep.
  • Paying insiders at service providers for internal data instead of buying from platforms.
Attacker mistakes defenders can profit from
  • Searching the exact brand name, which triggers vendor keyword alerts to the victim.
  • Reusing the same buyer account and payment rail across campaigns, which lets vendors cluster activity.
  • Trusting stale data: the legacy VPN portal was decommissioned last month, so the bought map is already wrong.
  • Buying credentials that a breach monitor already flagged, so the passwords were rotated before use.

4Blue Team / SOC Perspective

You will rarely catch this technique directly. Here is what you can see, where to look, and how to triage it.

Reality check

ATT&CK rates this technique as difficult to detect, and that is honest. The official detection guidance says much of this activity may take place outside the visibility of the target organization, may have a very high false-positive rate, and is best covered indirectly — for example by watching related stages such as Initial Access. Your goal shifts from "detect the lookup" to "see the echoes": vendor alerts, leak hits, and the downstream use of bought data.

What artifacts it leaves (and where)

  • Vendor-side telemetry: some intel platforms tell monitored brands when someone searches for them. Request this feed from your vendors.
  • Breach and leak monitors: corporate email addresses or passwords appearing in fresh infostealer logs and dumps.
  • Certificate transparency: attackers staging lookalike domains or buying cert data leave CT-log echoes (certificates for typosquats).
  • Data-broker footprints: employee PII appearing in people-search listings, visible via privacy-scan services.
  • Downstream logs: bought credentials eventually appear as password-spray or successful VPN logins — where detection finally becomes direct.

Why it matters for the chain

T1597 is the quiet phase that decides the quality of everything after it. If your response plan only starts at initial access, the attacker already chose the time, place, and password. Teams that track pre-attack signals gain a planning buffer: a leak alert three weeks early is free prevention; the same fact discovered during incident response is expensive.

It also shapes third-party conversations. Vendors hold logs about who researched you. Most contracts never mention them. Asking for portal-audit or keyword-alert access is a cheap, high-value clause.

Short triage flow (run it as a playbook)

  1. Signal in
    Exposure alert arrives

    Source: vendor keyword alert, breach monitor, CT watcher, or ASM tool. Assign it to a named owner — unowned queues are where these alerts die.

  2. Minute 0–15
    Verify and enrich

    Confirm the alert is real (not a feed artifact). Record exactly what was exposed or queried: which domain, which credential type, which report.

  3. Minute 15–60
    Score the possible damage

    If credentials: is MFA enforced on every portal that accepts them? If asset data: is the exposed asset still alive? Check your CMDB and attack-surface inventory.

  4. Hour 1–24
    Hunt downstream

    Search VPN and SSO logs for the exposed accounts, especially from residential proxies or new geographies. Search email for phishing that references the exposed data.

  5. Day 1–7
    Close the gap

    Rotate exposed credentials, enforce FIDO2 on affected portals, decommission dead assets, then write a one-page summary to leadership with the cost of inaction.

5.2Threat intelligence

Good decisions need good inputs. The grid below lists the sources that matter for closed-source recon, how each is collected, and how to apply it.

R

Commercial vendor reporting

Portals and analyst reports (T1597.001) describe which groups target your sector and which TTPs work. Collect through licensed portals and API pulls into the SIEM. Apply by tagging incidents with vendor-tracked actor names and requesting portal-audit logs contractually.

D

Technical data platforms

Passive DNS, WHOIS history, and certificate data sellers (T1597.002). Collect via your own licensed queries for your own domains — this is the defensive mirror of the attacker's workflow. Apply by diffing results against your asset inventory monthly.

M

MITRE ATT&CK

ATT&CK itself is the shared language. Map every exposure alert to T1597 and its sub-techniques, then use ATT&CK Navigator layers to show leadership which PRE techniques your program covers and which it ignores.

I

ISACs and communities

Sector ISACs (FS-ISAC, H-ISAC, MS-ISAC and peers) share pre-attack observations peers have already seen, such as surges in lookups about members. Collect via member feeds and calls. Apply with a confidence tag before acting.

L

Breach and leak monitoring

Commercial dark-web monitors watch infostealer logs and dumps for your domain. Collect via alert webhooks into the SIEM. Apply by forced rotation and MFA verification — treat every corporate hit as valid until proven stale.

T

Internal telemetry and red team lessons

Your own logs show which bought-data paths would actually work: which portals accept password auth, which assets have no MFA. Collect via quarterly exposure reviews. Apply by fixing the gaps red teamers demonstrate in reports.

5.4Logs and patterns

For each threat: where the log comes from, the fields that matter, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and the detection logic. Event shapes use realistic field names you can map to your schema.

TH-01 · Staff credentials in leak and infostealer data Critical Identity

Log source: commercial dark-web / breach monitor webhook → SIEM. Fields: alert_type, email, breach_name, credential_present, log_type, first_seen.

Normal: no hits on your corporate domain in fresh dumps. Old, already-rotated breaches may appear as low-value noise if you ingest historical feeds.

Suspicious: corporate email with credential_present: true in an infostealer log dated within the last 30 days.

Detection logic: alert on any event where the email domain equals your registered domain AND a credential is present AND first_seen is recent. Severity rises if the account is an admin, exec, or finance user.

TH-02 · Employee PII listed for purchase High People

Log source: brand / privacy monitoring service that scans people-search and data-broker listings. Fields: listing_type, subject_role, data_classes, site_domain.

Normal: generic company listings, marketing contact pages. Suspicious: role-tagged records for executives, finance, or IT staff with phone numbers and personal details clustered together. Detection logic: alert when subject_role is high-value and data_classes contains two or more contact channels — that combination powers convincing lures.

TH-03 · Brand searched inside a vendor portal High Third-party intel

Log source: vendor "monitored term" notification (many intel platforms alert brands that subscribe). Fields: query_term, query_count, source_account_type, account_country.

Normal: your own analysts, competitors' marketing teams, journalists. Suspicious: repeated searches of your exact domain from newly created accounts, or spikes far above the monthly baseline. Detection logic: alert on query_count above baseline AND source_account_type: new in the same week a sector event (funding, M&A, layoff) is public.

TH-04 · Purchased technical data used to stage lookalike infrastructure High Network

Log source: certificate-transparency watcher watching your brand strings. Fields: common_name, issuer, not_before, dns_names.

Normal: certificates you issued, plus benign strings like "examplecorp-partner.net". Suspicious: a new certificate combining your brand with login-, vpn-, mail-, or sso-style words, issued days after a vendor-search spike. Detection logic: brand string + auth keyword + never seen before + recent CT not_before.

TH-05 · Vendor portal account abuse Medium Cloud / SaaS

Log source: vendor SSO/audit feed for your analyst account on their platform (request it contractually). Fields: user, auth_method, source_ip, geo, action.

Normal: two named analysts from office/VPN ranges. Suspicious: new country, non-browser agent, MFA method change, or bulk export actions. Detection logic: impossible travel OR new device OR action: export volume above the account baseline.

TH-06 · Bulk automated lookups of your domain Medium Network

Log source: vendor API audit feed (who queried technical data about your domains — offered by several providers). Fields: api_key_hash, endpoint, query_count, client_country.

Normal: security researchers, your own ASM tooling, compliance checks. Suspicious: one client pulling WHOIS + passive DNS + cert history for many of your subdomains within an hour. Detection logic: count of lookups per client per day above threshold, spread over multiple endpoint types.

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

One Sigma rule and one matching Splunk query per threat. Sigma log sources for vendor feeds are custom categories — map them to your ingestion as noted. All rules are educational starting points: tune thresholds to your baselines.

SIG-01 · Corporate credential appears in fresh leak data Critical

Mapping note: SPL assumes a corp_users lookup with role and MFA fields. The freshness check parses first_seen with strptime before comparing against the 30-day window, so it works with ISO-8601 timestamps. The Sigma filter_stale cutoff implements the same idea with a fixed date — update that date as time passes, or replace it with a scheduled rule revision. False positives: feed replay of old breaches. Tuning: adjust the 30-day window to your median credential-rotation time.

SIG-02 · High-value staff PII listed on data-broker sites High

Mapping note: SPL keeps the Sigma intent but adds a breadth condition (listed on 2+ sites or 2+ data classes) to cut single-listing noise. Tuning: maintain the subject_role list with HR; review quarterly.

SIG-03 · Spike in vendor-portal searches for your brand High

Mapping note: Sigma aggregation (count by account > 20 in 24h) mirrors the SPL 24-hour bin and threshold. False positives: internal accounts — exclude a known-accounts list. Tuning: start at 20, then raise to 3× your weekly median after two weeks of data.

SIG-04 · New lookalike certificate using brand and auth keywords High

Mapping note: the filter_owned allowlist and the SPL NOT list must contain every domain you own. False positives: new legit regional domains. Tuning: age window of 14 days keeps results actionable; escalate any hit with a live resolver answer to P1.

SIG-05 · Anomalous activity on your vendor portal account Medium

Mapping note: the 198.51.100. and 203.0.113. ranges are reserved example ranges — replace them with your real VPN egress ranges. Tuning: export threshold of 5,000 rows suits small teams; measure real exports first.

SIG-06 · Bulk technical-data lookups targeting your domains Medium

Mapping note: Sigma and the SPL share one threshold: the sum of query_count per API key per hour above 100 — both fire on exactly the same events. The 2+-endpoint check stays in the SPL as triage context; promote it to a hard condition in a companion rule if noise requires. Tuning: allowlist your ASM tool's key hash first, then set the threshold at 3× your busiest normal hour.

Validation

Test each rule with the JSON examples above: replay them into your SIEM's ingestion path and confirm the Sigma logic and the SPL produce the same hits. Then inject one "normal" event per rule and confirm it stays silent. Document both runs as your validation evidence.

6Real-World Case Study

An illustrative composite built from common, publicly reported patterns. Names and details are fictional, but every step matches techniques defenders see in real incidents.

Composite

No major public incident is attributed solely to T1597, because it leaves few victim-side traces. This composite shows how bought data typically enables an attack that is later detected elsewhere. Treat it as a training scenario, not an attributed case.

Victim

Meridian Regional Bank (fictional), 1,400 staff, several regional branches, one legacy VPN portal left from a 2019 migration.

Adversary

A ransomware affiliate crew that buys access data from brokers instead of scanning, then runs its own intrusion.

Objective

Encrypt core file shares and extort; sell excess access if encryption fails.

Timeline

  1. Day −21
    Exposure signal appears, and dies

    The bank's dark-web monitor fires: a finance VPN account with a usable password appears in an infostealer log. The alert lands in a shared mailbox nobody owns. It is never triaged.

  2. Day −18
    Attacker buys the data

    The crew purchases the same credential record from a broker and pays a technical-data platform for passive DNS and WHOIS history of the bank's domains. The vendor logs the lookups; the bank has no feed from that vendor.

  3. Day −18 to −4
    Target selection

    Passive DNS reveals vpn-old.meridianbank.example — the legacy portal. A purchased certificate history confirms it once served staff. It still resolves, and it is not in the bank's CMDB.

  4. Day 0 · 02:14
    Initial access

    The crew logs into the legacy VPN with the bought password. MFA never reached this portal during the 2023 rollout. A valid login from a residential proxy passes the geo rules.

  5. Day 0 · 03:00–06:40
    Recon and staging

    Using the VPN, they enumerate internal shares, stage a ransomware binary on two file servers, and create a scheduled task for persistence. Defender visibility: EDR telemetry starts, but the alerts are medium severity and queued.

  6. Day 1 · 07:55
    Detection point — the catch

    EDR flags mass file renames on the finance share. The SOC confirms ransomware behavior in 20 minutes. Correlation with VPN logs shows the same session staged the binary. The incident is declared P1.

  7. Day 1 · 08:15–12:00
    Containment

    Both file servers are isolated at the switch, the legacy VPN is disabled at the firewall, all bought-and-reused credentials are force-reset, and sessions are revoked org-wide.

  8. Day 1–3
    Eradication

    Scheduled tasks and loader binaries are removed, the two servers are rebuilt, and the crew's access-broker listings are checked to confirm no second set of credentials is for sale.

  9. Day 3–10
    Recovery

    Shares are restored from backups with a 6-hour RPO. The legacy portal is decommissioned and its DNS record deleted. FIDO2 keys roll out to the last 8% of staff, closing the MFA gap.

Attack chain summary

  • T1597 — bought leaked VPN credential from broker data
  • T1597.002 — bought passive DNS + certificate history to find the legacy portal
  • T1133 — external remote service login (legacy VPN)
  • T1087 / T1135 — account and share discovery
  • T1053 — scheduled task persistence
  • T1486 — data encrypted for impact

Tools in play

Attacker side: broker marketplaces and a technical-data API (purchasing), a residential proxy (access), commodity ransomware loader with scheduled-task persistence (impact).

Defender side: dark-web monitor (the ignored early signal), EDR (the catch), SIEM correlation of VPN + endpoint events, and backup/restore tooling (recovery).

Impact and consequences

  • Two business days of degraded operations; 6 hours of data re-entry
  • ≈ $1.8M total: response, recovery, overtime, and hardening
  • Regulatory notifications filed; no proof of large-scale data theft
  • Customer trust hit covered by local press for one news cycle

Team response and communication

The SOC lead declared the incident and activated the ransomware playbook. IT, legal, and communications joined a bridge call within 40 minutes. Updates went to the board twice a day in plain language: what is known, what is assumed, what is next. The vendor-broker question ("is our data still for sale?") went to the intel team as a standing task. A single spokesperson handled all external questions so engineers could work.

Lessons learned

  • The earliest warning (Day −21) was free — and died in an unowned mailbox. Exposure alerts need a named owner and an SLA.
  • MFA coverage was measured over "primary portals", which quietly excluded legacy ones. Coverage must be 100% of authenticators, not the popular ones.
  • The legacy VPN was invisible to IT because DNS for it was never cleaned up. Decommission = remove the record.
  • Vendor logs existed about the attacker's research. No contract clause meant no feed. Ask during renewal, not during an incident.

7Mitigation and Defense

You cannot block a purchase, but you can make its results worthless. Controls are grouped by where they live. This matches MITRE's official mitigation position for the T1597 family (M1056 Pre-compromise): the activity happens outside enterprise defenses, so efforts focus on minimizing the amount and sensitivity of data available to external parties.

Home and small-office advice

P

One password per account

Use a password manager. If a broker sells one of your passwords, it only opens one door, not your whole life.

M

Turn on MFA

Prefer app-based or hardware keys over SMS. This single step defeats most bought-credential attacks.

A

Check for breaches

Use reputable breach-notification services for your main email. If your address appears, change that password immediately — before someone buys it.

Response checklist

  • Confirm the exposure alert is genuine and record exactly what was exposed or queried
  • Identify affected accounts and assets; check MFA status on every portal that accepts them
  • Force credential rotation for exposed accounts; revoke active sessions
  • Hunt VPN/SSO/email logs for use of the exposed data, including from residential proxies
  • Check that referenced assets still exist; decommission dead ones and purge their DNS
  • Open a data-broker removal case if staff PII is listed
  • Share sanitized findings with your ISAC and with the vendor that raised the alert
  • Write the one-page summary: what happened, what we fixed, what we changed
