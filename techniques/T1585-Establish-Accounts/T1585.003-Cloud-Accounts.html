T1585.003 Cloud Accounts

Cloud platforms give anyone reliable servers, storage, and email sending in minutes — including attackers. With a prepaid card or a free tier, an adversary registers a fresh cloud account. The result is trustworthy hosting for phishing pages, command channels, and tools. This page shows how that account is built, what it does next, and the proxy, DNS, and cloud signals that expose it.

Overview

What cloud account establishment means, why attackers rent instead of build, and what this page teaches.

Cloud Accounts (T1585.003) means the attacker registers a new account with a cloud provider such as Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure, or Google Cloud. The service types include IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service — rented virtual machines) and PaaS (Platform as a Service — hosting for your code). The list also covers SaaS (software as a service — ready-made apps) and serverless (short code that runs on the provider's servers). Within minutes the attacker has a working IP address, a URL, and compute resources, all inside a provider that security tools trust by default. The account is theirs, paid with a prepaid card, crypto, or a free tier, and it can be deleted without a trace.

This matters because the cloud solves the attacker's oldest problems: uptime, bandwidth, and reputation. A phishing page on a big provider's domain loads fast and rarely gets flagged by reputation filters. A command-and-control server on a known provider blends into the flood of legitimate cloud traffic your organization generates every day. Cost falls on a stolen card or a free quota, not on the attacker.

In the attack chain this sits in Resource Development. The accounts feed T1583.003 Server and T1583.005 Web Services infrastructure, T1071 command and control, and phishing delivery at scale. Keep this separate from T1136.003 (creating accounts inside a tenant you already breached) and T1078.004 (using valid cloud accounts you stole). T1585.003 is about accounts the attacker registers from scratch.

Related techniques: T1584 Compromise Infrastructure, T1102 Web Service, T1583.001 Domains, T1567.002 Exfiltration to Cloud Storage, and T1608.002 Upload Tool. T1584 hijacks infrastructure others own, T1102 abuses live services, and the last two are common downstream uses of these accounts.

You will not see the account creation. You see what it hosts and who visits it: first-seen hosts, beacon-like traffic, phishing pages. Provider abuse desks are your partner for the takedown half.

Renting the attack platform

Step 1 of 8

The same story in text

  1. Getting payment. The attacker buys a prepaid card or steals card data, and prepares a disposable contact address.
  2. Registering. A cloud account is created through a VPN with a believable small-business story. The provider sees a normal customer.
  3. Deploying. A static hosting bucket or worker hosts the phishing page. A small VM runs the command-and-control relay, and an email API account sends in bulk.
  4. Pointing a name. A fresh domain and TLS certificate aim at the new resources, so links look legitimate in mail.
  5. Testing. The attacker loads the page from clean IPs, checks headers, and verifies mail deliverability to seed inboxes.
  6. Operating. The phishing wave runs; targets click. The VM relays beacons from any machine that was compromised earlier.
  7. Earning or stealing. Harvested credentials are used; on some setups, cryptomining burns the free quota for profit.
  8. Takedown and blocking. The abuse desk suspends the account after reports, while your proxy and DNS blocks stop new contact within your estate.
3

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

Why renting beats building, what attackers deploy first, and where the setup leaks. Defensive framing throughout.

3.1Why attackers rent cloud accounts

Infrastructure in minutes

Self-hosting needs hardware, bandwidth, and uptime skills. Cloud gives a root shell or a URL instantly, with world-class uptime and no hardware to lose in a raid. For an attacker, someone else runs the data center and carries the compliance work.

Reputation by association

Security stacks allowlist big cloud ranges or treat them as trusted. A phishing page on a workers-style subdomain or a storage endpoint inherits that trust. Filters see the provider, not the purpose.

Payment friction is low

Free tiers, trial credits, and prepaid cards cover most needs. Stolen cards appear in documented cases of mining and bulk sending. The provider's fraud team is the main friction, and it reacts after spend, not before.

Rotation is free

Burned infrastructure is abandoned, not patched. New account, new IP, new subdomain within an hour. Defenders must therefore cluster behavior, not chase single IPs.

Named example — Storm-1152. Microsoft reported this criminal bulk account-creation service in December 2023 and took down its tooling. It sold automated signups and phone-verification bypasses, feeding fresh accounts into phishing and fraud platforms. This is the technique operating as a paid service; confidence in the example is high.

3.2How the account becomes infrastructure, step by step

  1. Prepare the paper. Prepaid or stolen payment, disposable mail, VPN, and a small-business cover story for signup.
  2. Register. Account created on an IaaS, PaaS, or SaaS platform; free tier first for many operators.
  3. Deploy fast. Static bucket or worker for pages, tiny VM for relays and C2, email API for sending, keys created with minimal logging.
  4. Attach identity. Fresh domain with TLS points at the resources; DNS records look like a small SaaS.
  5. Validate. Page loads from outside, headers are clean, mail lands in seed inboxes, relay accepts test beacons.
  6. Operate and rotate. Campaign runs; on takedown the resources are replaced from a fresh account the same day.

3.4Prerequisites

What the attacker needs

  • Payment instrument that passes first charge: prepaid, crypto-backed, or stolen card
  • Contact point and, on some platforms, phone verification workaround
  • Basic deployment skills: one VM, one bucket, one function
  • Clean IP reputation at signup, hence VPN or residential proxies

What makes it harder

  • Provider fraud checks on cards and signup patterns
  • Abuse desks that suspend within hours of reports
  • Reputation feeds covering provider subdomains
  • First-seen rules on your proxy and DNS

4

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Cloud rental is invisible until it touches you. Here is what that first touch looks like and how to act on it.

4.1How defenders can see it

At first contact

Proxy and DNS logs record users reaching the fresh host, and firewalls record beacons to its IPs. First-seen logic is the core sensor.

Through public records

Certificates, passive DNS, and TI feeds expose the setup before your users click. Certificate Transparency (CT) logs — public lists of issued TLS certificates — make brand-token watchlists cheap early warning.

From providers

Abuse desks publish takedown data, and fraud alerts surface mining and sending abuse. Some platforms notify ISAC (Information Sharing and Analysis Center) members of active campaigns.

4.2Artifacts this sub-technique leaves

Provider resources

VMs, buckets, workers with generic names; visible to the provider, invisible to you until reported.

TLS certificates

Free certs for fresh names appear in CT logs, often hours before the first mail or click.

DNS patterns

Young domains pointing at provider ranges, with short TTLs and occasional IP churn.

Telemetry on your side

First-seen destinations, beacon-like periodicity, POSTs to unusual hosts from browsers.

Payment trail

Card BINs and signup fingerprints repeated across accounts; visible to providers after fraud review.

Content fingerprints

Page HTML, kit markers, and template reuse that clusters campaigns across providers.

4.4Why it matters and where it fits

Fresh cloud accounts are the silent supplier for half the techniques you detect downstream. When you block a C2 IP or a phishing host, you are seeing the end of a T1585.003 chain. Detecting the chain's shape — young resources, provider ranges, first-seen behavior — turns one-off blocks into standing rules that keep working as attackers rotate.

4.5Triage flow for fresh cloud infrastructure

  1. Signal in. A first-seen host, a CT alert, a beacon pattern, or a user report. Capture indicator and time.
  2. Enrich. Domain age, hosting provider, passive DNS history, TI verdicts, page content in a sandbox.
  3. Classify. Phishing host, C2 relay, or benign new SaaS. Ask the platform team if your own apps use it.
  4. Scope. Search proxy, DNS, firewall, and mail for the host and IP set over 30 days. List every user and device.
  5. Contain. Block at resolver, proxy, and firewall; purge any mail; reset users who typed credentials; hunt for beacons.
  6. Report. File abuse reports to the provider with evidence; add the cluster to watchlists and your ISAC.
Note

Distinguish three cases before acting: attacker-registered (this technique), stolen tenant (T1078.004, see your cloud audit logs), and benign new service (talk to the app owner). The containment steps differ completely.

Analyze

5.4Logs and patterns

Six event shapes cover the ranked threats. All come from sources you already own or can switch on this week.

SC-01 · Phishing page on fresh cloud static hosting High

  • Log sources: proxy logs, CT logs, mail gateway (for the linked URL)
  • Key fields: url_host, url_path, host_first_seen_days, method, action, user_agent
  • Normal: staff visiting sanctioned apps; provider subdomains from your own engineering
  • Suspicious: first-seen host on generic cloud hosting with login-path traffic and form POSTs
  • Detection logic: provider host list + first-seen under 14 days + login or verify path, human user
proxy · event example
{
  "ts": "2026-06-03T11:22:40Z",
  "user": "k.oyelaran@corp.example",
  "src_ip": "10.20.7.14",
  "method": "POST",
  "url_host": "secure-northwind-login.workers.dev",
  "url_path": "/submit",
  "host_first_seen_days": 1,
  "url_category": "unclassified",
  "action": "allow",
  "user_agent": "Mozilla/5.0 (Windows NT 10.0; Win64; x64)"
}

SC-02 · C2 relay on a fresh cloud VM High

  • Log sources: firewall or flow logs, proxy logs, EDR (endpoint detection and response) network events
  • Key fields: src_ip, dst_ip, dst_port, bytes_in, bytes_out, interval_seconds, process
  • Normal: heavy but irregular traffic to your sanctioned cloud tenants and CDNs
  • Suspicious: small, regular check-ins to one rarely-seen provider IP, same interval, low bytes
  • Detection logic: destination first-seen in 30 days + periodicity within tolerance + non-browser process or odd hours
  • Process note: firewall flows have no process field. Join the flow to EDR events on host and time to supply the process name for the non-browser check.

SC-03 · Bulk phishing via provider email API High

  • Log sources: mail gateway logs, DMARC reports
  • Key fields: sender, sender_domain, sending_ip, spf_result, recipients_count, recipients, urls

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

Six rule pairs for the ranked threats. Cloud audit rules assume a normalized audit stream; proxy and firewall rules assume standard access logs.

S-D1 · Access to fresh cloud-hosted phishing page level: high

Sigma YAML
title: User Access to First-Seen Cloud Static Hosting Host
id: f1a3c5e7-9b0d-4f2a-84c6-9e0f1a2b3c49
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects a user reaching a login-style path on generic cloud static
  hosting that was first seen in the environment within 14 days. Such
  hosts are typically deployed on accounts created with MITRE ATT&CK
  T1585.003.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/003/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-05-11
logsource:
  category: proxy
detection:
  selection_hosts:
    url_host|endswith:
      - '.workers.dev'
      - '.pages.dev'
      - '.netlify.app'
      - '.vercel.app'
      - '.web.core.windows.net'
      - '.blob.core.windows.net'
      - '.storage.googleapis.com'
      - '.s3.amazonaws.com'
      - '.amazonaws.com'
      - '.herokuapp.com'
  selection_path:
    url_path|contains:
      - 'login'
      - 'signin'
      - 'verify'
      - 'secure'
      - 'owa'
  filter_sanctioned:
    url_host|endswith:
      - '.sanctioned-preview.example'
  condition: selection_hosts and selection_path and not filter_sanctioned
fields:
  - user
  - src_ip
  - url_host
  - url_path
  - host_first_seen_days
  - action
falsepositives:
  - Engineering previews and sanctioned internal tools
  - Vendor pages on shared storage endpoints
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585.003
  - attack.t1583.005
  - attack.initial_access
Splunk SPL
index=proxy sourcetype="proxy:access"
| regex url_host="(?i)\.(workers\.dev|pages\.dev|netlify\.app|vercel\.app|web\.core\.windows\.net|blob\.core\.windows\.net|storage\.googleapis\.com|herokuapp\.com|s3\.amazonaws\.com)$"
| regex url_path="(?i)(login|signin|verify|secure|owa)"
| lookup sanctioned_static_hosts url_host AS url_host OUTPUT owner
| where isnull(owner)
| stats count AS visits, dc(user) AS users, earliest(_time) AS first_seen,
        values(user) AS user_list, values(url_path) AS paths by url_host
| eval first_seen_days=round((now()-first_seen)/86400,1)
| table first_seen url_host first_seen_days visits users user_list paths
| sort + first_seen
  • Mapping note: host and path fields map directly to any proxy stream. The sanctioned-hosts lookup is maintained by your platform team and kept under change control.
  • False positives: engineering previews. Ask teams to register preview hosts in the lookup; never remove the rule because of them.
  • Tuning: add method="POST" as a second-tier alert; a POST to a login path on a first-seen host is near-certain credential capture.
  • Severity: high; this is the compromise moment for cloud-hosted phishing.

  - DestinationPort
  - ProcessId
  - User
falsepositives:
  - Administration tools syncing with sanctioned cloud services
  - Backup agents with provider endpoints missing from allowlists
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585.003
  - attack.command_and_control
  - attack.t1071
Splunk SPL
index=network sourcetype="fw:flow" earliest=-24h
| sort 0 src_ip, dst_ip, _time
| streamstats last(_time) AS prev_time current=f window=1 by src_ip, dst_ip
| eval delta_seconds=_time-prev_time
| stats count AS sessions, avg(bytes_in) AS avg_in, avg(bytes_out) AS avg_out,
        stdev(delta_seconds) AS jitter_seconds, min(_time) AS first_seen, max(_time) AS last_seen,
        values(src_ip) AS sources by dst_ip
| lookup known_cloud_telemetry dst_ip OUTPUT is_sanctioned
| where isnull(is_sanctioned)
| eval first_seen_days=round((now()-first_seen)/86400,0)
| where first_seen_days<=30 AND sessions>=200 AND avg_in<5000 AND avg_out<8000
| table first_seen dst_ip first_seen_days sessions avg_in avg_out jitter_seconds sources
| sort - sessions
  • Mapping note: written for flow logs; for EDR network events swap sourcetype and field names. Flow logs carry no process field, so the SPL omits the Sigma process condition; run it on EDR events to apply the process check. The CIDR prefixes shown are examples for major providers; replace with your TI feed's current ranges.
  • False positives: backup and monitoring agents. Keep known_cloud_telemetry current; the rule is only as good as that lookup.
  • Tuning: tune sessions and byte thresholds to your 24-hour baseline; a small standard deviation of the time deltas between consecutive connections is the beacon shape.
  • Severity: high; assume a foothold exists until EDR proves otherwise.

title: Payload Download From Fresh Repository Path
id: 4f2b9d6e-8c1a-4e5b-b7d3-2a9c0e6f1d84
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects egress downloads of executable or script payloads from a
  repository path with no prior organization history, or a destination

      - 'wget/'

      - '.ps1'

  - src_ip
  - url_host
  - url_path
  - user_agent
  - repo_first_seen_days
  - ti_match
  - action

  - CI pipelines pulling fresh packages or artifacts
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.t1585
  - attack.t1585.003
  - attack.resource_development
Splunk SPL
index=proxy sourcetype="proxy:normalized"
| where (match(user_agent, "(?i)(curl/|wget/|powershell)") AND match(url_path, "(?i)\.(exe|ps1|zip)$") AND repo_first_seen_days<=7) OR ti_match="malicious"
| stats count AS downloads, dc(src_ip) AS sources, values(user) AS users,
        min(repo_first_seen_days) AS repo_age, values(ti_match) AS ti,
        earliest(_time) AS first_seen by url_host, url_path
| table first_seen url_host url_path downloads sources users repo_age ti
| sort - downloads
  • Mapping note: the proxy pipeline joins repo_first_seen_days and ti_match onto each event as enrichment; the SPL evaluates the same four conditions as the Sigma.
  • False positives: developer downloads and CI pulls of fresh packages. Allowlist sanctioned repositories and raise the repo_first_seen_days threshold if your teams publish often.
  • Tuning: alert on TI-match hits immediately; treat agent-plus-extension-plus-fresh-path hits as a hunt queue until you know your baseline.
  • Severity: medium; a hit from a server or service account is a staging incident.
6

Real-World Case Study

Serverless-brand phishing waves, documented by several email security vendors from 2021 to 2023: attacker-created cloud accounts hosting Microsoft and bank login clones on platform subdomains. Timeline simplified from public reporting for teaching.

Context

Phishing operators discovered that pages hosted on serverless platforms inherited the platform's clean reputation. They registered free accounts, deployed login clones within minutes, and pointed fresh domains at them. Each burned page cost nothing and took minutes to replace.

Victim experience

An employee gets a "mailbox is full" or "password expires today" message. The link lands on a page that looks exactly like the company sign-in, hosted under a slightly odd but technical-looking subdomain. They sign in; the harvest begins.

Timeline

  • Hours before the waveFresh cloud accounts created; phishing pages deployed; TLS certificates issued for lookalike domains; CT logs record the setup.
  • Wave dayThousands of lure emails go out, each linking to a unique platform subdomain to slow blocklists.
  • Minutes to hoursCredentials arrive at the harvest endpoint; operators test them against real sign-in portals.
  • Same dayVendors and researchers report the pages; abuse desks begin suspending accounts; some operators rotate to new subdomains within the hour.
  • Following weekProvider takedown reports and TI publications let defenders retro-hunt and block the whole cluster.

Attack chain

  1. Cloud accounts registered with disposable payments (T1585.003).
  2. Phishing pages deployed on platform subdomains; lookalike domains pointed at them (T1583.001, T1583.005).
  3. Lure emails delivered, often from provider email APIs (T1566.002).
  4. Users authenticate on the clone pages; credentials and MFA (multi-factor authentication) codes harvested.
  5. Harvested identities tested and used for mailbox or VPN access (T1078).

Tools observed

  • Attacker: commercial phishing kit with brand login clones
  • Attacker: credential harvest panel collecting passwords and MFA codes
  • Defender: CT monitor watching brand tokens in certificates
  • Defender: detonation sandbox for linked pages
  • Defender: mail gateway and proxy logs joined per campaign
Detection point

The first reliable internal signal was the proxy log: users visiting login-path pages on first-seen platform subdomains. The mail gateway fired moments later on linked-URL rules. Both led to the same cluster, and provider abuse reports ended it.

Response phases

Containment

  • Blocked the subdomains and lookalike domains at proxy and resolver
  • Purged wave emails; disabled the links' reach
  • Reset credentials for everyone who visited with a POST

Eradication

  • Revoked sessions and tokens for exposed identities
  • Checked for attacker-registered MFA devices and inbox rules
  • Abuse-reported the hosting accounts with evidence

Recovery

  • Standing rule kept for platform-subdomain logins
  • Cluster added to TI watchlists for rotation tracking
  • Staff note: check the address bar, not the page design

Impact and consequences

Organizations saw dozens to hundreds of credential submissions per wave. Where MFA was enforced with push approvals, attackers pivoted to fatigue attempts. Financial loss stayed limited, but exposed identities required days of resets, session revocations, and monitoring. The campaigns returned monthly with new kit variants.

Team response and communication

Security and messaging teams ran a joint playbook. They purged mail, reset users, published a two-sentence warning with the actual subdomain pattern, and asked staff to report similar mail. The platform team registered sanctioned preview hosts so the standing rule stayed quiet.

Lessons learned

  • Trusted platforms need behavior-based rules, not reputation trust.
  • Proxy telemetry catches what mail filters miss, and vice versa; join them.
  • Unique subdomains per victim slow blocklists; cluster by page content instead.
  • Abuse desks act fast when evidence packs are complete; prepare templates.
  • Phishing-resistant MFA converts credential harvest into a dead end.

Cloud rental is unfixable at the source; your job is to make rented infrastructure fail against your estate and to catch tenant abuse fast.
