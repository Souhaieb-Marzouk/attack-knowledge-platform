T1585.002 Email Accounts

Email is still the main entry point of most attacks, and attackers control their own access. They register fresh mailboxes and lookalike domains days before a campaign. This page shows how those accounts are created and warmed up, and how mail telemetry, authentication records, and first-contact rules turn them into detectable signals.

Overview

What email account establishment means, why it is the attacker's favorite setup step, and what this page teaches.

Email Accounts (T1585.002) means the attacker creates a mailbox on an email service. The attacker may also register a domain and build mailboxes on it for use in an operation. The mailbox may be a free account on a big provider. It may also be a polished setup on a lookalike domain with proper SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records. These three standards prove which servers may send for a domain. Either way, the attacker owns it from day one and can abandon it without cost.

This matters because every later email attack needs a sender. Stolen mailboxes are risky, since the owner may recover them. Fresh accounts are disposable, controllable, and — if warmed up — surprisingly trusted by filters. Business email compromise (BEC), credential phishing, and malware delivery all start here. Defenders get an advantage: unlike social personas, email leaves rich telemetry the moment it touches you.

In the attack chain this sits in Resource Development. It usually feeds T1566 Phishing and its service-based variants. Once a foothold exists, it also feeds T1534 Internal Spearphishing. University phishing campaigns such as Silent Librarian showed how disciplined this preparation can be: new lookalike domains and mailboxes deployed each semester.

Three related techniques share this infrastructure. T1598 Phishing for Information uses the same mailboxes to harvest details before an operation. T1656 Impersonation builds the trusted identity behind the sender. T1102 Web Service covers attacker sites and legitimate services used to host the lure.

Why this one is detectable

The lookalike mailbox setup

Step 1 of 8

The same story in text

  1. Picking the name. The attacker chooses a lookalike domain one letter away from a real vendor or your own company, and checks that filters have never seen it.
  2. Registering. The domain is bought with a prepaid card through a VPN. The public WHOIS registration record now shows a creation date measured in hours.
  3. Building mail. Mailboxes like billing@ and support@ are created on the new domain, and SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records are published so authentication looks healthy.
  4. Warming up. Small, harmless messages go out to seed addresses for days, building a clean sender history before the real send.
  5. Crafting the lure. Display names copy a finance manager or vendor; the story is an invoice update or a payroll change with a real deadline.
  6. The wave. Targeted staff receive the phishing emails from the warm domain. Authentication checks may pass, because the attacker really controls the domain.
  7. Credential capture. A user replies or enters credentials on the linked page. The attacker harvests the result.
  8. SOC response. The gateway blocks the domain, messages are purged, credentials reset, and the registrar is asked to suspend the domain.
3

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

Why attackers build their own mail infrastructure and where it breaks. Defensive framing; every command shown is read-only against public data.

3.1Why attackers build their own mail

Full control of reputation

A fresh domain begins with no history. The attacker controls SPF, DKIM, and DMARC for it, so the mail passes authentication checks that fail for spoofed senders. They can also rotate: a flagged domain is replaced in hours.

Cheap and disposable

A domain costs a few dollars for a year; free mailboxes cost nothing. Compared with buying aged domains from reseller markets, self-registration is the low-risk default, especially for one-time campaigns.

Display names do the lying

Even with a plain free mailbox, the attacker sets a display name like "Northwind Finance". Many users read the name, not the address. For BEC, that single trick carries most campaigns.

Warm-up builds sender history slowly

Filters score sender history. Patient operators send tiny volumes for days and avoid links at first. That is why first-contact rules matter more than content rules for this technique.

3.2How the setup works, step by step

  1. Choose the identity. Lookalike domain for phishing at scale, or a free mailbox for quick BEC and persona handoffs.
  2. Register quietly. VPN exit, disposable contact, prepaid payment. The goal is a signup that looks like a small business.
  3. Build the records. Create mailboxes with trustworthy local parts, publish SPF and DKIM, set DMARC to monitor first.
  4. Warm the sender. Days of low-volume, link-free mail to seed inboxes; some operators use mail-warming automation.
  5. Stage the lures. Templates for invoices, payroll, document sharing; hosting for credential pages if needed.
  6. Send and rotate. Run the wave, watch for blocks, and switch domains or providers when reputation turns.

3.4Prerequisites

What the attacker needs

  • A registrable name close to a trusted brand or person
  • A mail provider or a cheap VPS (a rented virtual private server) with open ports 25/587
  • Payment and contact details that survive basic checks
  • Days of patience for warm-up, or willingness to burn fast

What makes it harder

  • Registrars with strong identity verification
  • Brand monitors catching typosquats at registration
  • DMARC enforcement at receiving organizations
  • First-seen sender rules with retroactive purge
Lab only — read-only mail checks

These commands read public DNS records of domains you are authorized to test, such as your own company or example.com. They send no mail and touch nobody.

bash · read-only authentication-record checks
# What authentication records does your domain publish?
dig +short TXT example.com | grep -i spf
dig +short TXT _dmarc.example.com
dig +short TXT default._domainkey.example.com

# Which servers receive mail for it?
dig +short MX example.com

# Compare a suspect lookalike (registered by your team for training)
dig +short TXT _dmarc.example.net

The most common failure is rushing warm-up. Domains that jump from zero to hundreds of messages are exactly what volume-plus-age rules catch. The second is template reuse, which lets defenders cluster and pre-block future waves.

4

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Mail gives defenders real telemetry. Here is what to watch, which artifacts exist, and a triage flow for a suspicious sender.

4.1How defenders can see it

Gateway telemetry

Every delivery attempt is logged: sender, recipients, SPF, DKIM, DMARC verdicts, attachments, and the final action. This is your primary sensor for T1585.002.

Public records

WHOIS age, DNS records, and certificate logs show the preparation before the first mail arrives. Brand watches convert them into alerts.

Users as sensors

A report button with fast feedback catches what filters miss, especially display-name BEC where technical checks pass.

4.2Artifacts this sub-technique leaves

WHOIS record

Young creation date, sometimes odd registrant data; the age is the reliable part.

DNS records

SPF, DKIM, DMARC published suddenly for a brand-adjacent name.

Mail headers

Message IDs, sending IPs, and mail server names that map to the attacker's stack.

Authentication results

SPF and DKIM may pass for the attacker's own domain while the display name lies.

Content fingerprints

Templates reused across mailboxes and campaigns; ideal for clustering.

Linked pages

Credential pages on young hosts; proxy and Certificate Transparency (CT) logs — public lists of issued TLS certificates — carry them.

4.3Where to look

SourceWhat to checkWhy it helps
Mail gateway logsFirst-seen senders, auth verdicts, recipient countsPrimary telemetry for every mail-based threat below
DMARC aggregate reportsWho sends as your domain from whereExposes spoofing attempts you never see delivered
WHOIS and CT feedsBrand-adjacent registrations and certificatesEarly warning before the wave
Proxy and DNS logsClicks to the wave's linked pagesIdentifies who interacted after delivery
User reportsForwarded messages with full headersHighest-precision signal for BEC
TI platformsSender domain and template reputationTurns one hit into a blocklist cluster

4.4Why it matters and where it fits

Mail account establishment is the supply chain of phishing. Blocking one wave stops a single incident; blocking the infrastructure that sends every wave prevents them all. The same artifacts also power retro hunts: every young-domain block should trigger a 30-day mail lookback, because the first wave often predates your rule.

4.5Triage flow for a suspicious sender

  1. Capture. Get the message with full headers, or the gateway's event record. Note every recipient.
  2. Authenticate. Read SPF, DKIM, and DMARC verdicts, and compare display name to the real sending domain.
  3. Enrich. Check domain age, passive DNS, and TI verdicts for the sender domain and any links.
  4. Scope. Search 30 days of mail for the sender domain and template; list every user who received, opened, or clicked.
  5. Contain. Block the domain, purge delivered copies, and reset credentials for anyone who interacted.
  6. Close the loop. Report the domain and mailbox to providers, add cluster indicators to watchlists, and tell reporters the outcome.
Note

SPF and DKIM passing is not safety. They only prove the sender controls that domain, which attackers do when the domain is theirs. Always judge the pair: who the display name claims to be versus who authenticated.

5.4Logs and patterns

Six event shapes cover the ranked threats. Normalize them once; the rules in 5.5 read exactly these fields.

SE-01 · Lookalike domain with fresh mailboxes High

  • Log sources: WHOIS/RDAP records (RDAP — the modern replacement for WHOIS record lookups), CT logs, DNS resolver logs, gateway logs once mail starts
  • Key fields: domain, creation_date, mx_host, spf_record, query
  • Normal: your own domains and known vendors with stable records and age in years
  • Suspicious: brand-adjacent domain under 14 days old publishing complete mail records immediately
  • Detection logic: brand token in name + young age + MX and SPF present = prepare block before first mail
dns / whois · event example
{
  "ts": "2026-04-02T08:05:12Z",
  "domain": "northwind-finance.com",
  "creation_date": "2026-04-01T21:44:00Z",
  "registrar": "registrar-x",
  "mx_host": "mail.northwind-finance.com",
  "spf_record": "v=spf1 mx -all",
  "dmarc_record": "v=DMARC1; p=none",
  "brand_token_match": "northwind",
  "age_days": 0
}

SE-02 · First-contact phishing from a young sender High

  • Log sources: mail gateway logs, DMARC reports, proxy logs for link clicks
  • Key fields: sender, sender_domain, domain_age_days, recipients, recipients_count, spf_result, dkim_result, dmarc_result, urls
  • Normal: senders with months of history and stable volumes; new senders usually known vendors
  • Suspicious: brand-new sender domain writing to several staff, with a link and an urgency story
  • Detection logic: first-seen sender in 30 days + young domain + recipients above threshold; quarantine first, ask later

SE-03 · Display-name BEC from attacker-owned mailbox Critical

  • Log sources: mail gateway logs, user reports, finance system change tickets
  • Key fields: display_name, header_from, sender_domain, subject, recipients, reply_to
  • Normal: internal finance mail from your tenant; vendors from their real domains with matching names
  • Suspicious: display name claiming an executive, vendor, or "Accounts Payable" while the sending domain is young or personal; reply-to differing from the sender
  • Detection logic: external direction + executive or finance display-name tokens + payment keywords, with internal-domain filter

SE-04 · Warm-up behavior from a young tenant Medium

  • Log sources: gateway logs aggregated per sender domain, DMARC reports
  • Key fields: sender_domain, domain_age_days, daily_volume, links_count, attachment_count
  • Normal: new legitimate senders ramp quickly to real volumes or come from known SaaS platforms
  • Suspicious: young domain sending 1 to 5 text-only messages per day for a week, then jumping to dozens with links
  • Detection logic: young domain + low steady volume + later volume jump; track per-domain daily stats for 14 days

SE-05 · Click-through to the wave's credential page High

  • Log sources: proxy logs, DNS resolver logs, mail gateway logs for the sender linkage
  • Key fields: user, src_ip, url_host, url_path, action, host_first_seen_days
  • Normal: logins to classified business portals and known vendor sites
  • Suspicious: a login-style path on a young host that mail telemetry recently saw as a first-contact sender
  • Detection logic: allowed visit + login-style path + host matches a recent sender domain = likely compromise moment

SE-06 · Thread hijack with reply-to diversion High

  • Log sources: mail gateway logs with thread metadata, user reports, finance system change tickets
  • Key fields: thread_id, subject, from_domain, reply_to_domain, recipients (the thread participants), recipients_count

  • Detection logic: group by thread_id; alert when from_domain or reply_to_domain differs from earlier messages in the same thread

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

Six rule pairs cover the ranked threats one for one, SE-01 to SE-06. Field assumptions are listed under each pair.

S-C1 · DNS query to a brand lookalike domain level: high

Sigma YAML
title: DNS Query to Brand Lookalike Domain
id: a3c5e7f9-1b2d-4f6a-8b0c-2d4e6f8a0b25
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects internal DNS queries for domains that contain a brand token
  on suspicious TLDs. Lookalike domains like this are registered and
  equipped with mailboxes under MITRE ATT&CK T1585.002 before phishing
  waves. The Sigma rule matches brand tokens in the domain name. Deeper
  similarity checks (edit distance) are a hunting task; see the tuning
  note under the rule.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/002/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-04-06
logsource:
  category: dns
detection:
  selection_brand:
    query|contains:
      - 'northwind'
      - 'contoso'
  selection_tld:
    query|endswith:
      - '.top'
      - '.xyz'
      - '.icu'
      - '.cfd'
      - '.online'
      - '.shop'
  filter_known:
    query|endswith:
      - 'northwind.com'
      - 'contoso.com'
  condition: selection_brand and selection_tld and not filter_known
fields:
  - client
  - query
  - qtype
  - answer
falsepositives:
  - Marketing campaigns on cheap TLDs run by your own company
  - Partner domains containing the brand token legitimately
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585.002
  - attack.t1583.001
  - attack.resource_development
Splunk SPL
index=dns sourcetype="dns:resolver"
| regex query="(?i)(northwind|contoso)"
| regex query="(?i)\.(top|xyz|icu|cfd|online|shop)$"
| lookup corp_domain_allowlist query OUTPUT is_allowed
| where isnull(is_allowed)
| stats count AS lookups, dc(client) AS clients, values(client) AS client_ips,
        earliest(_time) AS first_seen, latest(_time) AS last_seen by query
| table first_seen query clients client_ips lookups
| sort - lookups
  • Mapping note: query maps to your resolver's queried name; replace brand tokens with yours. A TLD is the top-level domain, the part after the last dot. The TLD set is identical in both versions: .top, .xyz, .icu, .cfd, .online, and .shop. The Sigma rule cannot do edit distance, so the token list is the pragmatic approach.
  • False positives: your own marketing domains and partner names. Maintain the allowlist lookup.
  • Tuning: run for two weeks in alert-only mode; then pre-block matched zones at the resolver for non-allowlisted clients. For deeper similarity checks, hunt with edit distance against your brand list.
  • Severity: high when age under 14 days; add the age check as soon as enrichment exists.

S-C2 · First-contact phishing from a young sender level: high

Sigma YAML
title: First Contact From Very Young Sender Domain
id: e5f7a9c1-3d4b-4a6e-9c2f-4b5d6e7f8a93
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects an inbound message from a sender domain younger than 14
  days writing to several internal recipients with a link. Captures
  the first wave of phishing from accounts established with MITRE
  ATT&CK T1585.002.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/002/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-04-13
logsource:
  category: email_gateway
detection:
  selection_young:
    sender_domain_age_days|lte: 14
  selection_external:
    direction: 'inbound'
  selection_link:
    urls|exists: true
  selection_recipients:
    recipients_count|gte: 3
  filter_bulk_senders:
    sender_domain|endswith:
      - 'amazonses.com'
      - 'sendgrid.net'
      - 'mailchimp.com'
  condition: selection_young and selection_external and selection_link and selection_recipients and not filter_bulk_senders
fields:
  - sender
  - sender_domain
  - recipients_count
  - subject
  - spf_result
  - dmarc_result
  - urls
falsepositives:
  - New vendors announcing services to several staff
  - Event invitations from new organizers
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585.002
  - attack.initial_access
  - attack.t1566.002
Splunk SPL
index=email sourcetype="seg:mail" direction="inbound"
| eval domain_age_days=coalesce(sender_domain_age_days, round((now()-strptime(domain_created,"%Y-%m-%d"))/86400,0))
| where isnotnull(domain_age_days) AND domain_age_days<=14
| where isnotnull(urls) AND recipients_count>=3
| search NOT sender_domain IN ("*.amazonses.com","*.sendgrid.net","*.mailchimp.com")
| stats count AS messages, dc(recipients) AS people, values(recipients) AS staff,
        earliest(_time) AS first_seen, values(subject) AS subjects
        by sender_domain, sender
| table first_seen sender_domain sender messages people staff subjects
| sort + first_seen
  • Mapping note: seg:mail stands for any secure email gateway stream. If your gateway does not enrich age, the SPL computes it from a WHOIS lookup export named domain_created; add that lookup in your environment. The SPL counts distinct values of the multivalue recipients array documented in 5.4.
  • False positives: legitimate new vendors. Quarantine-and-review beats hard block on day one.
  • Tuning: raise recipients_count threshold until noise is manageable; exempt HR and marketing tools via the bulk-sender filter pattern.
  • Severity: high; this is the delivery moment for most credential phishing.

S-C3 · Display-name BEC with payment keywords level: critical

id: 08f2b4d6-a5c7-4e9f-b1a3-5c6d7e8f9a04
status: stable
description: |

fields:
  - sender
  - display_name
  - header_from
  - reply_to
  - subject
  - recipients

  - Real vendors discussing invoices from their own domains

Splunk SPL
index=email sourcetype="seg:mail" direction="inbound"
| regex display_name="(?i)(CFO|Chief Financial|Accounts Payable|Finance Director|Head of Finance)"
| regex subject="(?i)(bank account|wire|payment details|invoice|payroll|IBAN)"
| search NOT header_from="*@corp.example"
| search NOT sender_domain IN ("yourbank.com","your-payroll-provider.com")
| stats count AS messages, values(recipients) AS staff, earliest(_time) AS first_seen,

        by sender, display_name, header_from
| table first_seen sender display_name header_from messages staff subjects replytos
| sort - messages

  • False positives: real vendor invoice threads. Add their domains to filter_notify and keep the list under change control.
  • Tuning: pair with a finance-process control: bank changes always verified by phone callback, so alerts feed the process audit, not panic.
  • Severity: critical; single incidents have produced the largest direct losses of any mail attack.

S-C4 · Warm-up pattern from a young domain level: medium

Sigma YAML
title: Young Domain With Link-Free Low-Volume Mail Ramp
id: 2a4c6e8f-0b1d-4a2c-8e5f-6a7b8c9d0e16
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects the warm-up signature of attacker mail infrastructure: a
  young sender domain sending small, link-free, attachment-free
  messages to a few staff, the pattern used to build reputation before
  a real wave (MITRE ATT&CK T1585.002). Sigma matches one per-day
  aggregate event; the Splunk version rebuilds those aggregates from
  raw gateway events and applies the same conditions.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/002/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-04-27
logsource:
  category: email_gateway
detection:
  selection_young:
    sender_domain_age_days|lte: 21
  selection_quiet:
    daily_volume|lte: 6
  selection_clean:
    links_count: 0
    attachment_count: 0
  filter_known_saas:
    sender_domain|endswith:
      - 'amazonses.com'
      - 'sendgrid.net'
      - 'mailgun.net'
  condition: selection_young and selection_quiet and selection_clean and not filter_known_saas
fields:
  - sender_domain
  - domain_age_days
  - daily_volume
  - recipients_internal
falsepositives:
  - New legitimate business partners writing personally
  - Support tools sending plain-text notifications
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.t1585.002
  - attack.resource_development
Splunk SPL
index=email sourcetype="seg:mail" direction="inbound"
| eval domain_age_days=coalesce(sender_domain_age_days, round((now()-strptime(domain_created,"%Y-%m-%d"))/86400,0))
| where isnotnull(domain_age_days) AND domain_age_days<=21
| search NOT sender_domain IN ("*.amazonses.com","*.sendgrid.net","*.mailgun.net")
| bucket _time span=1d
| stats count AS daily_volume, count(eval(coalesce(links_count,0)>0)) AS messages_with_links,
        count(eval(coalesce(attachment_count,0)>0)) AS messages_with_attachments,
        values(sender) AS senders by _time, sender_domain
| where daily_volume<=6 AND messages_with_links=0 AND messages_with_attachments=0
| stats dc(_time) AS quiet_days, avg(daily_volume) AS avg_daily, max(daily_volume) AS peak_daily,
        values(senders) AS senders by sender_domain
| table sender_domain quiet_days avg_daily peak_daily senders
| sort - quiet_days
  • Mapping note: Sigma expects one aggregate event per sender domain per day, like the 5.4 daily stat example. The SPL rebuilds those aggregates from raw gateway events and enforces the same conditions. They are: age 21 days or less, six or fewer messages that day, and zero links or attachments that day. The final per-domain summary (quiet_days, avg_daily) is enrichment for triage. domain_created comes from your WHOIS enrichment lookup.
  • False positives: companies announcing events, or new vendors ramping up mailings. Confirm with the business before blocking.
  • Tuning: for the volume jump itself, add a companion hunt that removes the quiet-day filter and alerts when a day reaches 30 or more messages. Without age enrichment, drop the age clause and rely on the volume shape.
  • Severity: medium; it is preparation, but a hit predicts the next wave days out.

S-C6 · Thread hijack and reply-to fraud level: high

Sigma YAML
title: Thread Reply With Changed Sender or Reply-To Domain
id: 30cef9fe-5e9c-4def-aadd-0e6d7489778e
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects an inbound reply inside an existing email thread that sets a
  reply-to address while the sender is external. Attackers hijack live
  conversations or change Reply-To mid-thread to divert payments, using
  mailboxes established with MITRE ATT&CK T1585.002. The mid-thread
  comparison of domains runs in the Splunk version; Sigma matches the
  per-message event shape.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/002/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-20
logsource:
  category: email_gateway
detection:
  selection_external:
    direction: 'inbound'
  selection_thread:
    subject|startswith:
      - 'RE:'
      - 'FW:'
      - 'FWD:'
  selection_reply_to:
    reply_to_domain|exists: true
  filter_internal:
    from_domain|endswith: 'corp.example'
  condition: selection_external and selection_thread and selection_reply_to and not filter_internal
fields:
  - thread_id
  - subject
  - from_domain
  - reply_to_domain
  - recipients
  - recipients_count
falsepositives:
  - Vendors that legitimately reply from a different domain than the thread starter
  - Ticket systems that set an external reply-to address
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585
  - attack.t1585.002
  - attack.resource_development
Splunk SPL
index=email sourcetype="seg:mail" direction="inbound" thread_id=*
| eval reply_mismatch=if(isnotnull(reply_to_domain) AND reply_to_domain!=from_domain,1,0)
| eventstats dc(from_domain) AS thread_from_domains, max(reply_mismatch) AS reply_diverted
        by thread_id
| where thread_from_domains>1 OR reply_diverted=1
| stats count AS messages, earliest(_time) AS first_seen, values(subject) AS subjects,
        values(from_domain) AS from_domains, values(reply_to_domain) AS reply_domains,
        values(recipients) AS participants by thread_id
| table first_seen thread_id subjects from_domains reply_domains messages participants
| sort - first_seen
  • Mapping note: Sigma matches the per-message shape: an external reply in an existing thread with a reply-to set. The SPL adds the correlation Sigma cannot express. It flags threads where the sender domain changed mid-thread, or where the reply-to domain differs from the sender domain. Field names follow the 5.4 SE-06 event shape.
  • False positives: long vendor threads where a colleague replies from another legitimate domain; ticket systems with external reply-to addresses.
  • Tuning: require at least two messages in the thread before alerting, and compare against the thread's first from_domain. Scope finance mailboxes first; a hit there is an active payment-diversion attempt.
  • Severity: high; replies inherit the trust of the original thread, so technical checks pass.

Real-World Case Study

Silent Librarian, tracked by vendors from 2016 to 2020: lookalike library domains and fresh mailboxes aimed at university users at the start of each semester. Simplified from public reporting for teaching.

Context

University libraries email students when books fall due. Attackers copied that pattern with high accuracy. Each semester, new lookalike domains imitated the library systems of dozens of universities. Fresh mailboxes sent urgent "library account expiry" messages with a login link.

Victim experience

A student or lecturer gets a realistic email during the exact week they expect library notices. The link shows a familiar login page. They type their credentials once; the attackers collect and resell access to the university network.

Timeline

  • Weeks before semesterLookalike domains registered for the target universities; mailboxes created; phishing pages copied from real portals.
  • Semester startWaves of expiry-notice emails hit students and staff at many universities at once, timed to real library notices.
  • Within daysCredentials harvested from victims who logged in through the fake pages.
  • Following weeksStolen accounts used for email access, VPN entry, and resale; some universities saw follow-on intrusions.
  • After takedownsNew domains appeared for the next semester, showing the campaign's refresh cycle.

Attack chain

  1. Lookalike domains registered; mailboxes and pages staged (T1585.002, T1583.001).
  2. Sending warmed with realistic, low-volume notices.
  3. Waves delivered at the moment users expected library mail (T1566.002 Spearphishing Link).
  4. Credential pages captured logins; sessions taken over.
  5. Accounts abused for data access and resale.

Tools observed

Attacker side

  • Phishing kit serving a copied library login panel
  • Hosted mailboxes on one lookalike domain per university
  • Bulk-sending tooling timed to semester start

Defender side

  • Mail gateway with first-contact and young-sender rules
  • Sandbox detonation of linked pages
  • DMARC report review for spoof attempts
Detection point

The winning detections were structural, not content-based: new-sender rules quarantined the wave on first contact, and lookalike domain monitors flagged the registrations weeks earlier. Content filters alone lagged, because the messages were plain and well written.

Response phases

Containment

  • Blocked the lookalike domains at gateway and resolver
  • Purged delivered copies from mailboxes
  • Forced password resets for exposed accounts

Eradication

  • Revoked sessions and tokens for affected identities
  • Checked for forwarding rules and OAuth grants left behind
  • Shared domains with peers and CERTs

Recovery

  • Multi-factor authentication (MFA) enrollment pushed for all users
  • Lookalike watch widened for the next semester
  • Library mails digitized with verified sender headers

Impact and consequences

Dozens of universities were hit across multiple semesters. Direct costs were modest per victim, but stolen credentials enabled deeper intrusions and were sold onward. The campaign proved that timing plus believable identity beats technical sophistication for bulk phishing.

Team response and communication

Security teams wrote short notices with real screenshots. They coordinated with the library to add verified banners to genuine mail. They also set a standing rule: library notices never ask for passwords. Help desk scripts were updated so reports became fast ticket templates.

Lessons learned

  • Time-based campaigns are predictable; pre-position blocks for known windows.
  • First-contact rules are worth more than perfect content filters.
  • MFA would have blocked most of the credential harvesting; enrollment is urgent, not optional.
  • Lookalike monitoring gives weeks of warning when brand tokens are watched.
  • Help desk scripts turn user reports into rapid, consistent containment.

Mitigation and Defense

Mail defenses are mature and affordable. The goal is a stack where attacker-owned mailboxes must beat authentication, reputation, and human reporting at once.

Home and small office

  • Turn on MFA for your mailbox first; it blocks the most common takeover paths
  • Read the full sender address, not just the name
  • Verify money or password requests by a channel you already know
