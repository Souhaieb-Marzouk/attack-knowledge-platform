T1585.001 Social Media Accounts

A friendly recruiter with a professional profile may not exist. Attackers create social media accounts that copy real people. They build the accounts up with posts and connections. Then they use them to reach your staff where they least expect an attack. This page explains how those fake identities are built and how defenders can catch them early.

Tactic: Resource Development Parent: T1585 ATT&CK: Enterprise v16 ATT&CK platform: PRE (pre-compromise) Version: 2.0 Reviewed: 2026-10-20

Contents

1

Overview

What this sub-technique is, why social platforms are attractive to attackers, and what this page teaches.

Social Media Accounts (T1585.001) means that an attacker creates an account on a social network and builds it into a believable person or company. The profile gets a name, a photo, a work history, and a small stream of normal posts. Often the identity copies a real employee of your organization, or a recruiter from a real company. The goal is simple: your staff should accept the profile as a person worth talking to.

This matters because social platforms are trusted spaces. People expect strangers to be recruiters, classmates, or vendors, not attackers. A connection request costs the attacker nothing, and platforms rarely demand strong identity proof. Once trust is built, the conversation usually moves to email or a chat app. There the actual attack happens: a malicious file, a credential-harvesting link, or a fraud request.

In the attack chain this sits in Resource Development, before initial access. It usually feeds T1566 Phishing through social engineering, or supports fraud and disinformation. The same personas feed T1656 Impersonation, and the handoff often lands on attacker-controlled T1102 Web Service endpoints. The closest documented pattern is the family of recruiter-lure campaigns reported by vendors since 2020, including Operation In(ter)ception and Operation Dream Job.

Animated Simulation

Follow a fake recruiter persona from creation to payload, with the defender view at every step. Hover nodes for details, or read the written story below.

The fake recruiter persona

Step 1 of 8

The same story in text

  1. Choosing the identity. The attacker copies a real recruiter's name and photo from an open profile. Or they invent a persona at a real company. Then they pick a target list from public staff pages.
  2. Creating the account. A new profile is registered through a VPN with a throwaway email. The provider sees a normal signup; the target sees nothing.
  3. Warming up. For days or weeks the persona posts small, harmless updates and adds dozens of real connections, so its numbers look human.
  4. Reaching out. Connection requests go to selected employees. The message praises their work and offers a role.
  5. Building trust. The chat stays polite and unhurried. Questions map the target's systems, using only public-sounding language.
  6. Moving channels. The recruiter suggests continuing by email or a messaging app, away from platform moderation, using a fresh mailbox from T1585.002.
  7. Delivery. A "job description" file or an "assessment" link arrives. It is the payload: malware or a credential-harvesting page.
  8. Discovery and response. The target reports the contact, or a colleague spots the copied identity. The SOC warns staff, reports the profile, and hunts for contacts.
3

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

How attackers approach persona building, in defensive terms. Nothing here is an operational guide; it is a model of what to expect.

3.1Why attackers love social platforms

Trust is built into the platform

Users accept connection requests from strangers daily. A recruiter persona matches that expectation perfectly. The attacker's real work is patience: a persona with three connections is suspicious, one with two hundred is not.

Target lists are public

Staff pages, conference talks, and press releases give attackers names, roles, and even tool choices. A persona can pitch a role that fits one exact person, which is far more convincing than bulk mail.

Moderation is slow

Platforms remove fake profiles, but reporting takes time and proof. Patient operators finish their outreach before any ban lands, then move to a fresh identity.

Attribution is weak

Persona accounts use scraped photos and invented names. Tying one account to a group needs evidence links across their infrastructure, like shared writing habits or reused hosting. That work takes investigators weeks.

3.2How a persona is built, step by step

  1. Pick the mold. Copy a real recruiter's identity, create a fictional one, or impersonate a specific employee. Each choice changes the risk to the victim differently.
  2. Gather materials. Photos from open profiles, a logo, a plausible history, and a phone or mail contact that survives a casual check.
  3. Register and age the account. Sign up from a clean IP, complete the profile fully, and let it rest. Age matters more than polish.
  4. Warm up with noise. Like posts, follow companies, add connections slowly. Activity patterns must look human to both users and platform checks.
  5. Select targets. Choose staff whose roles make the lure believable: engineers, HR, finance, executives.
  6. Engage and hand off. Chat on-platform, then move to email or chat apps, where the payload lands and moderation ends.

3.4Prerequisites

What the attacker needs

  • Clean IP space (a VPN, or a residential proxy that routes traffic through home IP addresses) to avoid signup blocks
  • Photos that pass reverse-image checks, often AI-generated today
  • A believable contact chain: email, phone, and sometimes a real website
  • Time: one to six weeks of warm-up for careful operators

What breaks the persona

  • Reverse-image search hitting the real owner of the photo
  • Employment checks: a call to the real company's main line
  • Profile age versus connection count mismatch
  • Pressure to move channels or open files quickly

The most common operational failure is impatience: connecting to targets before warm-up is done. Fast-connection personas are exactly what brand monitors and alert colleagues catch first.

4

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

What defenders can actually observe, which artifacts exist, and a triage flow for persona reports.

4.1How defenders can see it

People first

Most fake personas are found by employees: the photo is of someone famous, the company does not exist, or the request feels wrong. Make reporting easy and thank people publicly.

Brand monitoring

Services that watch for your company name, logo, and staff names on social platforms turn OSINT into alerts. Feed results into the SOC queue like any other alert.

When it touches your logs

The moment the persona hands off to email or a link, normal telemetry resumes: mail gateway, proxy, and DNS. Correlate reports with those channels.

4.2Artifacts this sub-technique leaves

Profile record

Creation date, handle, bio, and employer claim. Often a young profile claiming a senior role.

Media trail

Photos reused from real people or generated by AI; reverse-image search can expose both.

Connection graph

Growth pattern too fast for the claimed history, or clusters of strangers connected overnight.

Content history

Few posts, odd posting hours, or text copied from other sites.

Handoff artifacts

The email address or chat handle used later; this joins your infrastructure cluster.

Victim interaction

Screenshots and saved messages from staff; treat them as evidence and ask for full URLs.

4.3Where to look

4.4Why it matters and where it fits

A fake persona is the human face on top of infrastructure from T1583 and accounts from T1585.002 and T1585.003. Ignoring personas means ignoring the earliest stage of the intrusion. Responding well keeps trust high: staff who see fast, friendly action keep reporting.

4.5Triage flow for a persona report

  1. Acknowledge. Thank the reporter and confirm receipt the same day. Speed here keeps the reporting channel alive.
  2. Verify. Check the profile against your persona register, HR, and the claimed employer. Screenshot everything with timestamps.
  3. Enrich. Reverse-image the photos, note profile age and connection count, and search the handle on other platforms.
  4. Scope. Ask HR to list staff contacted; search mail and proxy logs for the handoff address or host if any.
  5. Act. Report the profile to the platform, warn matched staff with a short note, and add handoff indicators to blocklists.
  6. Follow up. Tell the reporter the outcome and watch for a new persona repeating the pattern within days.
Privacy note

Monitor public information about your brand and roles, not staff private accounts. Write the rule down: which tokens are watched, where, and who reviews the alerts. This keeps the program lawful and trusted.

Good practice

Publish a one-page Persona Reporting Charter: what to report, where, response time, and no-blame policy. It doubles as audit evidence and as the culture message that keeps reports coming.

5.2Threat intelligence

Social persona intelligence moves fast and lives mostly outside classic feeds. Your loop: collect public reports about persona campaigns. Analyze which target roles and industries match yours. Apply findings as staff warnings and monitor rules. Review which personas got through.

Collect

Vendor research on persona campaigns, researcher posts on X and Mastodon, platform transparency reports, and your own report queue.

Analyze

Cluster personas by photo reuse, bio text, target industries, and handoff addresses. Note the roles they pick; that predicts your exposure.

Apply

Send staff a short warning with the persona's pattern, not just a screenshot. Add handoff addresses to mail and proxy blocks.

5.4Logs and patterns

Personas leave few classic logs. The examples below show the event shapes you should normalize, so rules in 5.5 have fields to read.

SB-01 · Employee impersonation profile High

  • Log sources: brand monitoring alerts, staff reports, platform search snapshots
  • Key fields: platform, profile_url, profile_created, match_type, matched_employee
  • Normal: staff and alumni with real histories; partners listed on your website
  • Suspicious: new profile with a staff member's exact name and employer, few posts, fast connection growth
  • Detection logic: name plus employer match with profile age under 14 days, or any match reported by two sources
brand monitoring · alert example
{
  "alert_id": "BM-3120",
  "platform": "linkedin",
  "profile_url": "https://www.linkedin.com/in/d-keller-northwind",
  "profile_created": "2026-05-02",
  "match_type": "employee-name-and-employer",
  "matched_employee": "D. Keller (Head of Engineering)",
  "connections": 51,
  "posts": 2,
  "risk": "high",
  "reported_by": "brand-monitor-feed"
}

SB-02 · Recruiter persona delivers a payload High

  • Log sources: mail gateway (handoff messages), endpoint process creation, proxy
  • Key fields: sender, sender_domain_age_days, subject, attachment_type, process_parent, process_child
  • Normal: recruiting mail from corporate domains with standard formats and calendars
  • Suspicious: personal-domain or free-mail sender discussing a role, attachments like .iso, .img, .one, or macros; office apps spawning scripts
  • Detection logic: office application spawning a script interpreter, or archive or disk-image attachment from a first-seen sender

  "verdict": "malicious"

SB-03 · Brand impersonation for fraud Medium

  • Log sources: brand monitoring, support tickets, customer complaints via social channels
  • Key fields: platform, account_handle, account_created, logo_match, ticket_refs
  • Normal: verified support handles listed on your official site
  • Suspicious: lookalike handles replying to your customers, offering "support" that asks for payments or codes
  • Detection logic: handle similarity plus logo match, or support tickets referencing a non-official handle

SB-04 · Persona funnels to fresh phishing domain High

  • Log sources: proxy logs, DNS logs, mail logs for the handoff message
  • Key fields: user, url_host, host_first_seen_days, referer_domain, url_path
  • Normal: staff browsing job boards and company career pages on old, known domains
  • Suspicious: visit to a first-seen domain minutes after a social referral, path containing login or assessment keywords
  • Detection logic: referer from a social platform AND host age under 14 days AND human user

SB-05 · Executive impersonation persona High

  • Log sources: normalized mail gateway events, chat platform exports
  • Key fields: display_name, sender, sender_domain, corp_domains, sender_ip, recipients, subject

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

Six rule pairs covering the ranked threats. Normalize the fields once; the mapping notes list assumptions per source.

S-B1 · Impersonation profile on a social platform level: high

Sigma YAML
title: Young Social Profile Impersonating an Employee
id: 1d4f6a8c-2e3b-4a5d-9c7e-0f1a2b3c4d5f
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects a brand monitoring alert for a social media profile that
  matches an employee name and employer while the profile is younger
  than 14 days. Profiles like this are typically established with
  MITRE ATT&CK T1585.001 before recruiter-style targeting.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/001/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-03-02
logsource:
  category: application
  product: brand_monitoring
detection:
  selection_match:
    match_type|contains:
      - 'employee-name'
  selection_young:
    profile_age_days|lte: 14
  filter_verified:
    profile_verified: 'true'
  condition: selection_match and selection_young and not filter_verified
fields:
  - alert_id
  - platform
  - profile_url
  - profile_created
  - matched_employee
  - connections
falsepositives:
  - Real new hires whose profiles are not yet in the internal register
  - Namesakes with no employer match in the alert body
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585.001
  - attack.resource_development
Splunk SPL
index=osint sourcetype="brandmon:alert" match_type="*employee-name*"
| eval profile_age_days=round((now()-strptime(profile_created,"%Y-%m-%d"))/86400,1)
| where profile_age_days <= 14
| lookup verified_social_profiles profile_url OUTPUT verified
| where isnull(verified)
| stats values(platform) AS platforms, min(profile_age_days) AS youngest,
        values(matched_employee) AS staff, values(alert_id) AS alerts
        by profile_url
| table profile_url platforms youngest staff alerts
| sort + youngest
  • Mapping note: assumes any brand monitoring source normalized to the fields in 5.4. profile_age_days is computed in SPL from the creation date.
  • False positives: genuine hires before their profiles enter your register. Keep the register fresh; HR onboarding triggers the update.
  • Tuning: require employer match in the alert body, not name alone. Raise to critical if the matched employee is an executive.
  • Severity: high, because identity trust is the hardest control to replace.

S-B2 · Office document spawning a script interpreter level: high

Sigma YAML
title: Office Application Spawning Script Interpreter After Recruiter Contact
id: 6b8d0e21-4c5a-4f6b-8d2f-3a4b5c6d7e81
status: stable
description: |
  Detects a classic payload pattern delivered by recruiter personas:
  an Office application spawning a script interpreter or shell. This
  is the execution half of social media persona attacks (T1585.001
  feeding T1566 and T1204).
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/001/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1204/002/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-03-09
logsource:
  category: process_creation
  product: windows
detection:
  selection_parent:
    ParentImage|endswith:
      - '\WINWORD.EXE'
      - '\EXCEL.EXE'
      - '\POWERPNT.EXE'
      - '\ONENOTE.EXE'
  selection_child:
    Image|endswith:
      - '\powershell.exe'
      - '\pwsh.exe'
      - '\cmd.exe'
      - '\wscript.exe'
      - '\cscript.exe'
      - '\mshta.exe'
  filter_help:
    CommandLine|contains:
      - 'winword.exe /safe'
  condition: selection_parent and selection_child and not filter_help
fields:
  - Image
  - ParentImage
  - CommandLine
  - User
falsepositives:
  - Document automation and internal tooling that drives Office
  - Administrative scripts launched manually by IT from documents
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585.001
  - attack.execution
  - attack.t1204.002
Splunk SPL
index=edr sourcetype="edr:process" earliest=-1h
| search process_parent IN ("WINWORD.EXE","EXCEL.EXE","POWERPNT.EXE","ONENOTE.EXE")
| search process_name IN ("powershell.exe","pwsh.exe","cmd.exe","wscript.exe","cscript.exe","mshta.exe")
| search NOT cmdline="*winword.exe /safe*"
| stats count AS events, values(host) AS hosts, values(user) AS users,
        values(cmdline) AS commands by process_parent, process_name
| table process_parent process_name events hosts users commands
| sort - events
  • Mapping note: written for a generic EDR bridge; Sysmon Event ID 1 maps process_parent from ParentImage and process_name from Image.
  • False positives: legitimate document automation. Filter by service accounts and known automation paths.
  • Tuning: add OneNote to the parent list only if your users have it; watch for .one attachments in mail rules too.
  • Severity: high; this pattern precedes most persona-lure intrusions.

  - account_handle
  - account_created
  - customer_tickets
falsepositives:
  - Regional resellers authorized to use the logo
  - Community volunteers listed in your support docs
level: medium
tags:

Splunk SPL
index=osint sourcetype="brandmon:alert"
| search logo_match="true" OR bio_similarity>=0.85
| where isnotnull(customer_tickets)
| lookup official_support_handles handle AS account_handle OUTPUT status

  • Tuning: involve support leadership; they decide public replies while the SOC handles reporting and evidence.
  • Severity: medium; customer harm is real but direct compromise of your estate is rare from this path.

S-B4 · Social referral to a first-seen young host level: high

Sigma YAML
title: Social Referral to Newly Observed Host
id: 7f9a1c3e-5b6d-47f8-9a0b-2c3d4e5f6a71
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects a user following a link from a social platform to a domain
  first seen in proxy logs within the last 14 days. Captures the click
  moment where a persona established with MITRE ATT&CK T1585.001 hands
  off to fresh phishing infrastructure.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/001/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-03-23
logsource:
  category: proxy
detection:
  selection_referer:
    referer_domain|endswith:
      - 'linkedin.com'
      - 'x.com'
      - 'twitter.com'
      - 'facebook.com'
      - 'instagram.com'
      - 't.co'
  selection_suspicious_path:
    url_path|contains:
      - 'login'
      - 'signin'

      - 'assessment'
      - 'resume'
  filter_big_tech:
    url_host|endswith:
      - 'linkedin.com'
      - 'x.com'
      - 'facebook.com'
      - 'instagram.com'
  condition: selection_referer and selection_suspicious_path and not filter_big_tech
fields:
  - user
  - src_ip
  - url_host
  - url_path
  - host_first_seen_days
  - action
falsepositives:
  - Legitimate job boards and assessments on young domains
  - New vendor career portals
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1585.001
  - attack.initial_access

Splunk SPL
index=proxy sourcetype="proxy:access"
| search referer_domain IN ("linkedin.com","x.com","twitter.com","facebook.com","instagram.com","t.co")
| regex url_path="(?i)(login|signin|verify|assessment|resume)"
| search NOT url_host IN ("*.linkedin.com","linkedin.com","*.x.com","x.com","*.facebook.com","facebook.com","*.instagram.com","instagram.com")
| stats count AS visits, earliest(_time) AS first_seen, values(user) AS users,
        values(url_path) AS paths, max(host_first_seen_days) AS host_age
        by url_host
| where isnull(host_age) OR host_age <= 14
| table first_seen url_host host_age visits users paths
| sort - visits
  • Mapping note: referer_domain and host_first_seen_days assume enrichment. The host_age condition is an optional enrichment filter present only in the SPL; the Sigma rule intentionally fires on the referral pattern alone and stays broader. Without the age field, the SPL keeps every referral click. The NOT-list covers bare domains and subdomains, matching the Sigma endswith filter.
  • False positives: real job sites on fresh infrastructure. Ask HR for their current job boards and allowlist them.
  • Tuning: pair with S-B1 alerts: same day, same host equals near-certain campaign. Consider blocking unclassified hosts with social referers for 24 hours.
  • Severity: high; this is the moment of user compromise.

S-B6 · Persona-farm profile wave seen by proxy level: medium

Sigma YAML
title: Persona-Farm Profile Wave Seen by Proxy
id: 31997f17-60ca-4153-b5f3-a840eb08d048
status: experimental
description: |
  Detects several distinct users fetching profile-style URLs on major
  social platforms within seven days. Persona farms send direct
  messages to many staff at once, and the wave of profile checks shows
  up in proxy logs before any payload. The accounts behind such
  profiles are commonly established with MITRE ATT&CK T1585.001.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1585/001/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-20
logsource:
  category: proxy
detection:
  selection_platform:
    url_host|endswith:
      - 'linkedin.com'
      - 'x.com'
      - 'twitter.com'
      - 'facebook.com'
      - 'instagram.com'
  selection_profile:
    url|re: '/(in|profile)/[A-Za-z0-9_-]{3,}'
  condition: selection_platform and selection_profile | count() by url_host > 3
  timeframe: 7d
fields:
  - user
  - src_ip
  - url_host
  - url
falsepositives:
  - Recruiters and sales outreach whose profiles several staff view in one week
  - Shared background checks on the same public profile
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.t1585
  - attack.t1585.001
  - attack.resource_development
Splunk SPL
index=proxy sourcetype="proxy:access"
| regex url="/(in|profile)/[A-Za-z0-9_-]{3,}"
| where match(url_host, "(linkedin\.com|x\.com|twitter\.com|facebook\.com|instagram\.com)$")
| stats dc(user) AS users, count AS requests, values(user) AS user_list,
        earliest(_time) AS first_seen by url_host
| where users > 3
| table first_seen url_host users requests user_list
| sort - users
  • Mapping note: url and url_host follow the 5.4 proxy normalization. The platform filter uses match() with an anchored regex, mirroring the Sigma endswith list. Sigma counts profile-style requests per host; the SPL threshold uses distinct users, the quieter variant tuned for the persona-farm pattern. Both counts appear in the SPL output, so you can switch. Profile age is not visible to the proxy; host_first_seen_days enrichment is optional and is not part of the threshold here.
  • False positives: recruiters and sales outreach whose profiles several staff view in the same week.
  • Tuning: group by url instead of url_host to track a single profile, or raise the threshold for your size. Best paired with S-B1 brand-monitor alerts in the same week.
  • Severity: medium; the wave warns that several staff are being worked in parallel, before any payload lands.

Real-World Case Study

Operation In(ter)ception, reported by ESET in 2020: fake LinkedIn recruiters targeted aerospace and military employees across Europe and the Middle East. Details are simplified from public reporting for teaching.

Context

Beginning in June 2020, operators created recruiter personas targeting employees of military, aerospace, and government suppliers. The personas were patient and professional, mirroring real recruiting workflows. Suspicion fell on the Lazarus group, based on tooling and infrastructure overlaps.

Victim experience

An engineer is offered a great job. The "assessment" arrives as a document. On Macs it exploited a document vulnerability; on Windows it dropped a backdoor through a chain of files. Either way, the first click was the whole intrusion.

Timeline

  • June 2020Fake recruiter profiles connect with staff at defense and aerospace firms. Profiles carry real-looking histories and company ties.
  • June to JulyConversations move to email. The attacker sends a document described as a job description or skills test.
  • On openThe document runs a payload chain. Some targets receive follow-up files posing as salary details.
  • Weeks afterBackdoors beacon to attacker infrastructure; selected victims are escalated for data access.
  • September 2020ESET publishes the campaign; defenders exchange indicators across ISACs.

Attack chain

  1. Recruiter personas created and warmed up on a professional network (T1585.001).
  2. Targets picked from public defense-industry footprints (T1593).
  3. Trust built on-platform; conversation moved to email backed by fresh mail infrastructure (T1585.002).
  4. Malicious documents delivered and opened (T1566, T1204.002).
  5. Platform-specific payloads installed backdoors (T1071 for command and control).
  6. Follow-on access and collection on selected targets.

Tools observed

Attacker side

  • ISO disk images (ISO = a disk-image file format used to deliver malware)
  • LNK shortcut files inside the images that launch the payload chain
  • Custom backdoors for command and control traffic

Defender side

  • Mail gateway attachment analysis
  • Sandbox detonation of delivered documents
  • Proxy logs for beacon and callback checks
Detection point

Two independent detections worked: employees reporting odd recruiter messages, and endpoint telemetry catching the document-payload chain. Neither required seeing the profile creation, which happened weeks earlier on the platform side.

Response phases

Containment

  • Reported and documented the persona profiles
  • Blocked handoff sender domains and payload hosts
  • Isolated machines that opened the files

Eradication

  • EDR sweep for the backdoor family
  • Credential resets for affected users
  • Hunt for the same delivery pattern in mail history

Recovery

  • Clean machines restored; monitoring kept on the cluster
  • Staff advisory with the persona pattern
  • Attachment policy tightened for high-risk roles

Impact and consequences

The campaign reached dozens of organizations. Where payloads ran, attackers held footholds inside sensitive suppliers. The visible costs were response time, resets, and policy work; the strategic cost was exposure of defense-industry data. The campaign returned in later waves, which is typical for persona-based operations.

Team response and communication

Security teams published short internal advisories with screenshots and one behavior rule: verify recruiters through official channels. HR and recruiting teams were briefed first, then engineering leads. Executives received a weekly summary until hunts came back clean.

Lessons learned

  • Persona campaigns run in waves; save the cluster and watch for its return.
  • Public staff pages feed targeting; trim what you publish and teach staff to trim.
  • Documents from "recruiters" need the same suspicion as invoices from "finance".
  • Platform reporting works but is slow; your blocklist is the faster control.
  • Cross-industry sharing (ISACs) shortens everyone's response; participate.
