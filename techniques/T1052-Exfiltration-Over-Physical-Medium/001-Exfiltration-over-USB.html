T1052.001 Exfiltration over USB

This is the USB (Universal Serial Bus) sub-technique of exfiltration over physical medium. Data is copied onto USB storage and then carried across the room or across the world. The classic example is the path out of an air-gapped network — the drive itself becomes the network.

Tactic: Exfiltration ID: T1052.001 Parent: T1052 Platforms: Linux · Windows · macOS Type: Sub-technique Version 1.3 (technique) · created 11 March 2020 · modified 02 September 2026

What happens

Malware or a person copies files onto USB storage. The data then leaves with the device, not through any network sensor.

Chain position

The final exfiltration step. It is usually paired with a collection technique such as T1005 or archived data from T1560.

Biggest risk

There is no network trail on the isolated side. No egress logs, no data-loss prevention (DLP) inspection on the wire — the drive is the network.

Detect with

USBSTOR registry writes, file-writes on removable volumes (Sysmon 11 — Microsoft's System Monitor logging), and same-serial-on-two-hosts correlation in the SIEM (Security Information and Event Management).

Overview

What this technique is, why it matters even in 2026, and what this guide will teach you.

Exfiltration over USB means that data leaves an organisation on removable storage. Malware, or a person, copies files to a USB stick, an external drive, or a storage-capable gadget. Someone then carries that device past every network control you own. The technique is T1052.001 in MITRE ATT&CK, a sub-technique of T1052, Exfiltration Over Physical Medium.

Most exfiltration paths leave traces: proxy logs, domain name system (DNS) queries, flow records, mail gateway events. USB exfiltration leaves almost nothing on the wire. On a normal corporate network it is one quiet channel among many. On an air-gapped network — a network with no internet route at all — it is often the only practical way out. That is why the most valuable targets in the world (defence, energy, government) still lose data this way, as the USBStealer case study shows.

Note — what is an air gap? An air-gapped network is physically disconnected from the internet and from other networks. People move data in and out on removable media, usually under strict rules. If those rules are weak, or if malware rides on the media, the gap is a door, not a wall.

Where it sits in the attack chain

USB exfiltration is the last step. Before it, an attacker must collect data and usually stage it. The table below shows a typical chain. Notice how the two halves of the story live on different networks — that split is the heart of this technique.

The written story — all 8 steps

  1. Two disconnected worlds. The picture starts quiet. An internet segment and an air-gapped segment sit side by side with no route between them. A clean USB stick waits in the middle. What the defender sees: nothing at all — both networks sit inside their normal baseline, and no sensor can see the gap that is about to be crossed. Evidence: none; silence is the baseline.
  2. Malware waits on both sides. Two small implants are already resident: one on the air-gapped host, one on the drop host. Neither calls out to the internet. Each simply polls for a newly mounted volume, in the style of the USBStealer toolset. What the defender sees: a rare service registered on both hosts and a first-seen binary running from AppData — weak signals that are easy to miss without baseline tooling. Evidence: rare service (Sysmon 13), first-seen binary in AppData.
  3. The stick goes in (air-gapped side). A person plugs the stick into the air-gapped host. The implant wakes, enumerates the volumes, and reads the staged documents folder on the air-gapped file server over the isolated LAN (local area network). What the defender sees: a new USBSTOR serial number for this host and a hidden process opening a handle to E:\. Evidence: USBSTOR registry write (Sysmon 13), hidden process reading the volume.
  4. Files packed into a hidden container. The implant copies the staged files and packs them into a single container encrypted with XOR (a simple, reversible cipher) and given an odd extension — the "hidenc" pattern reported for USBStealer. Dozens of file writes land on the removable volume within seconds. What the defender sees: a sharp burst of Sysmon Event 11 file-create events on the removable volume, with an extension nobody has seen before. Evidence: Sysmon 11 burst on E:\, odd file extension.
  5. The courier walks. The person picks up the stick and walks across the room — or across the world. The gap is crossed by shoes, not packets. What the defender sees: nothing, anywhere. No log, no alert, no flow record. This silence is the defining problem of T1052.001 and the reason correlation matters so much. Evidence: none — the gap in telemetry is itself the clue.
  6. The stick goes in (internet side). The same stick is plugged into the drop host. The second implant reads the container from the volume. What the defender sees: a USBSTOR write plus a file-create on E:\ — but now on a different host than in step 3. Two hosts, one serial. Evidence: USBSTOR on second host, file-create on E:\.
  7. The container leaves the network. The drop host uploads the container to the attacker server over HTTPS. To the proxy it looks like a normal web upload. What the defender sees: a large upload to a rare domain, starting shortly after USB activity on the same host. Evidence: proxy upload record, rare destination domain.
  8. SOC connects the dots. The SIEM joins the two USBSTOR records by device serial: the same physical drive touched two hosts within 48 hours. A case opens, responders pull both host timelines plus the proxy record, and device control is tightened on both segments. What the defender sees: the correlation alert that finally makes the invisible relay visible. Evidence: same-serial-on-two-hosts correlation alert, device-control policy change.
Good practice. Notice which steps produced evidence: 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8. Only step 5 — the physical walk — is invisible. Every detection later in this guide aims at the visible steps, and the correlation in step 8 is what turns separate small signals into one clear case.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

Why a thumb drive still works, what the attack needs, and how to rehearse it in a lab you own.

When, where, and why attackers use it

Attackers pick USB exfiltration for two reasons. First, it bridges air gaps: if the target network has no internet route, a pocket-sized device is the only practical channel. Second, it produces no egress logs on the isolated side. Network DLP, proxies, and DNS monitoring all stay blind. It applies wherever files are readable and a port accepts media: air-gapped estates, classified networks, and ordinary offices with weak device control. Even on normal networks, a few gigabytes on a stick is invisible compared to a cloud upload.

MITRE lists seven procedure examples on the ATT&CK page for this technique. Agent.btz (S0092) wrote a thumb.dd log file to every connected USB drive. Machete (S0409) copied files from every drive into a hidden removable-media folder. Remsec (S0125) moved data from air-gapped networks via removable devices. Mustang Panda (G0129) used a customised PlugX variant to exfiltrate documents from air-gapped networks. SPACESHIP (S0035), Tropic Trooper (G0081) and USBStealer (S0136) complete the published set.

What the attack needs (prerequisites)

  • A way in on both sides. Either an implant, or a trusted insider, on the isolated network and on an internet-connected host that can upload.
  • A carrier. A person, or a device that physically moves — the stick itself can even carry the second-stage implant for the internet side.
  • A container plan. Collected files are usually packed into one encrypted blob so the copy burst is short and the contents are hidden from casual inspection.
  • Patience. Air-gap relays run for months. The stick may cross the gap only a few times, or only when a legitimate transfer is expected anyway.
⚠ LAB-ONLY — wait-for-volume loop (run on machines you own). This PowerShell snippet shows the implant behaviour: it sleeps until a removable volume (DriveType 2) appears, then exits. It writes nothing and hides nothing. Run it only on a lab VM you own — never on a work or family machine. The waiting loop: while($true){ $v = Get-CimInstance Win32_LogicalDisk -Filter 'DriveType=2'; if($v){ break }; Start-Sleep -Seconds 3 }.
⚠ LAB-ONLY — copy files onto the volume. To generate the telemetry discussed in section 5, copy a few files onto a lab USB stick. Use Copy-Item C:\LabStage\* E:\LabOut\ on your own lab VM. Then watch what Sysmon 11, Security 4663 and your device-control agent record. The point is to learn your own log footprint, not to hide it.
⚠ LAB-ONLY — why a container file is used. Attackers pack many files into one encrypted blob (reported XOR containers such as USBStealer's "hidenc" files). To understand the detection angle, create a plainly named single archive, e.g. Compress-Archive C:\LabStage\* E:\lab-bundle.zip, on your lab VM and observe the single big Sysmon 11 write. Do not implement encryption or hide the file; the educational value is in the log pattern, not the concealment.

Common variations

  • Collect-from-inserted-drives (the USBStealer pattern): the implant does not write to the drive — it reads files from whatever drive the victim inserts, into a hidden container. The victim does the carrying, unknowingly.
  • Copy-to-drive (the insider pattern): a person with legitimate access copies files on purpose. This is threat 1 in section 5.3 and is by far the most common real-world case.
  • Data in slack space or odd partitions: conceptually, small amounts of data can be tucked into areas of the device that file systems report as empty. Defenders should know the concept; this guide deliberately gives no implementation recipe.
  • Internet-side hand-off via sync tools: the drop host may relay the container through a personal cloud-sync folder or webmail. That moves the final upload from a rare domain to a trusted cloud domain, and it deliberately evades proxy tuning (see GoldenJackal in section 5.2).

Mistakes attackers make (and defenders should hunt for)

  • The same serial crosses twice. A stick used on both sides of the gap creates the same USBSTOR serial on two hosts — the single best correlation signal you have (rule 5.5-3).
  • Container timestamps leak context. Files inside a container often carry original timestamps, and the container's own creation time lands in USN journal and file-system artifacts, giving investigators a clock.
  • The implant touches the volume immediately on insertion. Immediate plug-and-play (PnP)-to-process activity is loud. Implants that wait a random 2–10 minutes blend in far better — so detections should look for volume access, not just insertion time.
Danger — ethics. Everything on this page exists for defence. Practice only on systems you own or are authorised to test. Creating real USB collection implants, or using them against any system you do not own, is illegal in most jurisdictions and is not covered here.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

What USB exfiltration actually looks like in your telemetry, the artifacts it leaves on both sides of the gap, and a five-step triage flow.

Here is the uncomfortable truth: at the exact moment data leaves on a stick, you see almost nothing. Defenders therefore do not hunt for the "exfiltration event" — they hunt for the moments before and after it. A device arrives (plug-and-play and USBSTOR events). A process touches the volume (process-create, file-create, handle audits). Files land on or leave the volume (Sysmon 11). On the internet side, a host that just saw a stick then uploads something (proxy, cloud access security broker (CASB), egress logs). Put those fragments together and the relay becomes visible.

Artifacts left behind

  • USBSTOR registry keysEvery mass-storage device writes a key under SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Enum\USBSTOR\. It records vendor, product and serial. It is per-host, persistent, and the join key for cross-host correlation.
  • PnP / DriverFrameworks eventsMicrosoft-Windows-Kernel-PnP Configuration events (device configured or started, plus 420/430 removals) and DriverFrameworks-UserMode 20001/20003 show device arrival and class.
  • Sysmon 1 / 11 / 12 / 13Process-create (what touched the volume), file-create (what landed on E:\), and registry-set (USBSTOR writes) — the workhorse events here.
  • Security 4663 / 4656Object-access audit records for handles opened against files on the removable volume, if "Removable Storage" auditing is enabled.
  • Device-control agent logsEvery block, read-only allow, or encryption check on the device, with device class, serial and (on good agents) data classification.
  • File-system tracesUSN (update sequence number) journal entries record volume writes. Jump lists and LNK shortcut files show files opened before copying. $MFT (master file table) timestamps survive on a seized stick.
  • Internet-side egressProxy and secure web gateway (SWG), CASB, DNS and firewall logs for uploads that follow USB activity on the same host within a short window.

Where to look, and why it matters

Look on both sides. The air-gapped segment is often monitored least, yet it is where the data is created. Build a simple inventory of every USB serial that has ever touched every host (a nightly USBSTOR sweep is enough), and keep it for at least a year. That inventory turns two boring log lines into one incident. It also matters for compliance. Media controls are explicit requirements in HIPAA (the US health-data law, 164.310(d)), ISO/IEC 27001 A.8.12, and NIST SP 800-53 MP-7 (see section 5.1).

In the attack chain, this technique is the exit. If you see collection behaviour (archive creation, mass file reads) on an isolated host and then a stick arrives, the chain is nearly complete. That makes the USB signals some of the highest-value, lowest-volume alerts you can build.

Triage flow — five steps

  1. Flag the rare process. Did an unusual or first-seen binary, or a scripting host, start around the time the volume appeared? Pull Sysmon 1 for that host.
  2. Check for the volume burst. Any Sysmon 11 cluster on the removable drive letter within minutes of insertion? Grab the file names and the writing process.
  3. Run the serial inventory. Take the USBSTOR serial and search it across all hosts. If it appears on two segments — this is now a suspected air-gap relay, escalate.
  4. Match internet-side egress. On the internet-side host, look for uploads (proxy, CASB, DNS for new domains) in the 24 hours after the stick was read. This confirms the data left.
  5. Scope and act. Identify the data owners, seize the device, preserve both hosts' artifacts, and review device-control mode. Containment guidance is in section 7.
Good practice — log retention is part of detection. USB evidence is tiny in volume but decisive in value. Give USBSTOR, Sysmon 11/13 and device-control logs the same retention you give authentication logs. A 48-hour-old serial match is worthless if last month's events are already gone.

5.2Threat intelligence

Good USB-exfiltration intelligence answers three questions: who has done this, how did the transfer work, and what did they touch on disk? Build your picture like this:

  • Primary sources. The MITRE ATT&CK site and its STIX/TAXII feeds (the standard machine-readable formats for sharing threat data), for the technique, tool and group objects used below.
  • Vendor research. ESET, Kaspersky, Microsoft, Mandiant, CrowdStrike and Red Canary publish the deepest air-gap reporting; ESET's work on USBStealer and GoldenJackal is the reference reading for this page.
  • OSINT (open-source intelligence) streams. abuse.ch, Any.run, malware-traffic-analysis.net and vx-underground surface the internet-side half (uploads, domains, samples).
  • Commercial feeds. Recorded Future and VirusTotal for domain reputation checks on the rare-upload signal in 5.4 (T3 and T6).
  • ISACs (Information Sharing and Analysis Centers). MS-ISAC, FS-ISAC, H-ISAC, E-ISAC and ICS-ISAC (the industrial control systems one) share sector-specific air-gap incidents that never reach the press.
  • Internal telemetry. Your own past incidents, honeypots and EDR (endpoint detection and response) history — the highest-relevance feed you own. Every stick ever plugged in is a data point.

Use the classic loop: collect (pull feeds and vendor reports on schedule), analyse (extract the patterns: which file extensions, which registry paths, which upload behaviours, which device classes), then apply (turn each pattern into a detection object in 5.5 and a hunting query for the serial inventory). Review the loop quarterly; USB tradecraft changes slowly but does change.

Confidence levels used in this guide

  • High — multiple independent vendor reports or official documents describe the behaviour.
  • Medium — one credible source, or partial telemetry; treat as plausible and test in your estate.
  • Low — limited or unconfirmed reporting; useful for context, not for building alerts.

T1 — Insider copies restricted files to USB

Log sources & key fields: Sysmon 11 (file-create: Image, TargetFilename, User), Security 4663 (object access: ProcessName, ObjectName, AccessList), device-control agent (device_serial, action, file_path, classification).

Normal behaviour: occasional, small copies to approved or encrypted devices; users copy presentations or installers. Suspicious behaviour: a burst of files from a sensitive share to a volume, archive files (zip/7z) created on the volume, copies by a user who never used USB before, or any copy to a device that is not on the approved list.

Detection logic: alert when a file-create burst (N files or M MB within minutes) on a removable-volume path comes from a user or host with no recent USB history, or when the target device is unapproved.

T2 — USBStealer-pattern collection from inserted drives

Log sources & key fields: Sysmon 1 (process-create: Image, CommandLine, ParentImage, User, Hashes), Sysmon 3 (network) to prove the process is silent, PnP arrival times (DeviceInstanceId), and rare file extensions observed on volumes via Sysmon 11.

Normal behaviour: users run visible tools; PowerShell from C:\Program Files or signed admin scripts. Suspicious behaviour: a process whose command line polls for volumes — WMI (Windows Management Instrumentation) classes such as Win32_LogicalDisk, or Get-Volume with DriveType=2 — started from an AppData path, running hidden, and starting right after a mass-storage device arrives.

Detection logic: alert on process-create where the image lives in a user-writable path and the command line enumerates removable volumes, especially when the process started within minutes of a device-arrival event on the same host.

T3 — USB container smuggled across an air gap

Log sources & key fields: Sysmon 13 (registry-set: TargetObject, Details) for USBSTOR on both hosts, Sysmon 11 on both volumes, proxy/SWG (bytes_out, cs_host), SIEM time as the join dimension. The detection is cross-host: the same device serial appearing on two hosts.

Normal behaviour: a stick's serial appears on one host, maybe two in the same office segment. Suspicious behaviour: the same serial on hosts in different segments (air-gapped vs internet) within 48 hours — or any stick that appears first on the air-gapped side and whose matching internet-side host then uploads a large file.

Detection logic: group USBSTOR registry-set events by extracted device serial; raise an alert when one serial is written by two or more distinct hosts inside the correlation window, and attach the Sysmon 11 and proxy records of both hosts to the case.

T4 — Worm copies staged data to every inserted drive

Log sources & key fields: Sysmon 11 (TargetFilename ending in autorun.inf), Sysmon 1 for the copying process, count of small file-writes per volume, and USN journal churn. DriverFrameworks 20001/20003 add device context.

Normal behaviour: almost nothing writes autorun.inf in 2026 — modern installers do not use it. Suspicious behaviour: any creation of autorun.inf on a volume, plus many small writes in one session (the worm dropping its payload file-by-file).

Detection logic: alert on any file-create named autorun.inf on a removable path, and secondarily on >50 small file-creates to a single volume by one process within five minutes.

T5 — Covert "badUSB-class" device used as portable storage

Log sources & key fields: Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-PnP/Configuration events (400/410 configured/started, 420/430 removed: DeviceInstanceId), DriverFrameworks-UserMode 20001/20003, and the parent/child relation between the composite device and its MI_00/MI_01 interfaces.

Normal behaviour: composite devices exist (a headset with buttons, a phone in USB mode), but each interface matches one expected class. Suspicious behaviour: one physical device whose instance paths include both a HID (Human Interface Device)/keyboard interface and a mass-storage (USBSTOR) child, especially if the same pattern was never seen on the estate before.

Detection logic: alert when a device-arrival event for a mass-storage child shares its parent serial with a keyboard/HID interface event within seconds, or when a USBSTOR instance path contains an MI_ function suffix that your inventory has never seen.

T6 — Personal cloud-sync folder copied to USB before travel

Log sources & key fields: device-control / endpoint-DLP agent (action, device_type, device_serial, file_path, classification, policy), CASB logs for the personal-tenant sync (actor, object_count, tenant_type), Sysmon 11 as corroboration.

Normal behaviour: business-approved sync tenants; encrypted corporate media allowed by policy. Suspicious behaviour: copies of a folder tagged Confidential (or matching DLP classifiers) to an unencrypted removable device, hours before a travel booking or from a new country login.

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

One complete Sigma rule and one matching Splunk SPL query per threat from 5.3. Assumptions: Sysmon events arrive in index=sysmon as sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational. Windows event logs arrive in index=wineventlog. The DLP agent arrives in index=dlp. Rename indexes, sourcetypes and lookup tables to match your environment — the logic, not the names, is the point.

Rule 1 (T1) — Suspicious File Write Burst to a Removable Volume

Sigma — file burst on removable volume
title: Suspicious File Write Burst to a Removable Volume
id: 3f6b9d2e-8a51-4c97-b2e4-19d0c7a53f41
status: stable
description: Detects a burst of file-create events on a removable-volume drive letter within
    five minutes, the signature of an insider bulk copy or an implant packing data onto a stick.
references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1052/001/
author: ATT&CK Guide Project (defensive)
date: 2026-01-15
logsource:
    category: file_event
    product: windows
    service: sysmon
detection:
    selection:
        EventID: 11
        TargetFilename|re: '^[D-Z]:\\'
    timeframe: 5m
    condition: selection | count() by Computer, User > 20
fields:
    - EventID
    - Computer
    - User
    - Image
    - TargetFilename
falsepositives:
    - Approved bulk transfers to encrypted corporate media
    - Backup, imaging or provisioning tools writing to removable targets
level: high
tags:
    - attack.exfiltration
    - attack.t1052.001
Splunk SPL — same detection
index=sysmon sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational EventCode=11
| regex TargetFilename="^[D-Z]:\\\\"
| bin _time span=5m
| stats count AS file_writes dc(TargetFilename) AS unique_files values(Image) AS writing_processes
    values(User) AS users by Computer, User, _time
| where file_writes > 20

Mapping note: the SPL mirrors the Sigma logic exactly: same removable-volume regex, same 5-minute bin, same count threshold, and the same Computer+User grouping. Optional precision add-on: build an approved_usb_volumes lookup (host → approved removable volumes) from your device-control inventory, then append | lookup approved_usb_volumes Computer OUTPUT approved | where isnull(approved) OR approved=0 to silence approved devices — that is an extra filter the plain Sigma rule does not have.

False positives: provisioning or imaging jobs writing many files to USB; a legitimate hand-off of a large approved dataset; users of approved encrypted drives during migration weeks.

Tuning: raise the threshold before lowering signal (start at 20, adjust per baseline); exclude known imaging hosts; require the volume to be seen by Event 13 in the same hour to confirm it is truly removable rather than a mapped drive.

Severity: high — single copies are noise, bursts are the behaviour, and volume is what an exfil looks like.

Rule 2 (T2) — Suspicious PowerShell Polling for Removable Volumes

Sigma — volume-polling implant
title: Suspicious PowerShell Polling for Removable Volumes
id: 5c8e2a74-9d31-4f6b-a7c8-2e40b9d16f72
status: stable
description: Detects a scripting host whose command line enumerates logical disks looking for
    removable volumes (DriveType 2) from a user context — the waiting behaviour of USBStealer-style implants.
references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1052/001/

    product: windows
    service: sysmon
detection:
    selection_img:
        EventID: 1
        Image|endswith: '\powershell.exe'
    selection_cmd:
        CommandLine|contains:
            - 'Win32_LogicalDisk'
            - 'Get-Volume'
            - 'DriveType=2'
            - 'DriveType = 2'
    filter_system:
        User|startswith: 'NT AUTHORITY'
    condition: selection_img and selection_cmd and not filter_system
fields:
    - EventID
    - Computer
    - User
    - Image
    - CommandLine
    - ParentImage
falsepositives:
    - Storage-admin scripts and helpdesk tooling that list volumes
    - Inventory agents using WMI disk queries
level: high
tags:
    - attack.exfiltration
    - attack.collection
    - attack.t1052.001
Splunk SPL — same detection
index=sysmon sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational EventCode=1 Image="*\\powershell.exe"
    (CommandLine="*Win32_LogicalDisk*" OR CommandLine="*Get-Volume*" OR CommandLine="*DriveType=2*" OR CommandLine="*DriveType = 2*")
    User!="NT AUTHORITY*"
| stats count AS matches first(_time) AS first_seen last(_time) AS last_seen
    values(ParentImage) AS parents by Computer, User, CommandLine

Mapping note: both versions fire on the first matching process — a PowerShell image, one of the four command-line variants, and a non-system user (the not filter_system clause). The stats block only tidies the output; there is deliberately no threshold. The SPL keeps ParentImage so the analyst can see who spawned the loop. Extend the image list with wscript.exe, cscript.exe and cmd.exe if your estate shows them used for the same purpose.

False positives: documented storage-admin or asset-inventory scripts; a helpdesk tool enumerating drives. Keep an allow-list of their exact command lines and hash-pin them.

Tuning: require user-context execution (the not filter_system clause); if alerts fire on inventory tools, filter by image path (signed vendor directory) rather than by command-line keywords; add -w hidden or AppData paths as secondary filters for precision.

Severity: high — this is the waiting half of the air-gap relay; catching it collapses the whole chain before any data moves.

Rule 3 (T3) — Same USB Device Serial Written on Multiple Hosts

Sigma — USBSTOR write (correlation watchlist pattern)
title: Same USB Device Serial Written on Multiple Hosts
id: 9a41c6b8-5f72-4e30-b8d9-71c4e0a2d583
status: stable
description: Detects a USBSTOR registry-set event whose device serial appears on more than one host.
    Plain Sigma evaluates single events and cannot correlate across hosts, so this rule matches the serial
    against a watchlist (value_list) of serials already seen on two or more hosts; that list is produced
    by the Splunk SPL below and refreshed on schedule. The full live correlation runs in the SIEM.
references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1052/001/
    - https://attack.mitre.org/software/S0125/
author: ATT&CK Guide Project (defensive)
date: 2026-01-15
logsource:
    category: registry_set
    product: windows
    service: sysmon
detection:
    selection:
        EventID: 13
        TargetObject|contains: 'USBSTOR\Disk'
    condition: selection
fields:
    - EventID
    - Computer
    - TargetObject
    - Details
falsepositives:
    - Shared docking-station drives moved deliberately between hosts under an approved procedure
    - Multi-host imaging rigs
level: high
tags:
    - attack.exfiltration
    - attack.t1052.001
Splunk SPL — cross-host correlation by serial
index=sysmon sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational EventCode=13 TargetObject="*USBSTOR\\Disk*"
| rex field=TargetObject "USBSTOR\\\\(?<usb_dev>[^\\\\]+)\\\\(?<usb_raw>[^\\\\]+)"
| eval usb_serial=mvindex(split(usb_raw,"&"),0)
| bin _time span=48h
| stats dc(Computer) AS host_count values(Computer) AS hosts min(_time) AS first_seen max(_time) AS last_seen
    values(usb_dev) AS device by usb_serial, _time
| where host_count > 1
Splunk SPL — alternative: transaction form
index=sysmon sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational EventCode=13 TargetObject="*USBSTOR\\Disk*"
| rex field=TargetObject "USBSTOR\\\\(?<usb_dev>[^\\\\]+)\\\\(?<usb_raw>[^\\\\]+)"
| eval usb_serial=mvindex(split(usb_raw,"&"),0)
| transaction usb_serial maxspan=48h
| where eventcount > 1 AND dc(Computer) > 1

Mapping note: the SPL extracts the serial from the USBSTOR path, then groups by serial inside fixed 48-hour buckets; dc(Computer) > 1 is the whole detection. If your forwarder names the host field differently (some use host, others Computer), adjust the dc() call. The Sigma rule stays single-event by design — feed the multi-host serial list back as a watchlist to raise its precision.

False positives: approved media that legitimately travels between hosts (kiosks, imaging rigs, shared presentation sticks); a docked external drive moved during desk changes.

Tuning: keep an allow-list of approved travelling devices by serial; widen or narrow the 48-hour bin to your media-movement reality; when a hit is confirmed on an air-gapped segment, treat it as a likely relay (see case study) and pull both hosts' Sysmon 11 timelines.

Severity: high — a two-host serial is the single strongest indicator of a physical-medium relay and deserves an automatic case.

Real-World Case Study

USBStealer: the documented APT28 toolset that collected files from USB drives on air-gapped networks. Based on public vendor reporting — an illustrative composite, not one victim's file.

Note — sources and honesty. This case study is an illustrative composite based on public vendor reporting: ESET's paper "An Island in the Sea of Unknowns" and BAE Systems' 2016 analysis, later reflected in MITRE ATT&CK entry S0136. Operational details below follow that reporting; details the vendors did not publish are marked as unknown rather than invented. It does not describe any single named victim.

Context

Between 2013 and 2015, the APT28 activity group (also tracked as Fancy Bear) operated a two-part toolset against high-value targets inside Russian government, military and scientific networks, including air-gapped segments. ESET named the toolset USBStealer (ATT&CK software ID S0136). Its purpose was simple and audacious: harvest documents from machines that had no internet connection at all, using the victims' own USB sticks as the transport.

The attack chain

The toolset came in two halves. On air-gapped hosts, a first-stage implant registered quietly and then waited — sometimes for a long time — until the user inserted a USB stick. When a volume appeared, it enumerated the files on the drive and copied selected documents into a hidden container encrypted with XOR and a fixed "hidenc"-style naming pattern, written onto the stick itself. On internet-connected machines, a second-stage implant waited the same way; when a stick arrived, it read the container and uploaded it to the operators. Neither implant called home on its own, which is exactly why both stayed invisible for so long. Public reporting does not fully document initial access; the vendors state the components appeared on both sides of the gap and that the sticks moved by ordinary human routines.

  • 2013

    First-stage implants are deployed on air-gapped machines in targeted Russian networks. They register no service visible to users and wait for USB insertions.

  • 2013 – 2015

    Quiet collection phase. Every inserted stick is enumerated; matching documents are packed into XOR "hidenc" containers on the media. Internet-side implants read and forward containers whenever the same sticks (or others) reach connected machines.

  • Mid-2015

    The activity is discovered and disrupted. By this point the toolset has had roughly two years of undisturbed collection across multiple organisations.

  • 2016

    ESET and BAE Systems publish their analyses. ATT&CK later records the toolset as S0136 under T1052.001, making USB-based exfiltration from air-gapped networks a documented, repeatable tradecraft pattern.

Detection point

The decisive join was physical media appearing on both sides of a gap. The same stick that received a container on the air-gapped side surfaced on an internet-connected machine, and the "hidenc" containers themselves were odd files on removable volumes with unusual XOR-encrypted structure. Either signal alone was a curiosity; together they were an incident. This is precisely the same-serial-on-two-hosts correlation automated by rule 5.5-3, plus the odd-extension file-create rule 1 — the public tradecraft maps directly onto the detections in this guide.

Containment and eradication

Responders physically seized and quarantined suspect media, blocked removable storage on both segments, and isolated the implicated hosts on each network — two response processes, because the networks were administered separately. Eradication required hunting the implants on both sides: first-stage components on the air-gapped hosts and second-stage components on internet-connected machines, re-imaging where confidence was low. Recovery meant restoring controlled media procedures (sanitised dedicated sticks, logged transfers) before any removable storage was allowed back.

Impact and lessons learned

The impact was the theft of documents from isolated networks over a period of years — the worst possible trade: the air gap protected connectivity, not data. The team response was complicated by the divided nature of air-gapped estates: two IT staffs, two monitoring stacks, one incident. Communication between network owners had to be established mid-crisis.

  • Inventory serials across every segment. The join key that would have caught this early — the same device serial on two hosts — only exists if you keep USBSTOR history for all networks, including the air-gapped one.
  • Watch hidden containers on removable volumes. Rare extensions and encrypted blobs on sticks are not user behaviour; they are stage-4 of the simulation in this guide.
  • Assume the gap is crossable. Design monitoring for the isolated network with the same rigour as the internet side, because a human courier crosses it without touching a single sensor.

Mitigation and Defense

Controls that stop USB exfiltration before it starts, blunt it when it happens, and make it visible either way — for enterprises and for small offices.

No single control stops this technique. A default-deny agent stops malware but not a determined insider; policy stops the insider but not a stolen admin credential. The defence works as a stack: block by default, allow encrypted media only, log everything, correlate by serial, and rehearse the response.

Enterprise controls, area by area

  • PolicyAcceptable-use policy that names removable media explicitly; an encrypted-media-only standard; a documented air-gap media procedure with sanitised dedicated sticks and signed transfer logs.
  • Identity & accessRestrict who can reach restricted file shares at all — USB exfil needs readable files. Least privilege here removes most of the attack surface before any device control matters.
  • EndpointDevice control in default-deny mode: mass storage blocked or read-only, encrypted devices allowed, all attempts logged. Deploy Sysmon with file-create, registry-set and process-create coverage on every host, including the air-gapped ones.
  • NetworkOn the internet side, egress filtering and proxy policies so a drop host cannot upload freely; watch for new-domain uploads within hours of USB activity.
  • Cloud & CASBBlock or warn on personal-tenant sync (the GoldenJackal exit path); require corporate tenants for any sync client, and alert on bulk downloads before travel.
  • LoggingUSBSTOR + Sysmon 1/11/13 + Security 4663 + device-control logs into the SIEM with ≥90-day retention; nightly serial-inventory build.
  • MonitoringThe 5.5 rule set deployed; weekly hunt: same serial on two hosts, first-seen devices, volume bursts.
  • BackupOffline or immutable backups — an air-gapped backup copy also survives a malicious device brought into the network (the T1091 reverse direction).
  • ResponseA written device-seizure procedure (chain of custody, serials, timelines on both segments) and a one-page decision rule for when a two-host serial match becomes a case.

Home and small-office advice

  • Never plug in found sticks. The classic test: a "lost" stick in the car park is bait; dropping it into a work laptop is the attack.
  • Turn on full-disk encryption (BitLocker, FileVault, LUKS) so a lost laptop is not also a lost data set, and buy hardware-encrypted USB sticks for anything sensitive.
  • Separate drives for work and life, and never let a work stick visit a family PC — home machines are where implants ride back across your own "gap".

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

Twelve concrete mistakes blue teams make around USB detection — each with the symptom you will notice, the root cause, the fix, and the habit that prevents it.

1. Treating USB events as noise and archiving them at zero retention

Symptom: the SIEM shows USBSTOR and Sysmon 11 events for a few days, then nothing; investigations find gaps exactly where the stick evidence should be.

Root cause: USB telemetry is high-volume in user count but low in value per event, so it gets routed to the cheapest index with a short lifespan.

Fix: route device events to a small dedicated index with ≥90-day retention (365 for USBSTOR); they are tiny records.

Prevention: a data-onboarding standard that assigns retention by evidentiary value, not by volume.

2. No cross-host serial inventory

Symptom: analysts review USB activity per host and never ask "which other host saw this stick?" — the one question that catches air-gap relays.

Root cause: monitoring is organised by host, and the USBSTOR serial is never extracted as a first-class field.

Fix: build the nightly serial inventory (SPL in 5.5-3) and alert on dc(Computer) > 1.

Prevention: treat the serial like an IP (Internet Protocol) address: a first-class join key in the asset model.

3. Ignoring macOS hosts

Symptom: the serial inventory is Windows-only; Mac laptops appear in no USB alert even though designers and executives carry the most data out.

Root cause: macOS has no native USB file-write logging comparable to Sysmon 11 — teams assume "no easy logs" means "no detection".

Fix: deploy a third-party endpoint agent with removable-media auditing on macOS, and at minimum enforce device control at the MDM (mobile device management) level.

Prevention: platform coverage is a standing column in the logging matrix — every source, every OS.

4. Chasing every USB insertion as an incident

Symptom: a flood of "device inserted" alerts; analysts tune everything off within a month.

Root cause: alerting on the arrival event instead of on behaviour around it; insertions are daily life.

Fix: alert on the 5.5 behaviours (bursts, polling, serial matches, composite devices), not on PnP arrivals.

Prevention: the rule-review checklist asks "what user behaviour would this alert fire on?" before deployment.

5. Allow-listing devices by friendly name

Symptom: device control allows "Kingston DataTraveler"; a badUSB-class gadget with the same name is allowed through (threat T5).

Root cause: friendly names are user-visible strings, freely spoofable; they were never an identity.

Fix: key policy on device class plus hardware serial, and require encryption for storage-class devices.

Prevention: annual red-team check that a renamed cheap stick is still blocked.

6. Logging enabled, but the Sysmon config drops the USB events

Symptom: "we have Sysmon" yet Event 11 for E:\ never arrives in the SIEM while Event 1 does.

Root cause: a filter or exclude line (often a drive-letter or path rule) silently swallows removable-volume writes.

Fix: lab-verify the config: insert a stick on a test VM and confirm events 1, 11, 12, 13 all forward.

Prevention: config changes ship with a before/after event-diff from the lab VM.

7. Tuning away odd extensions on removable volumes

Symptom: the ".hidenc"-style rule from 5.5-1 fires a few times for unknown tools, gets a broad exclusion, and stops firing ever again.

Root cause: tuning by suppression instead of by exception-with-owner; the rare extension was the signal.

Fix: convert each exclusion into a named exception (host, user, ticket, expiry) attached to the rule.

Prevention: quarterly exception review; expired exceptions close automatically.

8. No owner for the device-control policy

Symptom: exceptions accumulate ("temporary" blocks lifted years ago), the allow-list grows, and nobody can say who approved it.

Root cause: endpoint policy has no named owner; requests go to whoever answers the ticket.

Fix: assign an owning team, an approval workflow, and an exception register with expiry dates.

Prevention: the policy owner reviews the exception register monthly as part of change control.

9. Trusting the air gap — no monitoring inside the isolated segment

Symptom: the internet segment has a full stack; the air-gapped network forwards nothing, so step 3–4 evidence (simulation) is lost forever.

Root cause: "disconnected" was read as "invisible to attackers"; nobody budgeted sensors for the isolated estate.

Fix: ship Sysmon plus local SIEM forwarding (or offline log export on the same controlled media procedure) for isolated hosts.

Prevention: monitoring coverage is measured per network segment, not per site.

10. Watching only one side of the relay

Symptom: the air-gapped team and the internet-side SOC each saw half of the incident and neither connected it (the USBStealer failure mode).

Root cause: organisational, not technical: two tool stacks, two queues, no shared case.

Fix: one shared case process triggered by any two-host serial match, with both segments' timelines attached automatically.

Prevention: cross-segment tabletop once a year using the 8-step simulation as the scenario.

11. Never testing detection with a controlled USB drill

Symptom: rules were deployed months ago and have silently degraded after an agent update; nobody noticed.

Root cause: no purple-team exercise for physical-medium exfil, so the pipeline is never exercised end to end.

Fix: a quarterly lab drill: insert a marked stick on a test host, run the 8-step simulation, and confirm every 5.5 rule fires with the right fields.

Prevention: detection rules carry an owner and a last-tested date, like any other control.

12. Blocking devices but not logging the attempts

Symptom: device control blocks unapproved sticks, the SOC celebrates — and the blocked attempts are discarded, so recon patterns and insider probing stay invisible.

Root cause: the agent's log forwarding was never switched on; "block" was mistaken for "detect".

Fix: forward all block/allow decisions to the SIEM; alert on repeat-blocked serials (rule 5.5-6 pattern).

Prevention: the acceptance test for any control includes "prove the event lands in the SIEM".

Habits that keep the pipeline honest

Daily: review new device-control blocks and first-seen device serials; confirm forwarding health for the USB indexes; triage any 5.5 alert the same day.

Weekly: run the cross-host serial hunt and the "proxy upload within 24h of USB activity" hunt; sample-verify a handful of exceptions still match reality.

Monthly: review the exception register and policy owner sign-off; re-check macOS/agent coverage; update the serial inventory report and share metrics with data owners.

Response checklist — suspected USB exfiltration

  1. Pull the serials. Extract device serials from USBSTOR on the implicated host(s).
  2. Build the both-sides timeline. Sysmon 1/11/13, Security 4663 and device-control logs on every host that saw the serial — including the air-gapped side.
  3. Identify the container. Look for rare-extension or oversized files written to the volume; preserve them if the device is seized.
  4. Scope the internet-side egress. Proxy, CASB and DNS records for the internet-side host in the 24–48 h after the stick was read.
  5. Seize the device. Chain of custody; note serial, vendor, model.
  6. Notify data owners. Name the data set at risk; start the compliance clock (GDPR Art. 33/34, HIPAA, PCI) if personal or cardholder data is implicated.
  7. Review device-control mode. If the estate was in audit or read-only mode, decide on default-deny now, with the incident as the driver.
  8. Hunt for implants on both hosts. First-stage behaviour on the isolated host, second-stage on the internet host — assume both until proven otherwise.
