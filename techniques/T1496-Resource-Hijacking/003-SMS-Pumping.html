T1496.003 SMS Pumping

SMS pumping is telecom fraud that uses your own verification forms against you. A fraud ring leases a block of phone numbers, then floods your OTP (one-time password) and signup forms with automated requests. Your app pays for every SMS it sends, and a share of each delivery fee flows back to the fraudster. Vendors commonly report six-figure bills within days, while real users wait for codes that arrive late or never. This guide shows the whole flow, the logs it leaves, and how to detect and stop it.

Overview

SMS Pumping is a fraud technique that turns your own verification forms into an SMS-sending machine for criminals. You pay for every message. The attacker collects a share of the delivery fees. This page explains the method, animates the whole flow, and gives you detections, a response plan, and a case study built from public reporting.

What the sub-technique is

SMS pumping is telecommunications fraud. The attacker first obtains a set of phone numbers from a telecom provider or an aggregator. These numbers sit in high-termination-cost destinations: countries or networks where receiving an SMS pays the number owner a termination fee. Then the attacker abuses a victim's messaging setup, usually a public OTP or account-verification form, to send huge amounts of SMS to exactly those numbers.

The victim's app does all the work. It calls its messaging provider, such as Twilio, AWS SNS, or Amazon Cognito, and pays for each message. The telecom pays revenue share to the number owner, who is the attacker. Nobody breaks into your network to do this. The attack abuses a feature that works exactly as designed, and in most cases the attacker steals no data at all: the damage is cost and availability.

Why it matters

Money first. A pumping storm can add tens of thousands of dollars in SMS costs in a single day, because messages to some destinations cost far more than a normal local message. Twilio reports that its Verify Fraud Guard alone saved customers $62.7 million in fraudulent costs and blocked over 569 million fraud attempts between June 2022 and October 2024. That is the measured result at one provider alone.

Availability second. The message queue fills with fraud traffic, so real customers wait for passcodes or never get them. Signups can then fail during the busiest growth periods. In regulated sectors, a fraud problem can become an availability and reporting problem too.

Where it fits in the attack chain

SMS pumping sits in the Impact tactic of MITRE ATT&CK, as sub-technique T1496.003 under Resource Hijacking (T1496). Unlike most ATT&CK techniques, it often needs no intrusion at all. The attacker abuses a public form that exists precisely so strangers can trigger messages. When an intrusion is involved, it is usually small and direct: a leaked messaging API key from a code repository, or stolen provider credentials, which then let the attacker send SMS without touching your forms at all.

ATT&CK lists detection strategy DET0156 for this sub-technique. Its analytic AN0443 watches for automated, repetitive triggering of SMS through OTP and verification fields on SaaS platforms, through services like Twilio, AWS SNS, and Amazon Cognito, toward attacker-controlled numbers. The listed mitigation is M1013, Application Developer Guidance, with one concrete piece of advice: consider CAPTCHA protection on forms that send messages via SMS. That is the only mitigation ATT&CK lists for this sub-technique, and Section 7 builds the rest of the defense around it.

Written story of the simulation

The list below covers the same eight steps as the animation. Screen readers and printouts get the full content here.

  1. Step 1 — Leasing a number range. A fraud ring leases a block of phone numbers in countries where SMS termination fees are high. Every message delivered to those numbers pays the ring a share of the fee. This step happens in the telecom market, so the victim sees nothing yet.
  2. Step 2 — Studying the signup flow. The fraudster finds a public signup page and confirms that any phone number triggers a verification SMS. No account is needed. Web logs show a handful of OTP requests from one new ASN (Autonomous System Number — the network that owns the IP addresses), which looks like ordinary noise.
  3. Step 3 — OTP request storm. Bots fire thousands of OTP requests with rotating IP addresses, devices, and identities. Every request carries a phone number from the fraud range. API logs now show request rates far above baseline from a few ASNs, with near-zero completions.
  4. Step 4 — SMS out through the OTP API. Each request makes the app call its messaging provider. Thousands of SMS leave toward the fraud range and the victim's costs start to grow. Provider logs show the send spike and delivery receipts clustering on one prefix.
  5. Step 5 — Money flows back, costs explode. The telco delivers the messages and pays termination revenue toward the range owner. The victim pays per SMS, and real users see delayed codes. Spend alerts jump while support tickets rise.
  6. Step 6 — Conversion collapse triggers the alert. Analytics join sends with verifications: tens of thousands of codes sent, a handful verified. Fraud Guard and conversion alerts fire together. The sent-versus-verified ratio is the strongest single signal.
  7. Step 7 — Containment. The team enables CAPTCHA on the form, applies per-ASN and per-prefix rate limits, and blocks the fraud range at the provider. The send curve flattens within the hour.
  8. Step 8 — Hardening and budgets. The app adds destination allowlists, app attestation (a check that the request really comes from the genuine app), resend cooldowns, and hard spend budgets that alert the SOC. Fraud controls become permanent, and the entry point is closed, not just the bill.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

SMS pumping is a business, not a break-in. Understanding the economics is the best way to predict where it will appear next. This section describes attacker thinking at a conceptual level. The only commands shown are a lab-only demo against your own sandbox, so you can see what the logs look like.

When and why fraudsters use it

The economics are simple and attractive to criminals. A phone number in a high-termination-cost destination earns its owner a share of every SMS that lands there. The fraudster does not need exploits, malware, or access to your systems. They need three things: a leased number range, a script that can fill in forms, and a way to rotate their source IPs so simple rate limits do not stop them.

Targets are chosen for one feature: an SMS that any stranger can trigger. Fintech apps, wallets, crypto exchanges, marketplaces, and delivery services verify users by SMS at scale, so their signup flow becomes a source of income for the attacker. Pumping pays best where verification is frequent, required, and instant.

How it is performed (concept)

The pattern is almost always the same five moves. First, acquire number ranges in high-termination-cost destinations from providers or resellers. Second, find OTP endpoints and learn their parameters. Third, automate requests using residential proxy networks, emulator farms, or SIM banks so each request looks like a new device. Fourth, tune the storm: spread it over hours, randomize fingerprints, and stay just under obvious thresholds. Fifth, collect the revenue share as the telco chain pays out.

There is a second, intruder-flavored path: steal messaging credentials. Keys for Twilio, AWS SNS, or Cognito are regularly leaked into public code repositories. With a valid key the attacker skips your forms entirely and sends SMS directly from your account, which still generates the same bill and the same revenue share.

Ethics and legality

SMS pumping is criminal fraud in nearly every country, and running it "as a test" against a site you do not own can break computer misuse, telecom, and fraud laws at once. The economics above are explained so defenders can predict attacker behavior, not so anyone can copy it. Everything you execute yourself should stay inside a lab you own. Never point scripts at real endpoints, and never use real customer phone numbers.

Common variations

  • AIT (artificially inflated traffic) on marketing sends: the same fraud aimed at double opt-in forms, so every "confirm my subscription" text pays the ring.
  • Cognito and SNS signup pumping: user pools with SMS MFA on by default get pumped through mass account creation.
  • Toll fraud and PBX dial-through: the voice sibling with the same revenue model, older and still active.
  • Storm as cover: pumping waves sometimes mask credential testing on the same OTP endpoint, because noise hides the takeover attempts.
  • Free-trial farming: bulk signups on freemium apps whose welcome SMS bills the provider from day one.

Mistakes fraudsters make

  • Letting one ASN dominate the traffic profile — the easiest join for defenders.
  • Reusing the same JA3 fingerprint (a fingerprint of the client's encrypted-connection handshake) and user agent across a whole emulator farm.
  • Running traffic to one number range until the carrier's fraud desk flags it, instead of rotating ranges.
  • Hitting endpoints during local business hours, when delivery delays become visible to real users.
  • Ignoring conversion dashboards and continuing after defenses flip, which burns the range for no gain.
  • Forgetting that spend alerts page somebody: the finance team is a sensor too.
4

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

SMS pumping is very visible — if the right telemetry reaches the SOC. The problem is organizational, not technical: the best data sits in product dashboards and provider consoles that security often cannot see. This section shows what the attack looks like from inside and gives a triage flow for a busy shift.

How defenders can see it

Four telemetry families catch this technique. Application telemetry shows OTP request velocity per IP, ASN, and device fingerprint. Provider telemetry shows every SMS, its destination, its price, and its delivery status. Product analytics show the conversion ratio: codes sent versus codes verified. Finance telemetry shows spend against forecast. No single source is enough, because a pumping storm looks like a busy day in any one of them. The detections in Section 5 all join at least two.

Artifacts it leaves behind

  • OTP request rates per hour far above baseline, concentrated on few ASNs.
  • Delivery receipts clustered on one or two destination prefixes.
  • Sent-versus-verified ratio collapsing from a healthy baseline toward zero.
  • New or unknown device fingerprints, headless user agents, missing app attestation.
  • Provider usage triggers and budget alarms firing on the SMS line item.
  • Support tickets: "my code never arrived", arriving in clusters.

Triage flow: a spend alert arrives

  1. Join three dashboards. Open security (OTP velocity), fraud (AIT flags), and finance (spend) side by side. One of them alone is often a false lead.
  2. Rule out the legitimate reason. Check the marketing and launch calendar. A campaign in one country can look like prefix clustering; conversion stays healthy there, which is the difference.
  3. Cluster the requests. Group OTP requests by ASN, destination prefix, and device fingerprint. Real users spread out; a storm concentrates.
  4. Measure conversion. Compute sent versus verified for the same window. Near-zero conversion plus high volume is the pumping signature.
  5. Preserve evidence. Export provider message logs and gateway logs before you block anything, so the case can be reconstructed and disputed if needed.
  6. Contain. Turn on CAPTCHA, apply per-ASN and per-MSISDN (full phone number) rate limits, and block the offending prefix at the provider. Watch the send curve flatten.
  7. Feed detection. Open the provider fraud desk case, add the ranges to your blocklist pipeline, and file the incident record with numbers.

Why this matters beyond money

OTP endpoints that accept pumped traffic also accept account-takeover testing. The same weak form that drains your budget is the one an attacker uses to harass users with codes or to abuse resend logic. Fixing pumping with CAPTCHA, rate limits, and attestation hardens every flow that depends on the phone number being real.

Attack-chain fit: this technique sits in the Impact tactic of MITRE ATT&CK, so it usually ends an attack rather than starting one. When stolen credentials drive the sends, the chain runs through T1583 (Acquire Infrastructure) for the number ranges and T1078 (Valid Accounts) for the messaging account, with the pumping itself as the impact step.

They still apply, because OTP flows process personal data, because auditors test monitoring of abnormal activity, and because telecom rules forbid artificial traffic. Audit tip

The evidence auditors accept for this sub-technique is usually operational, not paperwork: a working conversion dashboard, a fraud-guard rule set, and a rate-limit policy on the OTP endpoint. Build those first, then write the policy around what you actually run.

5.2Threat intelligence

Here is the honest starting point: no ATT&CK group is mapped to T1496.003. This is a fraud-crime ecosystem, not an intrusion set. Attribution lives with telecom fraud desks, not with public threat reports. Intelligence work here means tracking the fraud pattern, the tooling, and the numbers, and accepting that names rarely come.

Source types and how to use them

  • MITRE ATT&CK — the sub-technique page defines the pattern, platform, detection strategy DET0156, and mitigation M1013. Vendor-neutral and free; start here.
  • Provider fraud reporting — Twilio documents artificially inflated traffic and publishes fraud-loss figures, including $62.7 million in avoided fraud costs and over 569 million blocked fraud attempts for Verify Fraud Guard customers between June 2022 and October 2024. AWS publishes SMS pumping guidance and ships pumping protections in End User Messaging.
  • Industry bodies — M3AAWG publishes messaging-abuse best practice; GSMA coordinates carrier-side fraud work. Both describe countermeasures you can copy.
  • Regulators — FCC actions against robotext operations show which behaviors draw enforcement, useful for policy conversations.
  • OSINT, commercial feeds, and ISACs — OSINT (open-source intelligence) covers fraud research blogs and leaked tooling write-ups; commercial feeds sell curated high-risk prefix and ASN lists; ISACs (Information Sharing and Analysis Centers) and M3AAWG share member alerts about active pumping waves.
  • Carrier fraud desks — your provider's fraud team is the closest thing to an intelligence partner here. Open the relationship before an incident happens.
  • Internal telemetry — your own conversion metrics are the best intel for your apps. Every incident permanently improves your baseline.

Confidence levels, in plain words

High means the pattern is documented by multiple providers and your own telemetry confirms it. Use it for alerting and blocking. Medium means the report is credible but you have not seen it locally — use it for hunts. Low means a single claim without corroboration, such as a named ring — use it for awareness only.

Apply intel in three steps. Enrich: load known fraud prefixes and high-risk ASNs into lookup tables your SIEM (security information and event management system) can join. Hunt: search 90 days of OTP and provider logs against each new indicator set. Detect: turn confirmed hunts into the Sigma rules of 5.5, and retire rules when the ranges rotate.

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

Each threat gets one Sigma rule and one Splunk SPL (Search Processing Language) query that implement the same logic. Index and sourcetype names are stated with each query as assumptions; adjust them to your environment. All queries assume fields are extracted at ingest time. Because pumping is about rates, the Sigma rules read pre-aggregated hourly events (as in 5.4), while the SPL does live aggregation.

SP-01 OTP velocity storm — Sigma (application log) + SPL

Mapping note: implements analytic AN0443 (repetitive OTP triggering) on the application side. The rule expects an hourly aggregate event per ASN, as produced by the metrics job shown in 5.4. Severity: critical.

Splunk SPL equivalent (assumes index=web sourcetype=aws:elb_logs for request velocity and index=saas sourcetype=app:metrics for the conversion feed; run both and compare windows):

False positives and tuning: exclude load-test ASNs by lookup; keep the conversion condition, which removes most legitimate campaigns; re-baseline the 300-per-hour threshold per endpoint every quarter. Severity rationale: every hour of an undetected storm is direct spend, and real users are blocked from signing up.

SP-02 Prefix concentration — Sigma (messaging provider) + SPL

Mapping note: implements the provider-side view of AN0443: sends clustering on attacker-linked number ranges, as documented by Twilio and AWS. Expects an hourly aggregate per prefix with cost fields. Severity: high.

Splunk SPL equivalent (assumes index=saas sourcetype=twilio:api with message resource fields extracted at ingest):

False positives and tuning: maintain a per-country conversion baseline so a genuine launch does not page; exclude subscriber-list sender IDs; tune the 0.35 share and $250 cost lines to your traffic. Severity rationale: concentration is the direct cash-out path — every clustered send pays the fraudster.

SP-03 Conversion collapse — Sigma (app analytics) + SPL

Mapping note: implements the AIT confirmation view of AN0443: the sent-versus-verified gap. Expects an hourly conversion window event as in 5.4. Severity: high.

Splunk SPL equivalent (assumes index=saas sourcetype=app:metrics emits otp_sent and otp_verified counters):

False positives and tuning: join delivery error codes first — a carrier rejecting your sender ID looks identical in conversion; baseline per flow and per country; alert on the gap, not on absolute conversion. Severity rationale: this is the confirmatory signal that turns a spend anomaly into a fraud case.

SP-04 Automated clients — Sigma (WAF) + SPL

Mapping note: implements the delivery-mechanism view of AN0443: bot clients (emulators, headless browsers, SIM-farm devices) hitting signup and OTP paths. Severity: medium.

Splunk SPL equivalent (assumes index=web sourcetype=aws:waf with normalized bot fields extracted by your AWS WAF TA):

False positives and tuning: allowlist monitors by user agent and ASN; mobile OTP without attestation is expected for old app versions — build a version floor; pair this rule with SP-01 before paging, since bots alone are common. Severity rationale: the bots carry out the attack but do not choose the targets — medium, and blocking them raises attacker cost immediately.

SP-05 Spend and quota events — Sigma (SNS budget alerts) + SPL

Mapping note: implements the finance-tripwire view of AN0443. AWS Budgets alerts ride SNS, and Twilio usage triggers arrive by webhook; the rule covers the SNS path, the SPL adds both. Severity: medium.

Splunk SPL equivalent (assumes index=cloud sourcetype=aws:cloudwatch for billing metrics and index=cloud sourcetype=aws:cloudtrail for the Cognito identity check; Twilio triggers land in index=saas sourcetype=twilio:usage_trigger):

False positives and tuning: mark planned campaign windows in a lookup so budget alerts during launches do not page; route every firing to both SOC and FinOps; escalate repeats within 24 hours to an incident. Severity rationale: medium as a detector, but it is the fastest executive-visible signal and often arrives first.

Validation habit

Test every rule monthly with a lab event (see the sandbox demo in Section 3). Fire two OTP requests from an unused ASN in a test window and confirm the SPL joins see them. A rule that has never matched synthetic data probably cannot match anything.

Real-World Case Study: The SMS Pumping Wave Against OTP Apps

This case is a composite. It is built strictly from public reporting by Twilio and AWS about the SMS pumping waves of 2022 to 2025. No single victim is named in public sources, so this study describes the documented pattern rather than one company. Every quoted figure comes from those published sources.

How to read this case

Composite case built from public Twilio and AWS reporting — no single victim is named in public sources. The $62.7 million figure is documented, and it covers many Twilio Verify customers together. Individual victims commonly report six-figure SMS bills within days, a scale vendors describe without attaching names.

Context

Between 2022 and 2025, messaging providers documented a wave of SMS pumping against applications that verify users by SMS. The targets share one profile: a public signup or login flow that sends an SMS, and a user base that expects instant delivery. Fintech apps, wallets, crypto exchanges, marketplaces, and delivery services are typical targets, because they verify aggressively and grow fast.

On the attacker side this is an industry, not a hobby. Number ranges are leased in high-termination-cost destinations, bot traffic is rented, and residential proxy networks rotate source IPs past simple rate limits. Twilio calls the core pattern artificially inflated traffic (AIT) and built Verify Fraud Guard against it. AWS publishes SMS pumping guidance and added destination allowlists and spend limits to its End User Messaging service.

Attack chain, as documented by the providers

  1. Recon: identify apps whose signup, login, or resend endpoints trigger SMS through Twilio, AWS SNS, or Cognito.
  2. Scripting: automate OTP requests with rotating IPs, emulator farms, and SIM banks, spread across hours to stay under obvious thresholds.
  3. Concentration: direct the sends to the number ranges the ring controls in high-cost destinations.
  4. Cash-out: the telecom chain pays termination revenue share toward the range owner.
  5. Result: the victim pays per-SMS costs, and real users face delayed or failed verifications.

Timeline of the documented wave

  • 2022

    The wave grows into an industry-wide fraud problem. Twilio expands Verify Fraud Guard and starts measuring avoided fraud from June 2022.

  • June 2022 - October 2024

    Twilio documents $62.7 million in fraudulent costs avoided by Verify Fraud Guard customers during this window, with more than 569 million fraud attempts blocked — the clearest published measure of how large the attacks were.

  • June 2024

    AWS publishes proactive threat detection guidance (Fletcher and de Vera) that explains SMS pumping; AWS End User Messaging offers destination-country allowlists and spend limits against it.

  • 2023 - 2024

    Victim stories keep appearing in vendor reporting: OTP storms, conversion collapse, and SMS bills commonly described as six figures within days, while real users wait for codes.

  • 2025

    AWS's builder blog sums up the phenomenon as a multi-billion dollar fraud. Defense becomes default practice: CAPTCHA, attestation, destination allowlists, and hard budgets.

Detection point

In the documented pattern, the first alarms came from the provider side: Fraud Guard alerts and spend anomalies. The teams that caught it fastest joined three signals: OTP velocity from one ASN, delivery receipts clustered on one prefix, and conversion collapsing from a healthy baseline toward zero. Teams watching only send volume saw nothing wrong for days, because a busy signup day is normal; teams watching the ratio saw it in the first hour.

Containment

Containment followed the same script in the reported cases. Turn on CAPTCHA for the affected forms. Apply rate limits per ASN, per IP, and per phone number. Block the offending destination prefixes at the provider, or disable the affected messaging route entirely, so failed verification is visible instead of fraud that keeps billing silently. Teams that exported provider message logs before blocking could later reconstruct the full range list and dispute charges.

Eradication

After the storm stopped, hardening removed the cause. Blocklists for the ranges and ASNs went into the provider configuration as permanent rules, not one-off filters. Where leaked API keys had been used, keys were rotated and secret scanning was added to the repositories. Fraud Guard rules and destination allowlists were turned on by default, so the next storm would hit a wall instead of a meter.

Recovery

Recovery meant restoring trust in the funnel: verification conversion returned to baseline, budgets were rebalanced, and monitoring was tuned so a real campaign would not re-trigger the fraud alerts. In the documented pattern, teams added a weekly review of prefix mix and spend, because pumping rings return with fresh ranges once a defense pattern becomes known.

Impact

The documented numbers are large. Twilio states that Verify Fraud Guard saved customers $62.7 million in fraudulent costs between June 2022 and October 2024 and blocked more than 569 million fraud attempts — money and traffic that would otherwise have paid fraudster ranges. Individual victims commonly report six-figure SMS bills within days. The softer costs are real too: signups that failed during the storm never converted, and support queues filled with missing-code tickets.

Team response and communication

The pattern that worked was a joint channel: security, fraud, product, and finance in one room with one number on the screen — current SMS spend. The provider fraud desk was engaged early, which sped up range blocking and charge disputes. Executives received short updates with figures: sends, verified ratio, spend per hour, and time to containment. Vague updates cost trust; the numbers kept everyone aligned.

Lessons learned

  • OTP abuse is a revenue model, not vandalism — attackers return as long as the fraud stays profitable, so defenses must be permanent.
  • Fraud controls are security controls: route provider fraud alerts and conversion dashboards to the SOC, not just to product inboxes.
  • Conversion, not volume, is the most useful metric; the sent-versus-verified gap caught in hours what volume alerts missed for days.
  • Product teams and the SOC must share telemetry — the log sources in 5.4 span four teams, and pumping hides in the seams between them.
  • Destination allowlists and spend limits cap the worst-case cost; they cost little and close the cash-out path.
  • Preserve provider logs before blocking; the evidence supports charge disputes and improves the permanent blocklists.
  • Assume the ring returns with fresh ranges; keep the weekly prefix and spend review running after the incident closes.

Mitigation and Defense

ATT&CK lists exactly one mitigation for T1496.003: M1013 Application Developer Guidance, with the advice to consider CAPTCHA protection on forms that send messages via SMS. That advice is the correct starting point, but it is not a complete defense. Everything below it comes from provider guidance and industry practice, arranged so the meter stays closed even when one control fails.

Product & app (the ATT&CK mitigation)

M1013 Application Developer Guidance is the only mitigation ATT&CK lists for this sub-technique. Put its advice, and the measures that support it, in place first:

  • CAPTCHA on every form that can send SMS — signup, login, resend, phone change (M1013).
  • Resend cooldowns and per-phone-number daily caps.
  • Verify the number before sending where possible: carrier lookup, line-type filter.
  • App attestation required for mobile OTP calls — Play Integrity and DeviceCheck, Google's and Apple's app-integrity services.
  • Email-first or authenticator-first flows for users who do not need SMS.

Messaging provider

  • Turn on fraud guard: Twilio Verify Fraud Guard or the AWS End User Messaging pumping protections.
  • Destination-country allowlists; block high-risk prefixes by default.
  • Spend limits and usage triggers with SOC-visible alerts.
  • Separate messaging services per flow, so one abused form cannot drain everything.

Identity & cloud

  • Cognito: adaptive authentication, no SMS MFA for low-risk signups, email verification first.
  • Least-privilege IAM for messaging APIs; no static keys in code; short-lived credentials.
  • Secret scanning in CI (continuous integration), so leaked Twilio or AWS keys are found quickly.
  • CloudTrail and provider audit logs on, retained, and searchable.

Logging & monitoring

  • Ship gateway, provider, analytics, and spend logs to one place (Section 5.4).
  • Deploy the five Sigma rules from 5.5 with their stated assumptions.
  • Conversion dashboard with a rolling baseline, alerted to the SOC.
  • Prefix mix reviewed weekly; new prefixes treated as change events.

Policy & people

  • Named owner for messaging spend and fraud posture — one person, not a committee.
  • Carrier AUP alignment reviewed at contract time; GSMA and M3AAWG practices mapped.
  • Incident classification mentions OTP outage, so NIS2-style clocks start correctly.
  • Runbook: who calls the provider, who flips CAPTCHA, who blocks prefixes.

Response

  • Joint fraud-security channel that exists before the incident.
  • Provider fraud desk contact card tested quarterly.
  • Evidence-first containment: export logs, then block.
  • Post-incident: every storm adds one permanent detection and one tuning change.

Home and small-office users

If you run a small app with SMS login, you are a target too. Five practical steps: (1) turn on your provider's fraud protection and set a hard spend cap today; (2) put a free CAPTCHA on the form that sends codes; (3) limit how often any one phone number can request a code; (4) watch the provider dashboard weekly — cost per signup is your health metric; (5) if you receive SMS codes you never asked for, report them as spam and change nothing else — your number may have been typed into someone's form by a bot.

Response-ready habits

Keep an emergency runbook titled "SMS bill spike": who calls the provider fraud desk, who enables CAPTCHA, who applies the prefix block, and where the evidence exports go. Practice it once a quarter as a tabletop with finance in the room. The documented wave shows fraud costs accumulate around the clock; the team that acts in the first hour pays the least.

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

These twelve mistakes come from post-incident reviews of pumping cases and from the public provider guidance. Each one lists the symptom you will notice, the root cause, the fix, and the habit that prevents it from returning.

1. Watching volume, not conversion

  • Symptom: the bill explodes while every volume dashboard looks "like a busy day".
  • Root cause: alerts threshold on send counts only; pumping hides inside ordinary-looking volume.
  • Fix: add the sent-versus-verified ratio to every OTP alert, with a rolling baseline per flow (SP-03).
  • Prevention: no OTP alert ships without a conversion condition.

2. Fraud alerts that never reach the SOC

  • Symptom: the product inbox has Fraud Guard emails from last week; the SOC hears about it from finance.
  • Root cause: provider alerts route to whoever configured the console, which is rarely security.
  • Fix: forward provider fraud and usage alerts into the SIEM as normal alert events (SP-05).
  • Prevention: the asset inventory lists every messaging account with a security contact.

3. IP-only rate limits

  • Symptom: limits tuned to stop one IP do nothing; the storm keeps flowing.
  • Root cause: residential proxies rotate IPs per request, so per-IP counters never fill.
  • Fix: rate limit per ASN, per destination prefix, and per device fingerprint, not just per IP.
  • Prevention: threat model the OTP endpoint against a rotating adversary, not a static one.

4. No destination allowlist

  • Symptom: sends arrive for prefixes the product has never served.
  • Root cause: the form will SMS any number on Earth, so the fraudster picks the destinations.
  • Fix: default-allow only your user countries; require approval for new prefixes (SP-02).
  • Prevention: review the prefix mix weekly; treat new prefixes as change events.

5. CAPTCHA on signup only

  • Symptom: the storm moves to login resend or phone-change the day after signup is protected.
  • Root cause: one form was hardened; the attacker, who only needs one, found the other.
  • Fix: inventory every SMS-sending path and apply CAPTCHA plus limits to all of them (M1013).
  • Prevention: a CI check that fails builds adding new SMS-sending endpoints without controls.

6. Thresholds set once, never re-based

  • Symptom: a real campaign fires the fraud alerts, or growth quietly buries them below the line.
  • Root cause: static thresholds written in the first week and never revisited.
  • Fix: rolling baselines per flow and per country; alert on deviation, not on absolute numbers.
  • Prevention: quarterly threshold review tied to the product roadmap.

7. Leaked sandbox and test keys

  • Symptom: sends in provider logs from a service nobody deployed, billed to your account.
  • Root cause: sandbox credentials committed to public repositories and never rotated.
  • Fix: rotate now, then add secret scanning to CI and pre-commit hooks.
  • Prevention: keys live in a secret manager with short lifetimes; the repo never sees them.

8. Fraud Guard alerts auto-closed

  • Symptom: the same AIT alert fires weekly and is closed weekly, until the monthly invoice makes it real.
  • Root cause: no runbook for the alert and no join with internal telemetry, so it looks like noise.
  • Fix: every fraud alert runs the Section 4 triage flow; closure requires a disposition note.
  • Prevention: auto-close rules are forbidden on fraud classes without a documented exception.

9. Nobody owns the provider console

  • Symptom: pumping protections sit off; MFA on the messaging account is missing; nobody can say why.
  • Root cause: the account was created by a developer who has since moved teams.
  • Fix: assign a named owner, enforce MFA, and audit console settings quarterly.
  • Prevention: access reviews include SaaS and messaging accounts, not only cloud IAM.

10. Rules never tested with synthetic events

  • Symptom: the SP rules have fired zero times in a year — because they cannot fire at all.
  • Root cause: field names drifted after a parser change; nobody replayed a test event.
  • Fix: replay the Section 3 sandbox shape monthly; alert on rules with no matches in 180 days.
  • Prevention: detection CI runs a synthetic event against every rule change.

11. Blaming the provider, skipping the join

  • Symptom: weeks of argument about whose fraud it is while the meter keeps running.
  • Root cause: provider data and app data live in separate silos, so nobody proves the pattern.
  • Fix: join gateway, provider, analytics, and spend logs in one investigation (Section 4 triage).
  • Prevention: the joint fraud-security channel meets monthly with the three dashboards on screen.

12. No retention on provider webhooks

  • Symptom: after blocking, the evidence needed to dispute charges or map ranges is gone.
  • Root cause: delivery receipts and fraud alerts were consumed and never stored.
  • Fix: store provider webhooks and message logs for at least 90 days in searchable storage.
  • Prevention: the logging standard lists provider sources with the same retention as app logs.
