T1200 – Hardware Additions

🎯Tactic: Initial Access 💻Platforms: Linux · Windows · macOS 🧩Complexity: Medium 🔍Detection difficulty: High 📌ATT&CK version: 1.7

Some attacks arrive in your inbox. This one arrives in an envelope — or waits in a parking lot. Hardware Additions (MITRE ATT&CK T1200) means the attacker physically introduces a device into your space. Examples: a USB drive that looks like a gift card, a keyboard that types by itself, a small box in a wall network socket, or a cable with hidden electronics. The hardware does the hacking. The operating system trusts new devices, so email filters, web proxies, and firewalls never see the attack. Do not confuse this with T1091 Replication Through Removable Media. That technique copies malware onto drives that are already in use, so it spreads to other machines. T1200 goes further: it adds a new device or a new function — a second keyboard, a network card, a whole computer — that then acts on its own. Real-world anchor: in 2021–2022 the FIN7 crime group mailed USB drives to hundreds of US employees. The drives pretended to be keyboards and typed their own PowerShell commands. This guide explains the technique in simple words, animates the full attack, and gives you real log samples, Sigma rules, and Splunk queries to build detections.

Think like the attacker

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

3.1 When attackers use this

T1200 is the technique of choice when email and the internet are too hard. If a company filters every attachment and blocks every download, the attacker looks for a physical way in. Timing matters. Real campaigns have used unsolicited "gifts", conference giveaways, parking-lot drops, and ordinary postal mail. The attacker does not need to be in the building. The postal system delivers the attack for them.

The technique also fits places where a network attack cannot reach: air-gapped factories, isolated card-payment networks, and bank back-offices. There, a USB drive or a small plugged-in box is often the only bridge between the attacker and the target. ATT&CK notes that public references are scarce, but many red teams and penetration testers use exactly these tools for initial access.

3.2 How attackers do it, step by step

  1. Pick the people. Search job sites and company pages for desk workers: HR, finance, reception, IT. Collect names and the company postal address.
  2. Buy or build the device. Cheap HID emulators — HID means human interface device, the device class for keyboards and mice (think Rubber Ducky). Multipurpose tools (Bash Bunny, Flipper Zero), implant cables, or a small single-board computer like a Raspberry Pi.
  3. Program the payload. A short HID script that opens a run box and types a PowerShell download command. Or a Linux drop box that opens a VPN tunnel back to the attacker once it gets network access.
  4. Disguise it. Print a "gift card" shell, a COVID-19 information notice, or a conference logo. Make the object look boring and official.
  5. Deliver it. Mail it in a real envelope, drop it in the lobby, leave it by the printer, or plug it in yourself during a visit.
  6. Wait for the plug-in. Curiosity does the rest. Most employees plug in a found drive within minutes, according to repeated controlled drop studies.
  7. Let the hardware act. The device shows up as a keyboard (HID), types its script, and the PowerShell command downloads the real malware. A network implant simply gets a DHCP lease and starts talking.
  8. Switch to hands-on work. From the new foothold (their first working access inside): persistence, password hunting, ransomware staging, or POS data theft.

3.3 Where it works best

🏢Open offices & lobbies

Many strangers, shared desks, open network sockets. A device left in a meeting room can sit for hours until someone plugs it in.

✉️Mail programs at scale

One letter costs a few dollars. Mailing hundreds reaches enough curious users that a few plug-ins are almost guaranteed.

🏭Air-gapped OT networks

Isolated plants still need USB for updates. A planted drive inside the perimeter becomes the only bridge in.

🎫Events & conferences

Attendees expect free gadgets. A "sponsor gift" USB or charger asks almost no questions.

🏦Banks & back-offices

DarkVishnya operators physically connected Bash Bunny and Raspberry Pi boxes to bank local networks to reach internal systems.

🛒Retail & hospitality

Stores process card data on local machines. Mailers to HR and IT staff open the path to payment lanes.

3.4 Why attackers like this technique

  • It skips every email gateway, web proxy, and attachment sandbox. There is no link to click.
  • Operating systems trust new hardware by default, so a keyboard is a keyboard — until it types by itself.
  • One curious employee is enough. No exploit, no stolen password, no zero day needed.
  • It works against air-gapped and low-connectivity sites where remote attacks fail.
  • At mail scale the cost per attempt is tiny, and each letter is an independent, untraceable attempt.

3.5 What attackers need first

  • Target names and a real postal address (or brief physical access to the site).
  • The implant device itself, plus firmware or a payload script tuned to the target OS.
  • A C2 or staging server with a clean-looking domain for the first download.
  • A mailing budget and patience — replies can take days or weeks.
  • For network implants: knowledge of the local VLAN (a separated part of the office network) and the DHCP scope. Also useful: the 802.1X posture (802.1X = the standard that checks devices before a network port lets them talk).

3.6 Tools and example commands

Defenders and purple teams (drill teams that replay attacks to test defenses) should know the standard kit. HID emulators (Rubber Ducky, Flipper Zero) and multi-tool platforms (Bash Bunny) are sold for authorized testing. Network drop boxes are emulated with small ARM boards. A good drill starts with a simple inventory: know every device your fleet accepts. The commands below are read-only and safe — run them on your own lab machines only.

usb-inventory.ps1 — list USB devices on a lab Windows host
# Show every USB device currently present
Get-PnpDevice -PresentOnly -Class USB | Format-Table Status, Class, FriendlyName -AutoSize

# Show all storage devices EVER connected, with serials (audit trail)
Get-ChildItem 'HKLM:\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Enum\USBSTOR' |
  Select-Object -ExpandProperty PSChildName

# Check the current device-control policy state (attack surface reduction rules, ASR)
Get-MpPreference | Select-Object -ExpandProperty AttackSurfaceReductionRules_Ids
usb-hotplug.sh — recent hot-plug events on a lab Linux host
# Kernel messages about new USB devices
sudo dmesg | grep -i 'new USB device'

# Vendor and product IDs of attached devices
lsusb

# Block devices and where they came from
lsblk -o NAME,RM,TRAN,MODEL,SERIAL

The same drills map to defense work. They cover USB device inventory audits, NAC bypass test plans (does an unknown MAC get a lease?), and keylogger-detection sweeps that compare HID device IDs against your known-good baseline.

Ethics and law

Plugging an implant into a workplace you do not own, or mailing malicious devices to real people, is a crime under computer misuse and fraud laws in most countries. Build and test only on machines you own, or with written permission from the system owner. Purple teams should replay FIN7-style devices only inside a controlled drill, with signed approval and a clear list of allowed targets.

Think like the defender

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

4.1 What defenders can see

The hardware is invisible to email filters, but once it is plugged in, it must talk to the operating system — and that leaves records. Windows writes a device-arrival event (Security 6416) and Kernel-PnP events (400/410/420) every time a new device is recognized. If the device claims to be a keyboard, PowerShell script block logging (event 4104) catches the command it types. A network implant must ask for a DHCP lease (the temporary network address the network hands out). It then shows up on DHCP servers and NAC systems with a new MAC address. A rogue Wi-Fi dongle shows up in WLAN-AutoConfig logs. None of these signals is loud alone. The chain — new device, then typed script, then new process, then new network connection — is what gives it away.

4.3 Where to look

  • Windows Security log: Event 6416 (new external device) and 6419–6424 (device disable/enable requests).
  • Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-PnP/Configuration: Events 400 (device started), 410 (device configured), 420 (device deleted).
  • Microsoft-Windows-PowerShell/Operational: Event 4104 script blocks, especially with download cradles (small loader commands that fetch the real payload).
  • Sysmon: Events 1 (process), 3 (network), 6 (driver), 11 (file create), 13 (registry) joined by device context.
  • DHCP server and NAC consoles (Cisco ISE, FortiNAC): new MACs, 802.1X failures, posture checks (NAC health checks for devices).
  • Microsoft-Windows-WLAN-AutoConfig/Operational: Events 8001/8002/11004 for new adapters and SSIDs.
  • Switch syslog: port-security violations, MAC flaps (the same MAC jumping between ports), unexpected MACs on access ports.
  • Device-control consoles (endpoint DLP, USB peripheral managers) and reception or mail-room logs.

4.4 Why this matters to the SOC

One plug-in can hand the attacker a full user session inside the office network. From there the path is short: steal sign-in tokens, reach the domain, stage ransomware. In air-gapped networks the stakes are even higher, because a single USB bridge is the only path in — and often the only path out for stolen data. Hardware additions also mix physical security with cyber detection: the SOC needs badge logs and device logs side by side. Finally, regulators care. PCI assessors ask how you block rogue devices; ISO 27001 asks how you monitor for them. A working device-arrival pipeline is audit evidence.

4.5 Where it fits in the attack chain

Before T1200, attackers do target research (T1591) and may gather employee names and addresses. T1200 itself is the door. After it, attackers run standard tooling: PowerShell execution (T1059.001), persistence via scheduled tasks or registry keys, command and control over HTTPS (T1071.001), and later exfiltration (T1041). The contrast with T1091 Replication Through Removable Media is worth repeating in your threat model. T1091 spreads payloads from drives that users already own. T1200 instead introduces hardware with a new function. Watch for both, but expect device-arrival telemetry only from T1200.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

Where threat intel comes from

📰Open sources (OSINT)

Vendor blogs (Securelist, ESET, Red Canary, Trellix), CISA alerts, abuse.ch URLhaus, AlienVault OTX, and BBB scam alerts. The FIN7 USB mailer wave was first publicly documented in the FBI's January 2022 flash alert, and the Better Business Bureau followed with its own alert.

🧭ATT&CK-based intel

The official T1200 page lists the DarkVishnya group (G0105) as the procedure example, plus mitigations M1035 and M1034 and detection strategy DET0069. Build your threat model directly from these objects.

💳Commercial feeds

Financial-sector ISAC feeds (FS-ISAC) and vendor intel portals flag mailer infrastructure: sender addresses, lure themes, and C2 domains like the look-alike CDN hosts used to serve payloads.

🏭Internal telemetry

Your own device-control inventory, DHCP lease history, badge logs, and mail-room records. For this technique, internal data is often the strongest source — nobody knows your baseline better than you.

The intel cycle for this technique

  1. Define priorities. Decide which entry paths hurt most: mailed USBs to office staff, implants at switch ports, or media in air-gapped plants.
  2. Collect. Pull device-arrival logs, DHCP leases, mail-room notices, and external reports about hardware campaigns.
  3. Process. Normalize into one schema: MAC, VID/PID, serial, host, user, time. Tag known-good devices from your baseline.
  4. Analyze. Join arrival events with the 60-minute follow-on activity window described in ATT&CK's DET0069 chain.
  5. Produce. Turn findings into hunts and rules — the Sigma set in 5.5 — and into short SOC playbooks.
  6. Feedback. After each drill or true positive, update the known-good device list and the lure examples used in training.

5.4 Logs & Patterns

Each threat below gets one realistic log sample in the format your SIEM will actually ingest, plus the pattern to look for. Samples use correct, real event IDs only. All hosts, users, MACs, and domains are invented lab examples.

Threat #1 — BadUSB / keyboard emulator auto-types PowerShell

A device is plugged in and Windows happily installs it as a second keyboard (HID). Within seconds it types a PowerShell download cradle. The strongest single record is a 4104 script block with no interactive PowerShell session behind it. Pair it with the Kernel-PnP 410 event for the new HID device in the same minute and the pattern is almost never a false alarm.

    <EventID>4104</EventID>

  • Script block Path is empty and the parent chain has no visible console — a signature of typed input.
  • Workstation normally used for office work only, no history of PowerShell development.
Threat #2 — Malicious USB storage auto-executed payload

The drive holds a lure file such as Employee_Rewards.lnk. A double-click (or the old autorun path) runs cmd, which starts PowerShell from the removable volume. Sysmon Event 1 records the process, and CurrentDirectory pointing at E:\ betrays the volume type. Join with Sysmon 11 file-create events on the same drive letter. This stays T1200, not T1091: the drive is attacker-added hardware planted for the target. T1091 would be malware copying itself onto drives that are already in use.

Threat #3 — Network implant plugged into office LAN port

A tiny box in the ceiling or behind a cabinet asks DHCP for an address. On a VLAN without 802.1X, it gets one — a lease to a MAC the DHCP server has never served. The isc-dhcpd log below shows the classic three-line pattern; NAC products (Cisco ISE, FortiNAC) would log the same as an unknown endpoint or a failed posture check.

dhcpd syslog — unknown client gets a lease

  • Hostname strings like raspberrypi, debian, or default vendor names on office hardware.
  • 802.1X failure or MAB fallback (MAC authentication bypass — letting a device in by its MAC address only) in NAC logs for the same switch port seconds earlier.

Threat #5 — Rogue LAN drop box tunnels traffic out

DarkVishnya-style: a planted computer sits on an internal port and tunnels traffic out. Two switch records catch it. First, port security flags a new MAC on an access port. Seconds later the firewall log shows a fresh outbound VPN handshake from that same office subnet to an external host on an unusual port.

Cisco IOS syslog — port violation + outbound tunnel
Nov  4 10:21:07.114: %PORT_SECURITY-2-PSECURE_VIOLATION: Violation occurred on interface GigabitEthernet1/0/14, caused by MAC address 00e0.4c68.1f9a.
Nov  4 10:21:07.120: %LINK-3-UPDOWN: Interface GigabitEthernet1/0/14, changed state to err-disabled
Nov  4 10:21:09.882: %SEC-6-IPACCESSLOGP: list BLOCK-TUNNEL denied udp 10.20.31.44(51820) -> 198.51.100.77(51820), 1 packet
  • PSECURE_VIOLATION on user access ports — any occurrence is worth a look.
  • Outbound UDP 51820 (WireGuard), 1194 (OpenVPN), or TCP 443 to raw-IP hosts from office subnets.
  • Same MAC appearing on DHCP (Threat #3 pattern) and on the switch within one hour.
  • Port goes err-disabled: check who badges into that room the same morning.

A USB wireless adapter is plugged into a wired-only, restricted machine and joins a non-corporate SSID. The device looks approved: same vendor, same product line. But its serial changed, meaning the drive was replaced or re-flashed. Sysmon Event 13 records the new USBSTOR enumeration key. Alone it is Low severity; combined with other tricks it is how an implant hides inside your allow-list.

Real-World Case Study

Who: FIN7, a financially motivated crime group long linked to the Carbanak/Carbon Spider ecosystem, known for stealing payment-card data from hospitality and retail companies. What: between roughly 2021 and 2022, FIN7 ran a USB-mailer campaign: physical letters sent to hundreds of US employees. Targets included companies in the hospitality, construction, travel, insurance, and defense industries. Why famous: it is the cleanest public example of T1200 at mail scale. No phishing email, no web link — a real envelope, a real USB drive, and the employee's own curiosity. The FBI documented the wave in a January 2022 flash alert. The Better Business Bureau then collected around one hundred complaints and published its own public alert in August 2022; security vendors tracked the same wave.

The letters looked official and harmless. One wave impersonated Best Buy: a note congratulating the recipient on a "$50 gift card" and instructing them to plug in the enclosed USB drive and follow instructions. Another wave, per the FBI flash alert, impersonated the US health agency HHS with COVID-19 themed letters. The drive inside was modified hardware — a BadUSB that presented itself as both a storage device and a keyboard at the same time.

How the attack chain worked

  1. A mailed letter creates trust with an official-looking offer and a physical "gift".
  2. The employee plugs the USB drive into an office PC — T1200 happens here.
  3. The drive claims a second identity as a keyboard and types a launch command.
  4. A batch file and PowerShell downloader pull the real tooling from attacker infrastructure.
  5. Backdoors call out to their C2 on a schedule; operators move to card-data systems and credentials.
  6. Data leaves the network the same way many others do — over HTTPS, quietly.

How it was detected

For most victims, the first "detection" was a human one: a colleague recognized the gift-card letter as odd, or the BBB alert reached their team. Technically, the strongest signals were all visible in logs that nobody was watching. Those logs include PowerShell 4104 script blocks with download cradles and no interactive session. They also show a new HID keyboard arrival (Kernel-PnP 410) seconds before, and outbound HTTPS to freshly registered look-alike domains. Teams that ran the Sigma Rule #1 and #6 patterns from this guide could have caught the plug-in within the first minute.

Containment

  1. Ask users to stop using unsolicited drives immediately and hand any device to security.
  2. Isolate hosts that logged a new HID device plus a 4104 script block in the same window.
  3. Block the known mailer and payload domains at proxy and DNS level.
  4. Hunt the whole fleet for the same chain (device arrival + script block + new outbound HTTPS).

Recovery

  1. Reimage affected workstations rather than trusting cleanup on a machine that ran implant tooling.
  2. Rotate credentials used on those machines, with priority on domain and payment systems.
  3. Deploy device control: block unknown USB devices, alert on HID installs, log 6416 everywhere.
  4. Brief staff with the real letters as pictures — a known lure is far easier to refuse.

Lessons learned

  • Hardware additions beat every mail filter — physical delivery is a real attack channel, not a movie plot.
  • A device can be two things at once; classify USB devices by behavior, not by what the label says.
  • Device-arrival logging (6416, Kernel-PnP) is cheap to enable and nearly free of noise if you baseline.
  • PowerShell 4104 with a download cradle plus a fresh HID arrival is a high-fidelity, low-effort detection.
  • Staff training must include physical lures: unsolicited gifts, chargers, and drives.
  • Mail rooms are a security control point — screen or quarantine unsolicited electronics.
  • Share the case internally; FIN7 kept working by swapping lures after public warnings.
Why this case is pure T1200

Nothing in the FIN7 mailer chain is T1091. The drive was not ordinary media that copied files — it added a new function (a keyboard) to the victim machine and acted on its own. That is exactly the ATT&CK distinction for T1200. A related campaign shows the same technique against critical infrastructure: CISA advisory AA22-077A (April 2022) described USB baiting used to deliver ICS-destroying malware in Ukraine. Microsoft later linked that cluster to the Sandworm group. One technique, from retail card theft to power-grid sabotage.

Mitigation & Defense

Defense in depth

No single control stops a USB “gift”. The winning stack is: policy first, hardware control second, monitoring third, people last — each layer catches what the previous one misses.

ATT&CK lists two official mitigations for T1200, and they matter more than anything else here. The first is M1034 Limit Hardware Installation — block unknown devices and accessories with endpoint security configuration and monitoring agents. The second is M1035 Limit Access to Resource Over Network — device certificates, 802.1X, and DHCP restricted to registered devices. M1049 Antivirus/Antimalware and M1017 User Training support the same goal. Everything below builds on those four.

Enterprise defenses

🚫Device control & allow-listing (M1034)

Use endpoint device-control (Defender for Endpoint, EDR suites, or dedicated USB managers) to block unknown devices by class, VID/PID, and serial. On locked-down hosts, block HID-over-USB entirely — a workstation that never needs a new keyboard should never accept one.

🔌Network access control (M1035)

Enforce 802.1X with device certificates on all switch ports, restrict DHCP to registered MACs, and enable port security with err-disable. NAC platforms (Cisco ISE, FortiNAC) turn a plugged-in implant into a failed port instead of a foothold.

✉️Mail & parcel screening

Give the mail room a simple rule: no unsolicited electronics reach desks unopened. Quarantine gift-like devices, photograph the parcel, and hand items to security for a safe review.

🧱Hardening & unused ports

Seal unused network ports and USB sockets in public areas. On high-risk hosts, disable mass-storage and new HID drivers via policy. Use charge-only (data-blocker) cables at kiosks and lobbies.

📡Monitoring & detection

Ship 6416, Kernel-PnP 400/410/420, PowerShell 4104, Sysmon, DHCP, WLAN, and switch syslog to the SIEM. Deploy the 5.5 rules and alert on the chain: new device → typed script → new process → new connection.

🎓User training (M1017)

Teach one rule people remember: never plug in anything you did not order or check in with IT — especially “free gifts”. Show real FIN7-style letters during onboarding.

🦠Antivirus / EDR (M1049)

Keep AV/EDR active with script inspection so the typed PowerShell download dies at AMSI or is blocked at download time. This caps the damage when a device still gets plugged in.

🧪Lab jails & response readiness

Review unknown media only inside an isolated “USB jail” VM with no network. Keep an IR runbook for hardware implants: preserve the device, isolate the host, hunt the chain.

Home user defenses

  • Never plug in a USB drive or charger you found, received by mail, or got at an event.
  • Keep OS and antivirus updates on; modern browsers and Defender block many typed downloaders.
  • Use a charge-only cable or power bank when charging in public places.
  • Lock your screen whenever you step away — even at home with visitors.
  • Destroy old drives before disposal; do not resell or give away unknown media.
  • On your router, turn off WPS and use WPA2/WPA3 — rogue bridges work best against old setups.
  • Report a suspicious device to your IT team instead of testing it “to see what it is”.

Best practices for day-to-day SOC work

  1. Run hypothesis hunts. “Which hosts saw a new HID device and a 4104 script block this week?” is a five-minute query with huge value.
  2. Purple-team the hardware path. Use a lab HID emulator to replay the FIN7-style chain and verify each 5.5 rule fires.
  3. Close the feedback loop. Every true positive updates the known-good device baseline and the training deck.
  4. Document the baseline. Approved devices, serials, SSIDs, and switch ports — detection is only as good as the “normal” it compares to.
  5. Measure MTTD for device events. Time from arrival alert to triage decision is your hardware-channel metric; drive it down.
  6. Correlate physical and cyber. Badge-in records next to device events turn “something happened” into “who was there”.
  7. Keep intel flowing. Watch CISA advisories and BBB alerts for new lure waves; add them to the hunt list monthly.
Final word

T1200 wins when nobody owns the space between the door and the desktop. Give one team the device logs, one rule the mail room, and one drill per quarter to your SOC. Then the next “gift card” in an envelope becomes an alert, not an incident.
