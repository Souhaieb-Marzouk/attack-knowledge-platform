T1667 Email Bombing

Attackers subscribe a victim's email address to hundreds of newsletters at once. The inbox drowns in junk — people sometimes describe this as "injecting" thousands of junk messages into a mailbox. Real security alerts get buried under the flood, and then a fake "IT support" call arrives to help. This guide shows you how the attack works, what it leaves in your logs, and how to detect and stop it.

Overview

What Email Bombing is, why attackers do it, and what you will learn on this page.

What is Email Bombing?

Email Bombing (MITRE ATT&CK T1667) is when an attacker floods one person's mailbox — their email inbox — with an overwhelming number of messages. A mailbox is the storage space where a person's email lives. The most common method is simple and cheap: automated programs called signup bots register the victim's address on hundreds of newsletters, web shops, and mailing lists that do not check that the address really belongs to the person who typed it. Every signup sends a welcome or confirmation email. Hundreds of signups mean thousands of emails.

This is not classic spam. Ordinary spam is noise that filters catch. Email Bombing is a deliberate attack listed under the Impact tactic of MITRE ATT&CK. (MITRE ATT&CK is a public catalog that describes how real attackers behave; a tactic is the attacker's goal, and Impact means the goal is to disrupt or damage something.) The declared impact type is Availability — the flood makes the mailbox unusable. But the hidden goal is often worse: the junk buries real security alerts, such as a multi-factor authentication (MFA) push or a password-reset email, so the victim never sees them.

MFA (multi-factor authentication) means signing in with something extra besides the password, like an app approval on your phone. Many alerts about MFA changes arrive by email — exactly the messages the flood hides.

Warning — the flood is a setup, not the crime

In the pattern that security vendors reported through 2024 and 2025, the flood is step one. Step two is a phone or Teams call from someone pretending to be IT support, offering to "fix your email problem". The caller walks the victim through a remote-access session or an MFA reset — and takes over the account. The danger is not the junk mail. It is what the junk mail hides and what follows it.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Email Bombing usually appears early or mid-way in an attack, not at the end. A typical chain looks like this:

  1. Find the target address. The attacker collects the address from a website, a leak, or a guess based on name patterns.
  2. Flood the mailbox (T1667 — this technique). Signup bots bury the inbox, or a mail server sends junk directly. The victim's Availability is the stated impact.
  3. Call and impersonate (T1598 Spearphishing Voice, T1656 Impersonation). The attacker poses as IT and offers help with the "email problem".
  4. Take over the account. The victim grants a remote session or re-registers MFA. The attacker now reads the mailbox (T1114 Email Collection).
  5. Monetize. Ransomware, invoice fraud, or direct Financial Theft (T1657). Phishing from the trusted mailbox (T1566) is also common.

Key terms in plain words

Inbox flood

Thousands of junk emails arriving in a short window, so the mailbox becomes unreadable and often hits its size limit.

Signup bot

A script that fills web signup forms automatically. If a form has no "confirm your email" step (double opt-in), the victim gets junk they never asked for.

MFA / security info

Extra sign-in proof, like an authenticator app. "Security info" is the registered list of phones and apps used for MFA. Changes to it are high-value events.

NDR (non-delivery report)

An automatic "your message could not be delivered" email, also called a bounce. Floods can turn a victim's own address into an NDR storm generator.

SOC (Security Operations Center)

The team that watches security alerts and investigates suspicious activity, usually working inside a SIEM.

Message trace

A per-email log kept by mail platforms such as Microsoft 365 (Exchange Online). It records sender, recipient, time, subject, and delivery status for every message.

OSINT

Open-source intelligence: information collected from public sources such as websites, social media, and paste sites. Attackers use OSINT to find addresses; defenders use it to spot leaked ones.

BCL

Bulk Complaint Level, Microsoft EOP's bulk-mail score (0–9; higher means the message looks more bulk-like). Floods ride on bulk mail, so BCL is a useful extra signal.

DMARC, SPF, DKIM

Three standards that prove mail really comes from the domain it claims. Enforced outbound, they stop attackers forging your addresses for bounce storms.

Frequently asked questions

Is this just spam with a scary name?

No. Spam is a side effect of marketing; Email Bombing is a deliberate attack with a target, a window, and usually a follow-up move. The same volume has completely different meaning when it is aimed, timed, and paired with a phone call.

Why not just block the senders?

You can, and you should throttle obvious bulk sources. But the fan-out uses hundreds of legitimate services, and the bots keep adding more. Recipient-side volume control is the durable fix; sender lists are cleanup.

Does MFA stop this?

MFA stops password-only attacks — which is one reason attackers switch to this pattern. The flood does not defeat MFA; the convincing phone call that follows tries to make the victim hand over the second factor.

Who is a likely target?

Anyone whose address is public and who can approve payments, reset access, or speak for the company: finance staff, executives, service desks, and IT admins. Service mailboxes are targets too, because nobody calls IT about them quickly.

Note — about the file name

The official ATT&CK title for T1667 is Email Bombing. You may also see the word "injection" used informally, because the flood "injects" thousands of junk messages into a mailbox. Both phrases describe the same technique; this page uses the official title.

The narrative arc of the attack

Watch the simulation with this arc in mind. First silence: recon, nothing visible. Then noise: signups and the flood — loud but unclear. Then burial: the real alert disappears; that is the strategic goal. Then contact: the fake call — the human trap. Then theft: access and persistence — the crime. Last comes the reckoning: correlation and containment. Each phase maps to a different log source, which is why single-source monitoring keeps missing it.

Written walkthrough — the same 8 steps
  1. Step 1 — Target picked. The attacker collects an employee's address, for example from a website or a leaked list. Defender view: nothing is visible yet. No logs show anything, because no mail has been sent.
  2. Step 2 — Bot signups. Automated bots type the victim's address into hundreds of newsletter forms that skip email confirmation. Defender view: if someone looks, a trickle of "welcome" emails from unknown domains starts arriving. Nothing alerts yet.
  3. Step 3 — Inbox flood. Welcome and confirmation emails arrive in waves, thousands within a few hours. A counter on the mailbox climbs fast. Defender view: message trace shows an abnormal inbound rate for one recipient, spread over dozens of unrelated sender domains.
  4. Step 4 — Real alert buried. A genuine security email — an MFA test push confirmation or password-reset notice — lands in the inbox and is pushed out of sight within seconds. Defender view: the platform still delivered the real alert normally. Only message-trace and audit logs prove it existed.
  5. Step 5 — Fake IT call. The attacker phones or Teams-calls the victim, claiming to be IT support fixing the "email problem". Defender view: no mail evidence. A call log or a help-desk system entry may be the only trace, if one is recorded at all.
  6. Step 6 — Access granted. The victim follows the caller's instructions and allows a remote-assist session or re-registers MFA. Defender view: the identity platform writes an audit event, for example "User registered security info". This is the first loud, high-value signal.

  8. Step 8 — SOC response. The SOC correlates the mail-volume spike, the help-desk contact, and the MFA change. Sessions are revoked, the user is verified by a callback to a known number, and the bot sources are blocked. Defender view: correlated alerts in the SIEM plus containment actions in the audit trail.

Red team / attacker view

Why attackers choose Email Bombing, how the attack is built, and where it usually breaks. This section is educational: all examples run only against your own lab.

Why attackers use it

Email Bombing is cheap, fast, and needs no malware. A rented bot network and a few hours are enough. It gives the attacker three things at once:

  • Noise. Real alerts disappear. Password resets, MFA prompts, and admin notifications get lost in thousands of welcome emails.
  • Urgency. A broken mailbox is stressful and visible to the victim, which makes a surprise "IT support" call feel welcome instead of suspicious.
  • Cover. When thousands of unrelated domains send the junk, crude blocklists struggle, and defenders may waste time chasing the senders instead of the story behind them.

How the attack is performed (conceptual)

  1. Harvest the address. Public websites, conference lists, leaked databases, or simple name patterns like first.last@company.com.
  2. Prepare the bots. The attacker sets up automation that can fill web forms with a chosen email address, often rotating through many IP addresses so no single source looks strange.
  3. Fan out the signups. The bots submit the address to hundreds of newsletters and shops that have no double opt-in. Each one sends a welcome email to the victim.
  4. Optionally add direct-send pressure. Some attackers skip newsletters and push mail straight at the company's mail servers from bulk hosts, stuffing random junk characters into subjects and bodies so content filters see a different message every time. This is sometimes called NOP padding, a name borrowed from older buffer-overflow tricks.
  5. Time the follow-up. While the inbox is drowning, the fake IT call arrives. The flood sets the scene; the call is the attack.
Danger — Lab only

Never point these techniques at a real person's mailbox or a real newsletter service: that is harassment and, in many countries, a crime. Prerequisites the attacker needs

A known address

The victim's exact email address, plus a plausible story about who they are and which IT team "supports" them.

Unprotected signup forms

Services that send a real email on every signup without asking the owner to click a confirmation link first.

Throwaway infrastructure

Bot hosts, burner phone numbers or spoofed caller ID, and a second identity for the fake IT persona.

Common variations

Newsletter fan-out

The default. Thousands of small, harmless senders. Hard to block per sender; easy to catch per recipient volume.

Direct-send flood

Bulk mail pushed straight at the tenant from a few servers, often with junk padding. Louder, easier to block by IP, but faster.

Bounce storm

The attacker forges the victim's address as the sender of junk to third parties. The replies and non-delivery reports (NDRs) flow back and flood the mailbox "for free".

Service-mailbox bombing

Instead of one person, shared boxes like finance@, it@, or hr@ get flooded so invoice threads and tickets go missing — a setup for payment fraud.

Cost and capability profile

What it costs the attacker

A few rented bot hosts, throwaway phone numbers, and hours of scripting. No exploits, no malware, no stolen credentials. This is why commodity actors can run it — and why you cannot rely on "we are too small to be a target".

What skill it needs

Low. The hard part is social, not technical: a calm voice, a plausible name, and patience. Expect the human to be better prepared than the tooling.

What it costs you

Analyst hours, mailbox cleanup, days of degraded mail, and — if the call succeeds — a full incident response. The asymmetry favors the attacker until detections exist.

When attackers pick it

When MFA blocks plain password attacks, when phishing filters are strong, or when they already know a help desk answers fast. The flood is often chosen because your other controls work.

Signup bot anatomy, in plain words

A signup bot is boring software — that is what makes it effective. It loads a form, fills the email field, presses submit, reads the result, and moves on. Loops like that run in parallel across many hosts. The only clever parts are form detection and polite pacing, so the requests look like real visitors.

1 · Loader

Reads a list of signup URLs from a file or a control server. Rotates browser fingerprints and proxies.

2 · Filler

Types the victim's address into the form. Some bots even click the confirmation link if the mail arrives — which requires reading the victim's mailbox, so usually they do not.

3 · Pacer

Sleeps between submissions and spreads work across hours, so no single signup service sees an obvious machine.

Where it applies, and where it does not

Cloud office suites

Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace are prime targets: addresses are predictable, signup services are plentiful, and the help desk is one call away. Most public reporting lives here.

On-premises Exchange

Older gateways without bulk controls flood even more easily, and message-tracking data often stays on the server. Detection depends on someone forwarding those logs to a SIEM.

Desktops and laptops

Linux, Windows, or macOS matter little for the flood itself — the mailbox is cloud-side. The endpoint enters the story when the remote-assist tool runs and the attacker works hands-on.

What it is not

Not a malware infection, not a server exploit, and not a filter misconfiguration. If your response plan only handles those three, the flood will exhaust the playbook and the call will still come.

Warning — words about legality and ethics

Mailbox flooding aimed at real people is harassment in many jurisdictions, and using it as a cover for account takeover is computer fraud. Everything in this section exists so defenders and researchers can build and test detections against their own lab systems. Point nothing here at anyone else.

Mistakes attackers make

  • Too-regular cadence. Bots send at machine-like intervals. Human mail never looks like a metronome.
  • Narrow source range. Cheap bot hosts reuse the same IP ranges, so sender IPs cluster even when domains differ.
  • Calling too early or too late. A "support" call minutes after the flood starts, or hours after it ends, breaks the story.
  • Forgetting bounces. A full mailbox starts bouncing mail, which leaves a loud, easy-to-spot trail.
  • Reusing the fake IT identity. The same caller name, number, or Quick Assist link appearing across victims links incidents together.

Blue team / SOC view

What defenders see, which logs matter, and a six-step triage flow you can follow when a mailbox starts drowning.

How it looks from the inside

From the SOC's side, Email Bombing is usually visible long before anyone understands the intent. The mail platform reports a sudden, huge inbound rate for one recipient. The messages come from many unrelated sender domains, and each one alone is harmless — a welcome email, a receipt, a "please confirm". Nothing matches a phishing rule. That is the trap: if you only look at message content, the attack is invisible. If you look at volume per mailbox, the numbers stand out clearly.

The second signal class appears when the attacker moves from noise to action: identity audit events such as "User registered security info", password resets, new mailbox rules, or a remote-assist session nobody requested. The core of the detection is joining the mail-volume signal with the identity signal.

Artifacts the attack leaves

In the mail platform

  • Message-trace records: hundreds of inbound messages per hour for one RecipientAddress, from dozens of distinct SenderAddress values and domains.
  • High bulk-complaint scores (BCL — Microsoft's bulk mail rating) with low spam scores.
  • Mailbox quota warnings, and NDRs when the mailbox fills up.

In identity and endpoint logs

  • Entra ID (Microsoft's identity service) audit events: security-info registration, MFA changes, password resets.
  • Mailbox rule creation that hides vendor or security mail.
  • Remote-assist tool launches, for example Quick Assist, plus new sign-ins from odd locations.

At the help desk

  • Tickets or calls: "my inbox is full of junk", "email is broken".
  • A suspiciously convenient call from "IT" that was never logged as a real ticket.

In the SIEM

  • The correlation itself: a mail surge window overlapping a help-desk contact and an MFA change for the same user.
  • Analyst notes and containment actions in the case record.

Where to look, concretely

  • Microsoft 365: message trace (Exchange admin center or Defender for Office 365 campaign views), Exchange Online Protection (EOP — Microsoft's mail filtering layer) anti-spam reports, and the Unified Audit Log for identity events.
  • Google Workspace: Gmail log search and admin audit for spam quarantines and message volume per user.
  • Third-party gateways: Proofpoint Targeted Attack Protection or an on-premises Exchange message-tracking log.
  • Help desk: ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, or Zendesk tickets, plus telephony and Teams call logs.
  • Identity: Entra ID audit and sign-in logs, MFA method changes, risky sign-ins.

Why it matters, and what comes before and after

Email Bombing sits in the middle of the chain. Before it, the attacker only reconnoitered — nothing burns. After it come the techniques that cost money: T1598 Spearphishing Voice (the fake call), T1656 Impersonation, T1114 Email Collection (reading the mailbox), T1657 Financial Theft, and T1566 Phishing from the trusted mailbox. If you treat the flood as a spam nuisance and close the ticket, you usually discover the real incident days later — as a ransom note or a drained vendor payment.

Six-step triage flow

  1. Confirm the surge window. Pull message trace for the affected mailbox and mark the exact start and end of the abnormal inbound rate, including the top sender domains and IPs.
  2. List what was buried. Query mail flow and audit logs for security-relevant messages and events inside that window: password resets, MFA prompts, new-device notices, administrator alerts.
  3. Reach the user on a verified channel. Call back on the number from the HR system — never a number offered in the suspicious call or a reply to the flooded mailbox.
  4. Check identity and mailbox changes. Review MFA method registrations, password changes, mailbox rules, and delegate or app access created during or after the surge.
  5. Contain. If anything checks out badly: revoke sessions, force a password reset, remove rogue MFA methods and rules, and block the flood sources at the gateway.
  6. Record and watch. Save the message-trace evidence, open a case, and keep the mailbox under heightened monitoring for at least the following week, because repeat contact is common.

5.2

Threat intelligence

Where the intelligence comes from

  • MITRE ATT&CK itself — the T1667 page lists the Storm-1811 procedure example, and MITRE publishes a matching detection strategy, DET0355, built around an abnormally high volume of inbound email to a single mailbox.
  • Vendor blogs and reports — through 2024 and 2025 several security vendors documented the same pattern: mass junk signups, then a help-desk call, then remote-access tooling and sometimes ransomware. Vendors generally avoid publishing every cluster name, so treat these as pattern intelligence.
  • ISACs and industry groups — sector sharing communities circulate flood campaigns and fake help-desk numbers that hit peers in your industry.
  • Commercial feeds — reputation lists for signup-bot infrastructure and temporary mail hosts can enrich your mail logs.
  • OSINT — open-source intelligence such as paste sites and researcher threads often exposes the newsletter services most abused for fan-out.
  • Internal telemetry — your own message trace is the best source: baselines of normal per-mailbox volume make every anomaly obvious.
  • Tracking the trackers — subscribe to the RSS or changelog feeds of the ATT&CK pages for this technique and its related ones; new procedure examples and detection strategies appear there first.

Turning intel into detections

Work from the pattern, not from single indicators. Sender domains change hourly; the shape of the attack — one recipient, many unique senders, machine-like timing, followed by an identity event — stays stable. Give each piece of intelligence a confidence level and let it drive different actions: High confidence feeds automated detection logic, Medium confidence feeds hunting queries, Low confidence only enriches context.

A simple collection plan

  1. Define the question. Example: "Which sender infrastructures and follow-up scripts are used against our sector this quarter?" Vague questions produce unusable feeds.
  2. Pick two or three sources that answer it: ATT&CK updates, two vendor blogs, and your ISAC. More sources add noise, not signal.
  3. Extract patterns, not indicators. Sender IPs die in days; the shape — one mailbox, many domains, a following identity event — lives for years.
  4. Write the detection (section 5.5), then record which intel source motivated it, so you can retire or tune it when the pattern shifts.
  5. Review quarterly. Check detection hit rates against the sources; drop feeds that never contribute.

Worked example — from intel to rule in five lines

  1. Read the reporting. Vendor posts in 2024–2025 describe bombing followed by a help-desk call. ATT&CK confirms the Storm-1811 procedure on the T1667 page.
  2. Extract the pattern. The transferable part is not an IP address; it is the sequence: surge, then contact, then security-info change.
  3. Check your data. Do you keep message trace? Entra audit? Tickets? If any leg is missing, that becomes the logging task, not a detection task.
  4. Write the rule. The TH-01 Sigma rule plus its SPL pairing encodes exactly that sequence with a 24-hour window.
  5. Record the lineage. Note in the rule description: "motivated by ATT&CK T1667 Storm-1811 procedure and 2024–2025 vendor reporting". Next year, when the pattern shifts, you know which rule to revisit.

Threat ranking and categorization

Six realistic threats built on T1667, ranked by combined impact and likelihood. Severity reflects a typical mid-size organization; adjust for your own environment.

TH-01 — Alert burial + fake help-desk takeover Critical

Description. The flood hides MFA and password alerts. The attacker then impersonates the victim — or impersonates IT to the victim — by phone or Teams, gets an MFA reset or a remote session, and takes over the identity. This is the documented Storm-1811 pattern.

Preconditions. Target address known; help desk verifies callers weakly (no callback to a known number); remote-assist tools available to any user.

Impact. Full identity compromise: mailbox, files, and often a stepping stone to ransomware. Recovery costs far exceed the mail problem.

Why Critical. It converts a nuisance into account takeover within hours, and the buried alerts mean the victim may never self-report.

Detection coverage. TH-01 rule (security-info registration) plus the SPL pairing; supported by TH-02's surge alert as the left side of the pair and by ticket monitoring for the call.

TH-02 — Mailbox quota exhaustion High

Description. Subscription waves fill the mailbox until it hits its size limit. New mail bounces: invoices, one-time passcodes, customer replies, system notifications.

Preconditions. Attacker knows the address; the mailbox has a modest quota; auto-expanding archives are off.

Impact. Direct availability loss for the person and anyone who emails them. Missed OTPs (one-time passcodes) can lock the victim out of other systems, which the attacker can exploit socially.

Why High. Business disruption is immediate and measurable, and it degrades the victim's ability to receive the very alerts that would expose the attack.

Detection coverage. TH-02 per-recipient volume rule; quota-bounce events as confirmation; the baseline worksheet keeps thresholds honest.

TH-03 — Direct-send flood with padding High

Description. Instead of abusing newsletters, the attacker pushes mail straight at the tenant from a few servers. They stuff random junk characters (NOP padding) into subjects and bodies. Every message then looks unique, so content filters cannot correlate them.

Preconditions. The tenant's MX hosts (the DNS records that receive your mail) are reachable; direct-send is not blocked; sender-IP reputation is rented or spoofed.

Impact. Faster flooding with fewer dependencies; filter evasion; gateway load. Often combined with TH-01 or TH-02.

Why High. It defeats naive content-based detections and can saturate the same detection budget that should be watching for the real attack.

Detection coverage. TH-03 per-IP burst rule with SPF conditions; gateway and firewall logs as corroborating sources.

TH-04 — Service-mailbox bombing Medium

Description. Shared boxes — finance@, it@, hr@ — are flooded so invoice threads and support tickets get buried. A follow-up call or email then redirects a payment or extracts data, tying this to Financial Theft (T1657).

Preconditions. Public service addresses; no per-mailbox volume monitoring on shared boxes; no out-of-band verification for payment changes.

Impact. Missed invoices and tickets; payment-diversion fraud; slow response to real security mail because nobody "owns" the noise.

Why Medium. Impact is usually financial-but-recoverable, yet the attack is common and often unnoticed for days.

Detection coverage. TH-04 ticket-cluster rule plus explicit inclusion of shared mailboxes in TH-02 scope; payment-change verification as the process backstop.

TH-05 — Bounce-storm amplification Medium

Description. The flood (or a forged sender) triggers automatic replies, vacation responses, and non-delivery reports that hammer the tenant's own infrastructure and hurt its sender reputation. The victim becomes an amplifier of their own outage.

Preconditions. Auto-replies enabled; the mailbox fills; a forged-sender campaign uses the victim's address externally.

Impact. Mail-flow degradation beyond one mailbox, quarantine backlog, and possible listing on blocklists that affects all outbound mail.

Why Medium. Mostly self-inflicted infrastructure pain, but it widens the blast radius and pollutes the evidence trail.

Detection coverage. TH-05 NDR-storm rule; EOP connector reports and reputation dashboards as secondary confirmation.

TH-06 — Targeted harassment bombing Low

Description. An individual — often an executive or a public-facing employee — is bombed to silence them and cause stress, sometimes during a dispute or a layoff period. Usually no financial motive and no follow-up call.

Preconditions. A known personal or work address; attacker motivated by grievance rather than money.

Impact. Availability and wellbeing harm for the person; occasional rule-of-thumb escalation to threats that HR or legal must handle.

Why Low. Serious for the individual but rarely affects business availability or security posture at scale. Still track it: harassment can be a precursor to insider-risk events.

Detection coverage. TH-06 VIP-list volume rule; HR and executive-assistant reports as the human channel.

TH-01 — Alert burial and fake help-desk takeover

Log sources: Microsoft 365 message trace, EOP anti-spam verdicts, Entra ID audit log (Unified Audit Log), help-desk system, Teams call logs. Key fields: RecipientAddress, SenderAddress, Direction, Operation, UserId, ticket caller_id.

Normal: a mailbox gets 20–80 inbound messages per hour, mostly from recurring senders; MFA changes are rare and announced. Suspicious: hundreds per hour from dozens of unique domains, then a security-info registration within 24 hours that the user cannot explain on a verified callback.

Detection logic (plain language): watch two facts together. First: a user receives more than 100 inbound messages in one hour from more than 30 unique sender domains. Second: the same user registers or changes security info within the next 24 hours. If both are true, raise a critical alert.

Hunt steps: first, run the surge query for the last 7 days and note every user over threshold. Second, for each, pull all security-info and password operations for the following 24 hours. Third, read the help-desk calls for those users — the fake technician often appears there before anything else looks wrong.

Field notes: the three message-trace rows stand for ten near-identical rows within one minute — only the sender changes. The Entra audit row is the pivot: a security-info registration right after a surge is the takeover moment. The ticket shows the social step; note the unverified caller identity.

TH-02 — Mailbox quota exhaustion

Log sources: message trace (bounce and deferred status codes), mailbox usage reports, Gmail log search, ticket system. Key fields: RecipientAddress, Status (SMTP codes), Size, Direction.

Normal: occasional single bounces; mailbox usage grows slowly. Suspicious: inbound rate jumps, then a burst of 5.2.2 (mailbox full) and 4.3.1 (deferred) statuses, and quota warnings for the same mailbox.

Detection logic (plain language): alert when a mailbox accumulates more than 200 inbound messages in one hour, or when more than 10 inbound messages to the same mailbox bounce with a quota-related status within 30 minutes.

Field notes: Status carries the SMTP failure code; 5.2.2 means the mailbox is full. A real invoice failing right after the surge is exactly the business damage this threat measures. Native message trace shows plain Failed here; the code itself appears in message-trace detail and in the NDR — this sample condenses both.

Hunt steps: chart inbound volume per mailbox for the last 30 days to set baselines. Then list every quota bounce and warning in the last week. Finally, check whether any bounced message was itself a security alert — those are the buried notifications TH-01 feeds on.

TH-03 — Direct-send flood with padding

Log sources: message trace with sender IP and SPF verdicts, EOP or gateway logs (Proofpoint, on-premises Exchange transport), firewall/syslog from mail hosts. Key fields: SenderIP, SPFResult, Subject, SenderAddress.

Normal: big senders use many IPs but pass SPF and keep a stable subject style. Suspicious: a very high message count from a few IPs, SPF failing or missing, and subjects stuffed with random characters.

Detection logic (plain language): alert when one SenderIP delivers more than 300 inbound messages in 30 minutes, or when more than 20 unique sender addresses arrive from a single IP in 30 minutes, especially with SPF fail or none.

Field notes: SPFResult is exposed by many gateways; in plain Microsoft message trace it may need to come from EOP reports — state your ingest assumption. The junk in Subject defeats exact-match filters; the IP count defeats that too.

Hunt steps: group inbound traffic by SenderIP over the last week and rank by message count. Inspect the top ten for unique-sender spread and SPF verdicts. Ask your mail team whether direct-send is even needed — if not, blocking it removes this threat class outright.

TH-04 — Service-mailbox bombing

Log sources: message trace for shared mailboxes, ticket system (ServiceNow, Jira, Zendesk), finance ERP mailboxes, Teams or phone logs. Key fields: RecipientAddress (shared boxes), ticket short_description, caller_id, assignment_group.

Normal: service mailboxes see steady, mixed traffic; invoice threads flow daily. Suspicious: a spike on one shared box plus a cluster of "my invoice disappeared" tickets, or an external "correction" email right after a flood window.

Detection logic (plain language): alert when a shared mailbox receives more than 150 inbound messages in one hour, or when more than 5 tickets per 24 hours mention a flooded or broken service mailbox.

Field notes: RecipientAddress for shared boxes looks like any user, so include finance@-style boxes explicitly in monitoring scope. The ticket text is often the first human clue that real mail got buried.

Hunt steps: list all shared and service mailboxes and check which are covered by monitoring. Search tickets mentioning those boxes in the last month. Cross-check any payment-change requests that arrived during ticket clusters — that overlap is the fraud setup.

TH-05 — Bounce-storm amplification

Log sources: message trace (outbound NDRs and auto-replies), EOP connector reports, gateway reputation dashboards. Key fields: SenderAddress (the victim becoming "sender" of NDRs), Subject (Undeliverable), Direction, RecipientAddress.

Normal: a handful of NDRs per hour per tenant; auto-replies are rare. Suspicious: one address generating dozens of NDRs per hour, or the tenant's outbound volume dominated by postmaster/Undeliverable traffic.

Detection logic (plain language): alert when any internal address sends more than 50 NDRs or auto-replies in one hour, or when NDRs exceed 20 percent of tenant outbound volume in an hour.

Field notes: the NDR's Subject quotes the original message, which lets you trace the flood. The EOP summary row is the kind of aggregate your mail admin dashboard already computes — pipe it to the SIEM.

Hunt steps: count outbound NDRs per internal address per hour for the last month. For any spike, pull the quoted original subjects to find the flood that caused it. Then review the tenant's outbound reputation trend to see how close the storm came to a blocklist.

TH-06 — Targeted harassment bombing

Log sources: message trace for VIP mailboxes, Google Workspace Gmail log search, ticket/HR reports, gateway logs. Key fields: RecipientAddress (VIP list), SenderAddress, unique sender-domain count.

Normal: executives get steady volume from known senders and newsletters they chose. Suspicious: a sudden ten-fold jump from dozens of never-seen domains, especially outside business hours or right after a public dispute.

Detection logic (plain language): for the VIP list, alert when one recipient gets more than 100 inbound messages in one hour from more than 40 unique sender domains, or when a mailbox exceeds 5 times its 30-day hourly baseline.

Field notes: the Gmail log row shows the same pattern as the Microsoft trace — evidence exists on every major platform. Keep a maintained VIP list; harassment targets are rarely random accounts.

Converting Sigma to Splunk — practical notes

  • Aggregation conditions. Sigma's condition: selection | count() by X > N maps to SPL stats count BY X | where count > N. The timeframe field becomes bin _time span=... plus a scheduled search of matching frequency.

TH-01 — Fake help-desk takeover (MFA change after a surge)

Mapping note. TH-02 — Mailbox quota exhaustion (volume spike)

Mapping note. service: exchange_online maps to the Exchange Online message trace. Quality note. Precision improves with the 5× baseline variant; the fixed 200 threshold favors recall on new mailboxes. Expect your first week to be tuning, not alerting.

TH-03 — Direct-send flood with padding

Mapping note. Basic Microsoft message trace does not always expose SPFResult; EOP reports, Defender for Office 365, or a third-party gateway (Proofpoint TAP, Mimecast) usually do. False positives: large shared sending platforms with broken SPF; a new marketing campaign from one provider. Tuning: raise unique_senders for known bulk providers; add a secondary threshold on Subject entropy to spot padding. Severity: High — fast, filter-evasive flooding that also loads your gateways.

Quality note. The per-IP count is a strong signal but blind to floods spread over many IPs; combine with TH-02 for coverage. Recalls well in gateways that log sender IP.

TH-04 — Service-mailbox bombing (ticket surge)

Mapping note. category: application / product: servicenow maps to your ServiceNow incident export ingested through DB Connect or the ServiceNow add-on into index=helpdesk sourcetype=servicenow:incident. False positives: a real mail-platform outage; attachment-heavy newsletters filling one mailbox by accident. Tuning: whitelist change windows; require at least two distinct callers before paging. Severity: Medium — an early indicator that the bombing has victims calling for help, which is when the fake IT persona typically calls too.

Quality note. Ticket text is free-form, so recall depends on keyword coverage; add your organization's own phrasing from real tickets. Precision rises when you require two or more distinct callers.

TH-05 — Bounce-storm amplification (NDR storm)

Mapping note. NDRs are recorded in message trace with the postmaster address as the nominal sender and the original subject quoted inside Subject. False positives: approved bulk sends to outdated lists; system-generated reports to decommissioned mailboxes. Tuning: baseline tenant NDR rates hourly; alert at 5× baseline and also watch the NDR share of outbound volume (over 20 percent is suspicious). Severity: Medium — self-amplifying noise that widens the outage and pollutes evidence.

Quality note. Watch precision during approved bulk-mailing jobs — the falsepositives list exists because this rule fires there first. The percentage-of-outbound variant is the more stable signal.

A four-step tuning workflow for all six rules

  1. Run in search-only mode for two weeks. Let each rule execute as a scheduled search that writes results to a dashboard. Collect every hit and label it true or false.
  2. Adjust thresholds from your own data. The numbers in this page are starting points from typical mid-size tenants. Your marketing campaigns and mailing-list culture will move them.
  3. Add context before severity. Join hits with the user's department, mailbox type, and recent tickets before raising alert levels; the same volume means different things for finance@ and for a hobby list.
  4. Document the final rule. Record the tuned threshold, the review date, and the last true-positive example. An untuned rule is a liability; a documented one is an asset auditors like.

Mistakes people make when writing these Sigma rules

Hardcoding tenant names in the rule

Rules stop working after a rename or a merger. Keep tenant specifics in lookups and allowlists, not in the selection logic.

Skipping the falsepositives list

An honest noise list is how the next analyst trusts the alert. Omitting it guarantees the rule gets disabled after its first marketing campaign.

Aggregating without a timeframe

A count() by condition without timeframe means "ever", which will eventually fire on history. Always bound the window.

Detecting content instead of behavior

Subject strings and sender names change weekly. Counts, spreads, and sequences survive attacker updates — that is why all six rules count.

Real-world case study

How one email bombing became an attempted payment fraud — and how the SOC caught it.

Composite scenario

This case study is a composite: it is assembled from the documented 2024–2025 Storm-1811 / email-bombing-then-help-desk reporting pattern and common incident-response experience. Names, times, and numbers are invented, and the domains shown are lab domains. It does not describe one specific real company.

Context

Northwind Logistics is a mid-size company on Microsoft 365. MFA is required, but the service desk verifies callers by name and employee number only, and any user can start a remote-assist session. Dana, an accounts-payable clerk, handles vendor invoices by email all day.

Day 1 · 09:12
The flood starts. Signup bots submit Dana's address to hundreds of newsletter services. Message trace later shows roughly 30,000 inbound messages in 3 hours from more than 900 sender domains.
Day 1 · 10:38
A Teams call from "Marcus, IT service desk" reaches Dana: "We're seeing a mail problem on your account, I'll clean it up with you." No ticket is opened. The call is not from the real service desk queue.
Day 1 · 10:52
Dana accepts a Quick Assist remote session. The "technician" shows Dana a fake cleanup screen while opening a second browser window on Dana's own session.
Day 1 · 11:05
Dana re-registers MFA under instruction. Entra ID logs the event "User registered security info" — the attacker's device is now a valid second factor.
Day 1 · 11:15
The attacker creates an inbox rule that moves vendor and security mail to an archive folder, then reads the mailbox and learns the vendor-payment process.
Day 1 · 14:30
From Dana's mailbox, the attacker emails a supplier: "Please update our payment details for invoice 2025-0147" with a new IBAN (international bank account number).
Day 1 · 16:20
The supplier's finance clerk does what policy says: she calls Northwind back on the number stored in the signed contract, not the one in the email. The change is denied. This callback is the moment the fraud dies.
Day 1 · 16:55
The SOC correlates three signals for one user: a mail-volume surge, an "inbox flooded" service-desk ticket, and a security-info registration. The case is opened as a suspected account takeover.
Day 2 · 09:40
Post-incident review. Three actions funded on the spot: service-desk callback policy, SIEM correlation rule for surge-plus-MFA changes, and restriction of Quick Assist to the support group with session logging.
Day 7 · 16:00
Heightened monitoring ends. A second, smaller flood attempt against the same mailbox in week one was caught by the new TH-02 alert in minutes — and contained with a gateway block, no caller this time.

Attack chain

Reconnaissance produced Dana's address from a conference attendee list. T1667 Email Bombing supplied the noise. T1598 Spearphishing Voice supplied the fake technician. The Quick Assist session supplied hands-on-keyboard access, the MFA re-registration supplied persistence, and the inbox rule supplied cover. The final step — invoice redirection — is textbook Financial Theft (T1657) that failed only because one supplier verified out of band.

Detection point

The decisive detection was correlation, not any single event: per-mailbox inbound volume over 100 messages per hour, an "email broken" ticket for the same user, and a security-info registration within the surge window. Each signal alone has innocent explanations; together they do not. The SIEM rule from section 5.5 (TH-01) reproduces exactly this pairing.

Containment

  • Revoked all sessions and refresh tokens for the account; forced a password reset over a verified phone call.
  • Removed the attacker's MFA method and re-enrolled Dana's real device.
  • Terminated active Quick Assist sessions and blocked the caller's number and tenant.
  • Added the flood's top sender IPs and domains to the gateway block list.

Eradication

  • Deleted the malicious inbox rule and checked for others (none found).
  • Reviewed sign-in logs for the attacker's locations and devices; found no other accounts touched.
  • Verified no mail-flow or transport rules were changed tenant-wide.

Recovery

  • Restored normal mail flow by purging the flood from the inbox and raising monitoring for one week.
  • Re-verified all pending vendor payment details with every supplier through contract numbers.
  • Rebuilt Dana's MFA and re-issued trusted-device certificates.

Impact and consequences

No money was lost. Costs included two days of degraded mail service for the accounts-payable team, roughly a week of analyst time, and delayed supplier payments. The near-miss in reporting terms: the attacker came within one unverified phone call of a six-figure payment redirect.

Team response and communication

The service desk routed the "email broken" tickets to the SOC within an hour of the correlation alert — earlier they were treated as spam complaints. Messaging went out company-wide the next morning: "IT will never call you out of the blue about your mailbox; hang up and call the number on the intranet." Executives were briefed with the timeline above, which unlocked budget for the service-desk callback policy.

Lessons learned

  • Callback verification would have stopped the call chain. A 60-second callback to a known number defeats the fake technician entirely.
  • Monitor MFA changes after mail surges. The 24-hour pairing rule is cheap and catches the takeover moment.
  • Restrict and log remote-assist tools. Quick Assist was available to every user with no logging hook into the SIEM.
  • Put the ticket system into the SIEM. Tickets are early, human-readable evidence that a flood is landing.
  • Never trust contact details inside a suspicious message. The supplier's contract-number callback saved the payment.

Mitigation and defense

Controls that prevent the flood, blunt its effect, and cut the follow-up attack off — plus advice for home users and a prioritized action list.

Enterprise controls

Mail authentication: SPF, DKIM, DMARC

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are standards that prove a message really comes from your domain. Enforce them outbound so attackers cannot forge your staff's addresses for bounce storms, and watch inbound DMARC reports for abuse of your domain.

Bulk thresholds and quarantine

Set per-mailbox and tenant bulk-mail limits. Legitimate newsletters tolerate a spam-folder trip; a flood does not survive it. Tune EOP or gateway bulk scores (BCL) and quarantine high-bulk waves automatically.

Double opt-in on your own forms

Make every public signup form confirm by email before it sends anything more. You remove your own infrastructure as a weapon — both against others and against yourself.

Block direct-send where unused

Most tenants never need direct SMTP injection from the internet. Block or rate-limit it at the gateway so TH-03-style floods need nothing more exotic than your MX record.

Per-mailbox volume anomaly alerts

The core detection: alert when any mailbox — user or shared — exceeds its baseline inbound rate. Deploy the section 5.5 rules and include finance@, it@, hr@ in scope.

Identity change alerting

Alert on security-info registrations, MFA resets, and password changes — with extra weight when they happen within 24 hours of a mail surge for the same user (the TH-01 pairing).

Help-desk callback verification

Policy: any password or MFA reset requested by phone, chat, or a follow-up call must be confirmed by calling the employee back on the number in the HR system. This single control defeats the fake-technician step.

Restrict and log remote-assist tools

Allow Quick Assist or similar tools only for named support groups, log every session start to the SIEM, and require a ticket number to be quoted at connect time.

Ticket system in the SIEM

Ingest ServiceNow, Jira, or Zendesk tickets. "Inbox flooded" tickets are early evidence, and their absence from correlation is how attacks hide in plain sight.

User education with the right script

Teach one sentence: "IT will never call you unexpectedly about your mailbox — hang up and call the service desk yourself." Rehearse it in phishing-simulation briefings, including voice-based ones.

Home and small-office advice

  • Use an alias for signups. Give shops and newsletters a secondary address, and keep your main one for people you know. If the alias floods, delete it.
  • Turn on your provider's spam and block tools and learn where the quarantine lives, so you can find buried real mail fast.
  • Never trust a surprise "support" call. Real support teams do not cold-call about junk mail. Hang up and contact the provider yourself from their official app or website.
  • Report the flood to your provider (Microsoft, Google, or your mailbox host). They can throttle the wave and see bot infrastructure across other victims.
  • If alerts went missing during a flood, change your password and check your account's security and recovery settings from a device you trust.

Response runbook — the first 30 minutes

  1. 0–5 min: confirm the surge is real (message trace, one mailbox, many domains) and open a case. Do not close the user's mailbox.
  2. 5–10 min: query identity audit for security-info, password, rule, and session events in the window; page on any hit.
  3. 10–20 min: reach the user by callback to the HR number. If unreachable and identity events exist, treat as confirmed compromise and contain.
  4. 20–30 min: revoke sessions, reset credentials through the verified channel, remove rogue MFA and rules, and block the flood's top sources at the gateway.
Note — practice it once

Run this runbook as a 30-minute tabletop with the service desk once a quarter. Two failures are common the first time: the callback number list is out of date, and session revocation waits hours for approval. Fix both on a calm day, not during a flood.

Config examples you can copy

Adapt the two examples to your platform. The DMARC record starts in monitor mode (p=none) for two weeks; move to quarantine once reports show clean sources. The transport rule is written as pseudo-config on purpose — every gateway expresses it differently, and the idea is the point: internet hosts should not talk SMTP to your MX unless you allow it.

Common mistakes and best practices

Twelve real blue-team mistakes around email bombing, each with the symptom you will see, the root cause, the fix, and the prevention that keeps it fixed.

1 · Closing the flood as a spam ticket

Symptom

Tickets say "lots of junk mail" and are resolved as spam within minutes.

Root cause

The team sees volume, not intent; T1667 is not in the runbook.

Fix

Reopen tickets that mention floods and run the section 4 triage flow on them.

Prevention

Add T1667 to the triage matrix with a mandatory check for identity events in the surge window.

2 · No per-mailbox volume baseline

Symptom

Nobody can say whether 400 messages an hour is abnormal for that mailbox.

Root cause

Only tenant-wide mail statistics are kept; per-recipient history is missing.

Fix

Build a 30-day hourly baseline per mailbox from message trace.

Prevention

Keep the baseline in a lookup and alert at 5× baseline instead of fixed thresholds only.

3 · Message trace without identity audit

Symptom

Analysts can see the flood but cannot tell whether the account was taken over.

Root cause

The Entra ID / Unified Audit Log was never onboarded to the SIEM.

Fix

Onboard identity audit logs and backfill at least 30 days.

Prevention

Make identity audit ingestion a standing requirement for any mail-source onboarding.

4 · MFA changes are not alerted

Symptom

The attacker's authenticator was registered for hours before anyone noticed.

Root cause

"User registered security info" is treated as routine user activity.

Fix

Deploy the TH-01 rule and page on registrations without a matching ticket.

Prevention

Pair every MFA-change alert with the recent mail-surge context automatically.

5 · Auto-replies left on during a flood

Symptom

The victim's out-of-office reply fires thousands of times, feeding the storm.

Root cause

Nobody checks mailbox settings while containing the flood.

Fix

Turn off auto-replies for flooded mailboxes immediately.

Prevention

Make "disable auto-reply" a checklist item in the mail-flood runbook.

6 · Blocking the victim instead of investigating

Symptom

The response is to disable the noisy mailbox — hiding the evidence and the victim.

Root cause

Volume alerts are treated as a hygiene problem, not a security event.

Fix

Unblock the mailbox, quarantine flood senders, and investigate the user's identity events.

Prevention

Separate controls: throttle senders at the gateway, never silence the target.

7 · Bulk-deleting surge alerts to cut noise

Symptom

Analysts mass-close flood alerts; the one real MFA alert in the window is closed too.

Root cause

Noisy rules without context breed alert fatigue.

Fix

Re-open the window and review every buried security event for affected users.

Prevention

Correlate first (rule TH-01), and only then allow bulk closure of the pure-volume alerts.

8 · No callback verification policy

Symptom

The service desk resets MFA for anyone who sounds stressed and knows a name.

Root cause

Identity proofing relies on social plausibility, not verified channels.

Fix

Enforce callback to the HR-system number for every reset request that arrives by phone or chat.

Prevention

Drill the policy quarterly, including voice-based social-engineering tests.

9 · Remote-assist tools open to everyone

Symptom

Attackers guide any user into starting a remote session in minutes.

Root cause

Quick Assist or similar tools are unrestricted and unlogged.

Fix

Restrict sessions to named support groups and log session starts to the SIEM.

Prevention

Require a valid ticket number at connect time; alert on out-of-hours sessions.

10 · Ticket system missing from the SIEM

Symptom

Correlation rules cannot see that a flood victim called the service desk.

Root cause

The ticket system is managed as an IT tool, not a security log source.

Fix

Ingest tickets (TH-04 rule) and join them on user identity.

Prevention

Include the ticket system in the SIEM's source-coverage review.

11 · Chasing newsletter senders instead of the pattern

Symptom

Hours spent blocklisting domains while the bot keeps signing the victim up elsewhere.

Root cause

Reacting to the visible senders instead of the recipient-side mechanism.

Fix

Stop chasing single senders; switch to recipient-volume detection and report the infrastructure to providers.

Prevention

Runbook states explicitly: per-recipient controls first, sender blocklists second.

12 · Service mailboxes outside monitoring

Symptom

The first sign of trouble at finance@ is a vendor asking why their invoice bounced.

Root cause

Volume monitoring covers licensed user mailboxes only.

Fix

Add shared mailboxes to the TH-02 and TH-04 rules today.

Prevention

Maintain a monitored-mailbox inventory that includes every shared and service box.

Quick questions

Is email bombing just spam?
No. Spam is bulk mail sent to many people for profit. Bombing is aimed at one address with a goal: bury alerts, exhaust the mailbox, or set up the fake help-desk call.
Should we block all the newsletter senders?
You can try, but bots sign the victim up elsewhere within minutes. Alert on per-recipient volume instead, and report the signup infrastructure to the providers.
The flood stopped. Are we done?
Not yet. Check the surge window for buried security emails (password resets, MFA changes) and any security-info registration in the next 24 hours. The flood's job was to hide something.
What do we tell users?
"If your mailbox floods, we will never call you first. Hang up, and report it on the known support channel." Put that one sentence in training and on the help-desk site.
Where do I start with one hour a week?
Deploy the TH-02 per-recipient volume rule, deploy the TH-01 security-info pairing, and write the callback policy. Those three break the whole attack chain.
Does the victim need to do anything?
Yes: change the password from a trusted device, review the account security info and mailbox rules, and report which security emails they never saw. The buried-mail list matters as much as the flood itself.
Do other mail platforms need different rules?
The logic is identical; only the field names change. Google Workspace uses lowercase fields and often BigQuery export, and on-premises Exchange has its own message-tracking log — the section 5.5 notes show what to swap.
What proves intent, not just noise?
The sequence: flood, then a help-desk contact for the same user, then a security-info change. Any two of the three within a day is worth a case; all three is an incident.
Whose problem is this — mail team or security?
Both, on purpose. The mail team owns quotas and transport; security owns the pairing of the flood with identity changes. Write that split into the runbook so nobody assumes the other side is watching.
