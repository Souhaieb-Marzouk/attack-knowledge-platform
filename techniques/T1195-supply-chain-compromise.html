T1195 – Supply Chain Compromise

🎯Tactic: Initial Access 💻Platforms: Linux, SaaS, Windows, macOS 🧩Complexity: High 🔍Detection difficulty: High 📊Version: 1.7

T1195 – Supply Chain Compromise is an attack on trust. Instead of attacking the victim directly, the attacker poisons something the victim is waiting for: a software update, an open-source library, a build tool, or even a piece of hardware. The victim then installs the malware themselves, with full confidence, because it comes from a source they trust. ATT&CK splits this technique into three sub-techniques: compromise of software dependencies and development tools (T1195.001), compromise of the software supply chain (T1195.002), and compromise of the hardware supply chain (T1195.003). It is one of the hardest techniques to stop, because the delivery channel is designed to be trusted and usually updates itself. A real anchor: in 2020, attackers hid a backdoor (SUNBURST) inside signed SolarWinds Orion updates, and about 18,000 organizations downloaded it. For defenders, the key question changes from "is my network safe?" to "do I trust everything I install, and can I prove it?" This guide explains the attack, shows an animated simulation, and gives you detections, Sigma rules, and Splunk queries you can start building today.

Think like the attacker

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

3.1 When attackers use this

Supply chain compromise is a patient, high-value move. Attackers choose it when a direct attack is too hard, too noisy, or too small, and when they want to reach many victims through one door. ATT&CK defines T1195 as manipulating a product or its delivery path before the customer receives it, and the tactic is Initial Access: the poisoned update is the first step into the victim's network. It is common for attackers to enter a vendor many months before any victim feels the effect. In the SolarWinds case, attackers reached the build systems in September 2019, and customers received the trojanized update from March 2020. Attackers also use "second-order" compromise: they abuse the access from one supply chain hit to poison a second product. That is what happened with the 3CX desktop app in 2023, after the attackers had first compromised a dependency of its build environment.

Timing matters. Deployment usually happens in waves that look like normal releases, often during business hours, because the whole point is to look boring. The malicious code often waits days or weeks before it activates, which breaks the link between "install" and "attack" in the victim's memory.

3.2 How attackers do it, step by step

  1. Pick the delivery channel. The attacker studies vendors used by the target industry: an update server, a build system, an open-source package (npm, PyPI, NuGet), an RMM tool, or even hardware ordered from a distributor. Popularity wins — a package used by thousands of projects multiplies reach.
  2. Get a foothold inside. This can be stolen vendor credentials (T1078), an exploit of the vendor's edge device (T1190), a developer's laptop phished (T1566), a compromised CI/CD pipeline account, or a slowly earned maintainer position on an open-source project. The XZ Utils case shows how patient this can be: a trusted contributor spent roughly two years earning commit rights.
  3. Study the build and release process. The attacker maps how code becomes a signed installer: repositories, build agents, signing servers, release checklists. Weak spots are self-hosted build servers, service accounts without MFA (multi-factor sign-in), and signing done automatically on the same network.
  4. Inject the backdoor. The attacker adds malicious logic to the source code (T1195.001), to the built binary, or to the update package itself (T1195.002). Good implant design is quiet: sleep timers, low victim checks, and triggers that avoid sandboxes.
  5. Build and sign as usual. The poisoned product passes through the normal pipeline and gets the vendor's valid digital signature. The signature is the attack's shield — it makes the file look exactly like every other release.
  6. Distribute through the trusted channel. The auto-updater or app store pushes the update to all customers. Victims install it themselves. SolarWinds reached about 18,000 organizations this way; Kaseya VSA pushed ransomware through one MSP console to roughly 1,500 downstream businesses in July 2021.
  7. Sleep, then call home. The implant waits (SUNBURST slept about two weeks), then beacons to attacker infrastructure. SUNBURST used encoded subdomains of avsvmcloud.com so DNS traffic looked like a CDN (content delivery network) lookup. Phone-home traffic is rarely needed — some implants only activate on specific hosts.
  8. Select victims and follow up. Most infected machines are never used. The attacker looks at telemetry, picks high-value victims, and delivers a second stage (SUNBURST chose targets via a "task" that delivered TEARDROP, which loaded Cobalt Strike). Then they do hands-on intrusion: credential theft, lateral movement, data collection.

3.3 Where it works best

🏢Managed service providers

One RMM (remote monitoring and management) console such as Kaseya VSA, ScreenConnect, or AnyDesk manages hundreds of customers. Compromise the MSP, and you reach all of its clients — one break-in scales for both espionage and ransomware.

🧰Open-source ecosystems

npm, PyPI, and Maven packages are pulled automatically by builds. A poisoned dependency (T1195.001) or a typosquat (a fake package name that imitates a popular one) reaches every project that trusts it, including CI/CD (automated build and release) runners that hold secrets.

🏭Enterprises with auto-update trust

Organizations that install any signed vendor update everywhere, on day zero, are the ideal victim. Signed binaries often bypass both allowlists and casual SOC review.

🛰High-end espionage targets

Hardware implants (T1195.003) — factory-infected media or modified devices — are rare and expensive, but well documented against telecoms and governments, as the Elderwood reporting and the IBM/Schneider USB advisories show.

3.4 Why attackers like this technique

  • It defeats perimeter security completely. No exploit, no phishing email, no firewall rule change — the victim's own update mechanism delivers the payload inside.
  • Valid signatures lower suspicion. AV (antivirus) and EDR (endpoint detection and response tools) see a trusted publisher. Admins see a routine update. Alert fatigue (people stop reacting to routine alerts) does the rest.
  • Massive scale from one operation. A single poisoned release can touch tens of thousands of organizations, and the attacker can then pick only the best targets.
  • Time separation hides cause. Because the implant sleeps, the victim rarely connects the incident to the update they installed weeks earlier.
  • Hard attribution and cleanup. The vendor must recall the update, and every customer must respond — attacker infrastructure can rotate while the recall is still running.

3.5 What attackers need first

  • Access to a vendor, maintainer, build system, or distribution path — via stolen credentials, a phishing of a developer, an unpatched vendor appliance, or a fake maintainer identity.
  • Software engineering skills: how builds, signing, and package managers work; how to write code that evades detection and sandboxes.
  • Patient infrastructure: C2 (command-and-control) domains registered early (avsvmcloud.com was prepared long before), throwaway code-signing or CDN fronting, and patient hands-on operators.
  • Operational security discipline: small footprints, staged rollouts, and restraint — touching every victim at once would expose the access.
  • For hardware supply chain attacks: insider access at a factory or in shipping, which is why T1195.003 is mostly seen at nation-state level.

3.6 Tools and example commands (for authorized lab use)

Red teams and purple teams can simulate the defender's view of these attacks with standard supply chain security tools. These commands audit dependencies, build a software bill of materials (SBOM — a list of every component in your software), and verify signatures. Run them only on systems you own.

dependency_audit.sh — find risky open-source components
# Audit Python requirements for known-vulnerable or deprecated packages
pip-audit --requirement requirements.txt --strict

osv-scanner --lockfile=package-lock.json

# Generate an SBOM so you can prove what shipped in release 1.4.2
syft packages dir:./app -o json > sbom-1.4.2.json

# Verify a Sigstore/cosign signature before deploying an internal image

A purple-team exercise can also drop a "canary update": a test release with an inert beacon, deployed only to a lab ring, to prove your DNS and egress monitoring would catch a real poisoned update. Do not tamper with third-party code, repos, or signing keys outside your own environment — that is illegal and dangerous.

Ethics and legality

Everything in this section is for education and authorized testing only. Tampering with software, build systems, or update channels that you do not own — or intercepting deliveries — breaks computer misuse laws in almost every country and can put real users at risk. Practice only inside your own lab, with written permission for any customer system, and always with code that can be safely switched off.

Think like the defender

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

4.1 What defenders can see

A supply chain attack is quiet at the delivery moment, but it is not invisible. The implant still has to run, stay alive, and talk. Each of those needs logs, files, or network traffic, and each leaves traces. The three best watch points are: (1) the update/install event itself — who spawned what, was a file replaced or re-signed; (2) first-run behavior — new child processes, unsigned modules loading, services registering; and (3) first egress (first outbound connection) — DNS queries to brand-new domains, encoded subdomains, or connections to hosting providers the vendor never used before.

4.3 Where to look

  • Endpoint telemetry — Sysmon (EIDs 1, 3, 6, 7, 11, 13, 22), EDR process trees, and Windows Security events 4688 / 7045 / 6416.
  • Network telemetry — Zeek/NetFlow, DNS server logs, proxy logs; watch for new domains, encoded subdomains, and odd protocols from quiet applications.
  • Build & release logs — CI/CD audit trails, artifact repositories, code-signing service logs; compare release hashes over time.
  • Package/registry data — internal mirrors (Artifactory, Nexus), lockfiles, SBOMs, and vulnerability scans of dependencies.
  • Threat intel — vendor security advisories, CISA alerts, ISAC notices, and IOC feeds for known poisoned releases (hashes, domains, certs).

4.4 Why this matters to the SOC

The blast radius — how many systems one incident can hurt — is the key difference from most techniques. A normal initial-access incident is one user; a supply chain incident can be every endpoint that installed the update, including servers that EDR treats as fully trusted. Trust is also the problem for tooling: many SOCs whitelist signed vendor software, so the implant runs without an alert. Business impact can be severe and wide: SolarWinds led to intrusions into US government agencies and major companies; the Kaseya VSA attack (REvil, July 2021) reached about 1,500 businesses through a single MSP and interrupted operations worldwide; NotPetya, delivered through the M.E.Doc update mechanism in 2017, caused over 10 billion USD in global damage. When a poisoned update is confirmed, the SOC's job shifts fast: identify every host that received it, contain them, recall the software, and hunt for the second stage the attackers may already have delivered.

4.5 Where it fits in the attack chain

Before: the attacker usually compromises the vendor with T1190 (Exploit Public-Facing Application), T1078 (Valid Accounts), or phishing of a developer (T1566); they may also have gained CI/CD access via T1078.4 cloud accounts. During: T1195 has three sub-techniques — T1195.001 (dependencies and dev tools), T1195.002 (software supply chain, e.g. trojanized updates), T1195.003 (hardware supply chain). After: execution via T1059 (Command and Scripting Interpreter) or T1129 (Shared Modules), persistence via T1543 (Create or Modify System Process) or T1053 (Scheduled Task), C2 via T1071.004 (DNS) or T1568 (Dynamic Resolution), then credential access, lateral movement (T1021), collection, and exfiltration (T1041). Treat T1195 as the door — the real damage usually happens two or three tactics later.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

Where threat intel comes from

📰Open sources (OSINT)

Vendor security blogs and advisories, CISA alerts (AA20-304A SolarWinds, AA21-131A Kaseya), Microsoft and Mandiant reports, abuse.ch URLhaus/ThreatFox, AlienVault OTX, Shodan (for exposed build/RMM systems), and VirusTotal (hashes of poisoned installers).

🎯ATT&CK-based intel

MITRE lists these users of T1195: Ember Bear (G1003), OilRig (G0049), and Sandworm Team (G0034) on the group side, plus software delivered by supply chain means such as Lumma Stealer (S1213) and Raccoon Stealer (S1148). Their ATT&CK pages link the primary reporting.

💳Commercial feeds

Vendor threat intel platforms add actor tracking, finished reports, and ready-made IOC sets. Useful for small teams that cannot do full research, but always test the IOCs before pushing them into detection.

🏠Internal telemetry

Your own data is the best feed: software inventory, SBOMs, update history, DNS logs, and package registry logs. A "known-good baseline" of every installed release lets you spot the one release nobody can explain.

The intel cycle for this technique

  1. Define priorities. Ask: which vendors are in our supply chain tier 1? Which packages do our builds pull? What update channels run with admin rights?
  2. Collect. Gather vendor advisories, CISA/ISAC alerts, actor pages (G1003, G0049, G0034), IOC feeds, and your internal install/telemetry baselines.
  3. Process. Normalize the data: map IOCs to your software inventory, tag which hosts run which vendor products and versions.
  4. Analyze. Decide relevance: a poisoned update in a vendor you do not use is context; one you installed last week is an incident. Judge reachability, not just headlines.
  5. Produce. Turn findings into hunts and detections: hash blocklists, DNS rules (like Sigma #2), Sigma rules for the release process, and a vendor recall playbook.
  6. Feedback. Track which intel produced true positives, retire stale IOCs, and record vendor incidents in the risk register for the next assessment cycle.

How to apply intel here

  • Pivot from reports to your estate: when a supply chain story breaks, immediately map it to your software inventory, SBOMs, and update logs — hours matter (SolarWinds customers had ~9 months of exposure).
  • Watch infrastructure, not just files: hunt for the attacker's C2 patterns (encoded DNS subdomains, new CDN-fronted domains) — hashes change, infrastructure habits repeat.
  • Join the feeds that fit your sector: ISAC memberships and CISA advisories are how most victims first learn a vendor is poisoned.

Threat #5 — Malicious or stolen code signing

Attackers do not always need to break a signature — they steal one. With a leaked signing certificate (or a rogue intermediate), malware is signed by a publisher your fleet already trusts. Sysmon logs driver (EID 6) and image (EID 7) loads with signer data, so you can catch a trusted name signing from the wrong path. 5.5 Sigma Rules & Splunk SPL

Each rule below pairs with the log pattern of the same number in 5.4. Sigma rules are written for standard pipelines (Sysmon via XmlWinEventLog, Zeek, Fortinet, Squid, AWS CloudTrail, Windows Security). Tune thresholds and allowlists for your environment before deploying.

Sigma Rule #1 — Vendor application host spawns shell (matches Threat #1)
sigma — vendor_app_host_spawns_shell.yml
title: Vendor Application Host Spawning Command Shell

status: stable
description: |

date: 2025-08-14
tags:
    - attack.initial_access
    - attack.t1195

    - attack.t1059

    product: windows

spl — vendor app host spawns shell
index=ep_sysmon sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational EventCode=1

Learn from a real incident

Real-World Case Study

The SolarWinds SUNBURST supply chain attack (2019–2021)

In December 2020, security firm FireEye disclosed that attackers had stolen its red team tools — but the deeper story was how the attackers first got in. The entry point was SolarWinds Orion, a widely used network monitoring product. Attackers later tracked as APT29 / Cozy Bear / Nobelium / UNC2452 (Russian-linked intelligence, per US government attribution; MITRE records the incident as campaign C0024) compromised SolarWinds' build environment and inserted a backdoor (called SUNBURST or Solorigate) into Orion Platform builds released between March and June 2020. The updates carried a valid SolarWinds code signature, so roughly 18,000 customers installed them as routine maintenance. The backdoor slept about two weeks, then used encoded DNS queries to avsvmcloud.com to check in. Only a select set of victims — around 100, including at least nine US federal agencies — received the next stage (TEARDROP/RAINDROP loaders feeding Cobalt Strike) for hands-on espionage. The case became the defining supply chain attack of the decade and led to US Executive Order 14028 and a global push for SBOMs and build-system security.

How the attack chain worked

  1. Vendor foothold: the attackers reached SolarWinds' internal systems (method never fully published) and learned the build and release process.
  2. Build injection (T1195.002): malicious code entered the product through the build process, not the source repository, defeating source-vs-binary integrity checks.
  3. Trusted delivery: digitally signed Orion updates were pushed through the normal update channel to ~18,000 organizations.
  4. Dormancy and beaconing: after ~2 weeks the implant woke, used DNS-based C2 via avsvmcloud.com, and blended into allowed CDN-like traffic.
  5. Selection and second stage: chosen victims received TEARDROP/RAINDROP, which loaded Cobalt Strike entirely in memory.
  6. Hands-on objectives: credential theft, lateral movement, email and document collection at agencies and Fortune 500 companies.

How it was detected

The break came from one unusual authentication: FireEye's SOC saw a VPN login that used a second MFA device the attacker had registered themselves, coming from a system running SolarWinds Orion. That single anomaly — not an antivirus hit, not a signature — unwound the entire campaign. Corroborating signals existed months earlier at other victims: DNS queries to avsvmcloud.com with machine-generated subdomains (exactly the pattern of Sigma #2 in this guide), and Orion processes spawning shells (Sigma #1). After disclosure, the sinkhole data identified victims within days.

Containment

  1. Disconnect or isolate all Orion Platform installations; block communication to avsvmcloud.com and related C2 at DNS and firewall level.
  2. Pivot from the sinkhole lists and update logs: find every host that received builds 2019.4 HF5–2020.2.1.
  3. Hunt for the second stage on selected hosts: TEARDROP artifacts, Cobalt Strike beacons, and new MFA device registrations in identity logs.
  4. Reset credentials for accounts the operators touched; revoke and re-issue VPN and service-account trust.
  5. Preserve forensic evidence (Orion installers, DNS logs, identity logs) before cleanup, for attribution and regulator reporting.

Recovery

  1. Apply SolarWinds hotfixes and move to clean builds released from the rebuilt, externally reviewed pipeline.
  2. Rebuild affected identity infrastructure: password resets, MFA re-enrollment, token and certificate rotation.
  3. Recover email and data loss scope with legal and compliance (several agencies had months of mailbox access to assess).
  4. Adopt the structural fixes: staged update rings, SBOM tracking, and vendor build-security attestations (EO 14028 direction).

Lessons learned

  • A valid digital signature proves the vendor signed the file — not that the vendor's build was clean.
  • One anomalous login detected and escalated well beats ten signatures that never fire; fund the detection basics.
  • Know your software inventory and SBOMs before the crisis, so "who installed Orion build X?" takes minutes, not weeks.
  • DNS telemetry was decisive: encoded-subdomain patterns are catchable (see Sigma #2) and were visible for months.
  • Victim selection means most infected hosts are never touched — containment is still mandatory for all of them.
  • Build systems are top-value production assets: isolate them, log them, and audit changes like domain controllers.
  • Staged update rings (canary first) would have cut 18,000 victims to hundreds.
Why this case defines T1195

SUNBURST is the purest example of T1195.002: the attacker never attacked the victims directly — the victims installed the attack themselves, from a vendor they trusted, with a signature they checked. Every defense in this guide (rings, SBOMs, DNS analytics, build isolation) traces back to a lesson from this incident.

Layered defenses that work

Mitigation & Defense

Defense in depth

You cannot audit every byte of every product you install — so layer your defenses: harden who can build software, slow down how fast updates land, and watch what updates do after they run. ATT&CK lists six official mitigations for T1195: M1013 (Application Developer Guidance), M1046 (Boot Integrity), M1033 (Limit Software Installation), M1051 (Update Software), M1018 (User Account Management), and M1016 (Vulnerability Scanning).

Enterprise defenses

📋Policy & governance

Keep a vendor inventory with update-channel owners, run supplier risk reviews (ISO 27001 A.5.19–A.5.23, NIST SP 800-161), and require SBOMs for critical software so you can answer "do we run product X, build Y?" in minutes.

🔐Access control on build systems

Treat CI/CD like a domain controller: MFA everywhere, PAM on service accounts, phishing-resistant sign-in for developers, and short-lived tokens (M1018). Most supply chain hits start with a stolen pipeline credential.

🏗Build & release integrity

Isolate build networks, log every build, pin dependency versions to internal mirrors (M1033, M1013), and use reproducible builds plus signed provenance (Sigstore/cosign) so artifact hashes can be compared release over release.

🔄Update controls

Verify vendor updates before broad rollout — staged rings with a canary group, 72-hour hold for non-urgent patches (M1051). Fast-track only critical security fixes with extra monitoring on the first ring.

🌐Network controls

Egress filtering and DNS monitoring catch what signatures miss: block new domains by default on server segments, alert on encoded-subdomain patterns (Sigma #2), and baseline every application's destinations (Sigma #9).

🖥Endpoint controls

Deploy Sysmon/EDR process-tree telemetry (Sigma #1, #6), enforce driver blocklists, and alert on signed binaries from user-writable paths (Sigma #5). Avoid blanket "trust any signed file" allowlists.

🧪Monitoring & testing

Continuously scan dependencies (M1016: pip-audit, osv-scanner), test the Sigma rules in 5.5 with purple-team exercises, and run canary-update drills to prove the DNS and update telemetry actually flows to the SOC.

🚨Response readiness

Write the poisoned-update playbook now: who recalls software, who queries install logs, who notifies regulators (GDPR Art. 33 / NIS2). Include boot-integrity checks (M1046) for hardware suspicion and vendor recall steps.

Home user defenses

  • Turn on automatic updates for your operating system and apps — for home users, the speed benefit beats the (rare) poisoned-update risk.
  • Download software only from official stores or the vendor's own website; never "crack" or keygen sites (they deliver Lumma/Raccoon today).
  • Keep antivirus/antimalware protection enabled and let it scan everything, even "known" installers.
  • Use a password manager and MFA on important accounts, so one stolen password is not a disaster.
  • Back up important files to a place that is always offline or versioned — ransomware like Kaseya's reaches many devices at once.
  • Remove apps you no longer use; every installed product is an update channel you must trust.
  • Check router and IoT firmware updates too — those are supply chain products as well.
  • If a program you did not install asks to run right after an update, pause and ask an expert before clicking.

Best practices for day-to-day SOC work

  1. Baseline before you hunt. Keep a known-good record of installed software versions, update history, and per-application egress. Every hunt in this guide needs that baseline to say "anomalous."
  2. Run hypothesis hunts monthly. Example: "any vendor-signed process spawning a shell in the last 30 days?" (SPL #1) and "any parent domain resolved by more than five hosts with generated subdomains?" (SPL #2).
  3. Purple-test the Sigma rules in 5.5. Use a lab host with an inert canary update to prove telemetry reaches the SIEM and the rules fire. Fix the pipeline before the real event.
  4. Keep a vendor incident watchlist. Subscribe to advisories for your tier-1 vendors; rehearse "day one" pivots: who queries install logs, who isolates, who calls the vendor.
  5. Feed metrics back. Track detection coverage per ATT&CK data source and MTTD for canary tests; report the supply chain rules' hit rates, not just alert counts.
  6. Document decisions. When you allowlist a false positive, record who approved it, why, and when to re-review — stale allowlists are how implants hide for years.
  7. Close the loop with risk. Turn every confirmed gap (missing logs, unmonitored vendor) into a tracked backlog item linked to the regulation table in 5.1.
Keep going

You will not stop every poisoned update — no one can — but layered defenses shrink the blast radius dramatically. Watch what software does after it runs, slow down delivery with rings, and keep your inventory ready. The next SolarWinds will be found by the team whose basics were in place; with the rules in this guide, that team can be yours.
