T1590.003

Network Trust Dependencies

Companies do not work alone. They trust vendors, partners, and cloud services, and they give those groups some access. Attackers map exactly this trust, then attack the weakest company on the map. This guide shows the method, a real case, and the detections that catch the moment the trust is used.

01

Overview: What Is This Technique?

MITRE ATT&CK is a free, public catalog of attacker behavior. It sorts attacker actions into tactics and techniques. A tactic is the goal, for example Reconnaissance. A technique is the method used to reach the goal, and each one has an ID. Security teams use these IDs as a shared language. A report can say the attacker used T1590.003, and every team knows the exact step.

This page covers T1590.003, Network Trust Dependencies, a sub-technique of T1590 (Gather Victim Network Information). Its official description says this: attackers may gather information about the victim's network trust dependencies that can be used during targeting. That information includes details about second- and third-party organizations and domains. Examples are managed service providers and contractors. These groups often have connected network access, and sometimes that access has elevated rights, which means more power than a normal user. In simple words: the attacker draws a map of who the victim trusts. The attacker wants to find the weakest trusted company, because one vendor account can open the victim's door. Attackers collect this information through phishing for information (T1598) or through open data sets. The result feeds more reconnaissance, helps the attacker collect operational resources, and often leads to initial access, for example through the Trusted Relationship technique (T1199).

In this guide you will learn what the attacker does, step by step, in an animated simulation. You will see the red team view and the blue team view of the same activity. You will get a full detection deep dive. It holds mapped regulations, threat intel, a ranked threat list, log patterns, five Sigma rules, and matching SPL. You will also study a real breach, the SolarWinds trust attack, and finish with layered defenses, common mistakes, and a glossary.

Why should you care?

  • Large breaches often arrive through a trusted vendor, not through the front door. The SolarWinds 2020 case reached thousands of organizations this way.
  • Vendor information is public by design. Job posts, partner pages, and website scripts tell an attacker what your company depends on.
  • One vendor account can reach many customers at once. This makes vendors a high-value target for ransomware and espionage groups.
  • SOC teams often trust vendor traffic and stop looking closely at it. Attackers know this and hide inside that trust.
  • Detection engineers can turn this into strong, low-noise alerts: watch vendor accounts, vendor IP ranges, and update channels.
How to read this guideWatch the simulation first. It shows the whole story in a few minutes. Then read the red team view and the blue team view to understand both sides. The final sections give a real case, defenses, mistakes, and a glossary for every technical word.

03

Red Team View: How Attackers Do It

Attackers rarely start with the victim. They start with the victim's vendors, because public pages and job posts describe those vendors in detail. This section explains the method so defenders can run the same exercise first. It is for learning and defense only. Test systems you own or have written permission to test, and never use stolen data.

When attackers use this

  • Early, for planning. This is Reconnaissance work. It happens before any intrusion. The trust map shapes the whole operation: which portals exist, which update channels are trusted, and which door looks quietest.
  • When a direct attack is hard. The victim has patched systems, good monitoring, and a trained SOC. The small vendor next door may have none of these, so the attacker goes around the strong wall.
  • When planning a supply chain attack. If the goal is many victims at once, one vendor or one managed service provider reaches them all through existing connections (T1195.002).
  • Later, for staging. After landing inside a vendor network, attackers keep studying the victim from a position of trust.

Where the research happens

  • From their own infrastructure. A laptop, a VPN exit, or a cheap cloud host. All queries go to public pages, so nothing touches the victim's logs.
  • Public sources only, at first. Search engines, job boards, LinkedIn, press releases, partner pages, case studies, and the victim's own website code.
  • Inside the vendor, later. Once a vendor foothold exists, the attacker reads the vendor's documentation, support tickets, and customer lists to learn exactly how the trust path works.

How the collection works, step by step

The method is patient reading. The attacker collects vendor names first. They search the victim's name next to words like partner, supplier, vendor, and case study. Job posts are the richest source, because one line like experience with VendorVPN names a product in use. Next comes tech-stack fingerprinting. A tool such as BuiltWith lists the third-party scripts a website loads. Then the attacker looks for portals: VPN logins, support portals, and update servers, often found in public documents or DNS records. Finally, the attacker ranks the trust links by power. A tunnel, a shared account, or a trusted update channel is worth more than a plain website link.

Why does this work? Trust bypasses perimeter controls. Traffic from a vendor is expected, so firewalls and VPN concentrators allow it. One vendor often reaches many victims, which multiplies the value of a single break-in. What is needed to start? Almost nothing: public information and patience. A quiet collection phase can take weeks and leaves no trace in the victim's own logs.

The output above is fake, but the idea is real. Each matched line names a third party. The chat widget is one trust link, the help portal is another, and the comment names a remote-access product with a version number. A defender who runs the same two commands against their own website finds the exact list that the vendor inventory in section 07 should contain.

Ethics and the lawNever buy, sell, trade, or use stolen data. Logging in with leaked credentials is a crime in most countries, even as a joke. If a legal monitoring service reports that a vendor's staff emails appear in a public leak, the correct response is to force a password reset and add MFA, nothing more. Only test what you own or have written permission to test. This guide supports learning and defense only.

A simple repeatable step list

  1. Collect the victim's public pages, press releases, and partner lists.
  2. Extract every vendor, product, and SaaS name from them.
  3. Repeat the same collection for each vendor found, one level deep.
  4. Look for remote-access products, portal URLs, and update endpoints in job posts and documents.
  5. Rank the trust links: who has a tunnel, a shared account, or trusted code?
  6. Defenders: run the same six steps against your own company and fix what you find first.
04

Blue Team View: How Defenders See It

From inside the victim, this technique is almost invisible. The collection happens on the public internet and inside other companies, so no victim log records it. Defenders see results only later, when the attacker starts to use the trust path. Your job is to make that moment loud.

Why the quiet phase stays invisible

While the attacker reads job posts and fingerprints websites, nothing touches your network. Your firewalls, VPN gateways, and identity provider have nothing to log, because the attacker is browsing public pages and other companies' sites. This is why PRE-stage techniques produce no alerts by themselves. The realistic goal is not to detect the reading. It is to detect the use of what was read.

The use begins when a vendor account logs in from a place it never logged in from before. That event is fully inside your visibility, and it is the moment this guide's detections target.

Where traces appear when the trust path is used

  • VPN and firewall auth logs for vendor accounts. Logins at odd hours, from new IP addresses, or directly after failed attempts.
  • Egress and DNS logs. Servers calling remote-management tool domains or vendor update endpoints they never called before, often on a fixed schedule.
  • Cloud SSO logs for third-party users. New devices, new countries, or new MFA factors for vendor identities in your identity provider.
  • Windows logon events. Type 3 (network) logons for external or guest accounts at unusual volume or unusual hours.

Why early visibility matters

A trust attack moves fast after the quiet phase. The vendor login is hours away from lateral movement. The update beacon is days away from credential theft. If your first alert fires at the data theft stage, you are weeks late. If it fires at the first vendor login or the first strange beacon, you can stop the attack one company away from your crown jewels. Early visibility also gives you clean evidence for the vendor conversation: which account, which tunnel, which server, and when.

How this fits the attack chain

T1590.003 is preparation. It sits in the Reconnaissance tactic, before Initial Access. The information it collects feeds later techniques. T1199, Trusted Relationship, is the abuse of an existing trust link to reach the victim. T1195, Supply Chain Compromise, is the attack through vendor software, updates, or hardware that the victim installs. T1195.002 is its software supply chain form. If your detections cover T1199 and T1195 but not the vendor account activity that precedes them, you are watching the door but not the key.

What MITRE says about detection (official note)MITRE notes that much of this activity happens outside the visibility of the target organization. It has a high occurrence rate and a high false positive rate, which makes detection difficult. MITRE recommends focusing detection efforts on related lifecycle stages, such as Initial Access. In practice: accept that the reading phase is invisible, and build strong alerts for the first use of the trust path.
Defender mindset: your vendor's password is your keyA vendor login is a door in your wall that you did not build and may not watch. Inventory every vendor with remote access or privileged data. Lock each login behind MFA. Shrink each account with least privilege and time limits. Log every use. When a vendor tells you they were breached, your first questions should be: which accounts, which tunnels, and when were they last used?

Detection Engineering Deep Dive

This section is the working reference for detection engineers and SOC analysts. Start with regulation mapping to see which controls already ask for vendor monitoring. Then review threat intelligence sources, the ranked threat list, and the log patterns that reveal a used trust path. Finish with five complete Sigma rules and a Splunk SPL quick reference you can adapt to your own indexes. One honest warning first. MITRE notes that this activity mostly happens outside the victim's visibility. Occurrence and false positive rates are high, so detection is difficult. The rules below therefore watch the trust path being used, not the reading phase.

5.1Regulation Mapping

Vendor trust is not only an attacker problem; it is also an audit problem. Most security frameworks already ask you to know your suppliers, to control their access, and to monitor it. The table below lists the controls that map directly to T1590.003, so you can reuse this work for compliance and detection at the same time.

Read the table in two passes. First pass: find the frameworks that apply to you, and note the exact control numbers. Second pass: for each control, write down the log source and the rule from this guide that proves it. The control numbers matter in audits, but the proof is always a log and a working alert.

Ownership also matters. The vendor inventory belongs to procurement and the vendor manager. The MFA and segmentation controls belong to IT. The monitoring controls belong to the SOC. A mapping that does not name owners usually stays on paper, so add a name to every row before you present it.

5.2Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence tells you which vendors are under attack right now, and which trust paths groups prefer. Good sources for this technique are easy to reach, and most of them are free. The table below shows what each source gives you and how to use it.

For this technique, intelligence has one main job: it tells you which trust paths attackers like right now. A vendor story in the news is not just news. It is a warning that the trust path between you and that vendor is being tested somewhere else today. Teams that connect the two move faster than teams that treat news as background noise.

A simple intel workflow

Collect:

  1. List every vendor with remote access, data access, or code access to your environment.
  2. Subscribe to the security bulletins of each critical vendor.
  3. Watch leak-site and stealer-log sources through a legal commercial service, scoped to your vendor domains.

Analyze:

  1. Map each incoming event to ATT&CK techniques, starting with T1590.003, T1199, and T1195.
  2. Match the event to your vendor inventory and rank the affected trust paths.
  3. Decide if the event changes your risk picture or only your watchlist.

Apply:

  1. Tune the Sigma rules in section 5.5 with the new indicators.
  2. Run the vendor review steps in section 07 for the affected vendor.
  3. Report the change to leadership in one page: what happened, what you changed, what you watch now.

Keep the workflow small and repeatable. A weekly 30-minute review of new vendor stories beats a large report that nobody reads. One person collects, one person maps, and the whole team sees the result. When a story matches a critical vendor from your inventory, that is your trigger to check the logs from section 5.4 for the same pattern.

5.3Threats: Ranking and Categorization

Not every trust link is equally dangerous. The ranking below orders the most common trust-based threats, with the worst first. Severity means how bad it is for the victim if the recon succeeds and the threat plays out. Use the list to decide which vendor accounts get MFA, segmentation, and monitoring first. The top five threats map one to one to the Sigma rules in section 5.5.

Severity here means harm to the victim, not skill of the attacker. A critical row can stop the business or expose its most sensitive data. A high row causes serious damage but is easier to contain. A medium or low row is still worth fixing, because cheap doors get used first. The platform chips show where the damage appears, so they also tell you which team owns the fix.

5.4Logs and Patterns

The attacker's quiet reading phase leaves no logs on your side. Everything below is about the moment the trust path is used. Each example uses fake, safe values from the reserved documentation ranges. The victim network is 192.0.2.0/24. The approved vendor egress pool is 203.0.113.128/27 (addresses 203.0.113.128 to 203.0.113.159). Attacker systems sit at 203.0.113.50 and inside 198.51.100.0/24. Learn the shape of each line, then tune the pattern for your own estate.

Each pattern below names the log source that holds the evidence. In most estates, the VPN auth log lives on the firewall or concentrator. Sysmon events arrive through your endpoint agent pipeline. Cloud sign-in logs come from your identity platform. Zeek logs come from your network sensor. Send all of them to one search platform, because the story only makes sense when the logs sit side by side. Keep at least 90 days of these logs online, and archive the rest, because trust abuse is often discovered late.

5.5Sigma Rules (Detection as Code)

The five rules below are complete and valid Sigma. Each rule catches one of the top five threats from section 5.3. Open a rule to see the YAML, then use the matching Splunk SPL right after it as a translation shortcut. Replace the approved vendor IP lists with your real ones, and see section 5.6 for index naming notes.

To deploy a rule, copy the YAML into your Sigma tooling. Convert it to your search platform's language, or paste the SPL version straight into Splunk. Start every rule in silent mode. Two weeks of silence with real vendor traffic tells you more than a month of guessing. When the noise list is stable, give the rule an owner and a response step. An alert without an owner is just a log line.

R1 · Vendor Login from Unlisted Source IP high

This rule catches a vendor or service account logging in to the VPN from an IP outside the approved vendor pool. Level: high. Main false positive: a vendor engineer traveling from a new location.

Sigma YAML · R1
title: Vendor or Service VPN Login from Unlisted Source IP
id: 7c2f1a9e-4b8d-4e63-9a51-c0f3d8b2e101
status: stable
description: Detects a vendor or service account logging in to the VPN or firewall from a source IP outside the approved vendor egress pool. This is the classic sign that a trust dependency credential was stolen and is being used.
references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1590/003/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026/09/29
logsource:
    category: network
    product: pfsense
    service: openvpn
detection:
    selection:
        action: 'login'
        result: 'success'
        user|startswith:
            - 'vendor-'
            - 'svc-'
    filter_approved:
        src_ip|startswith:
            - '203.0.113.12'
            - '203.0.113.13'
            - '203.0.113.14'
            - '203.0.113.15'
    condition: selection and not filter_approved
falsepositives:
    - A vendor engineer traveling and connecting from a new location
    - A new vendor egress IP that is not on the approved pool list yet
level: high
tags:
    - attack.reconnaissance
    - attack.t1590.003
Splunk SPL - same logic
index=fw sourcetype=pfsense:openvpn action=login result=success (user=vendor-* OR user=svc-*)

R4 · Repeated Calls to a Vendor Update Endpoint high

This rule catches many HTTP calls to a vendor update path from one server in a short window, which is the shape of a beacon. Level: high. Main false positive: a server that legitimately proxies updates for its whole subnet.

Sigma YAML · R4
title: Repeated Calls to a Vendor Update Endpoint from an Unexpected Server
id: 9b3e6a12-47f5-4d0c-8e29-1c6b0d8a4104
status: stable
description: Detects many HTTP calls to a vendor update path from one source in a short window. A trusted update channel that repeats on a fixed schedule can hide a beacon from a trojanized update.
references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1590/003/
author: Detection Engineering Team

logsource:

detection:

        host: 'update.vendorco.example'
        uri|startswith: '/update/'

Real-World Scenario: One vendor, thousands of doors — the SolarWinds trust

This scenario follows CISA advisory AA20-352A (December 2020) on the SolarWinds compromise. The advisory title is Advanced Persistent Threat Compromise of Government Agencies, Critical Infrastructure Entities, and Private Sector Organizations. Details are simplified for learning, and no real indicator values are included.

Background

SolarWinds made a network management product called Orion. Thousands of organizations used it, including government agencies and large companies. The attackers studied a trust dependency first. Customers trusted Orion updates. The updates were signed by the vendor and delivered through the vendor's own channel. The attackers reached the vendor's build system and placed a backdoor inside the Orion code while it was being built. The result was a trojanized software update, which is the software supply chain technique T1195.002. Public figures from that period say about 18,000 organizations received the trojanized update. The attackers then picked a small group, roughly one hundred organizations, and followed them with more access attempts.

Attack chain timeline

Months before spring 2020

Mapping the trust

The attackers study the vendor and its customers. They learn who ships the updates, who signs them, and why customers install them without question. This is the trust dependency reconnaissance of T1590.003.

Before spring 2020

Reaching the vendor build system

The attackers gain access to the vendor's build environment. From there they can touch the code that every customer will later install. The victim networks are still untouched.

March to June 2020

The trojanized update ships

The attackers add a backdoor during the build. The finished updates carry a valid vendor signature, so they pass normal checks. Trust in the update channel does the rest.

Spring 2020 onward

Customers install the trusted update

Thousands of organizations install the update through their normal patch process. About 18,000 receive the backdoor. For most of them, nothing visible happens, which is the point.

About two weeks after install

The backdoor wakes up

The implanted code waits, then calls out in small steps that look like updater traffic. The calls carry hidden data in DNS names and HTTP calls, so the traffic blends with normal use.

Summer to late 2020

Selective follow-up

The attackers choose a small group of interesting victims and send them a second stage. They steal credentials and reach cloud services and email. The other victims stay dormant.

Mid-December 2020

Found through its own steps

A security team investigating an unusual event in their environment traces suspicious activity back to the vendor update. The finding becomes public on December 13, 2020, and CISA publishes advisory AA20-352A days later.

December 2020 into 2021

Containment and rebuild

Organizations block the vendor channel, remove the affected software, reset credentials, and audit their environments. CISA's advisory lists the response steps that agencies must follow.

2021

The lesson spreads

Update channels stop being invisible infrastructure. Vendors publish build-system evidence, and buyers start asking vendors hard questions about their pipelines. One year later, attackers abused the trust around a managed service tool called Kaseya VSA. They pushed ransomware to hundreds of small businesses at once. The trust pattern was the same (CISA Alert, July 2021).

How it was detected

The trail started with the attack's own noise. A security team noticed an unexpected device registration in their identity logs, tied to VPN access. Other defenders saw the beacon shape in their egress and DNS logs. The shape was repeated, fixed-interval calls to new infrastructure. The calling machines had just processed an update. Neither signal was loud alone. Analysts found the truth by connecting an identity anomaly with network beacons and asking what had changed on the affected hosts. The lesson for a SOC: a quiet beacon is still a beacon, and an identity change nobody requested is always worth one question.

Containment steps

  • Block the vendor update channel at the firewall and proxy, so no more trojanized content arrives.
  • Isolate the servers that ran the affected software until they are checked.
  • Reset credentials for accounts the affected systems could reach, starting with privileged and service accounts.
  • Revoke and reissue tokens and certificates held on affected hosts.

Recovery steps

  • Remove or rebuild the affected software from known good sources, following the advisory guidance.
  • Rebuild compromised hosts instead of cleaning them, where the evidence says attackers ran code.
  • Re-secure the trust path: new credentials, tighter vendor firewall rules, and updated allowlists.
  • Keep enhanced logging for the vendor channel until the environment is stable again.

Impact and consequences

About 18,000 organizations had to check whether they were affected, and the response work ran for months. Agencies followed CISA's directive steps, and security teams worldwide audited update channels. The business impact reached far past the direct victims. Trust in signed software updates took a lasting hit. Supply chain security moved to the top of leadership agendas. For the small group of followed targets, the cost included stolen data, displaced staff, and long cleanup programs.

Lessons learned

  • Treat update channels as critical infrastructure. A signed update is a delivery truck with a master key. Watch it, log it, and limit it.
  • Keep a vendor allowlist for egress. Servers should call only the update endpoints they are approved to call, on schedules you know.
  • Monitor egress for fixed-interval beacons. The same call, the same path, the same rhythm: this shape catches trust abuse early.
  • Segment around vendor tooling. A management tool or update agent should never hold wide rights to the whole network.
  • Trust the signature, but verify the behavior. A valid signature proves who built the code, not that the code is safe.
  • Ask vendors hard questions. Build-system security, update monitoring, and breach notification duties belong in contracts now.
Team response and communicationThe SOC opens a bridge with identity, network, and endpoint teams on the first confirmed signal. The vendor manager contacts the vendor for a written incident statement, while legal prepares holding language for regulators and customers. Leadership receives facts, not guesses: which systems ran the affected software, what the logs show, what was blocked, and what happens in the next 24 hours. One written timeline keeps every team aligned, and one named incident lead keeps it that way.
Public sourcesThis scenario is simplified from: CISA Alert AA20-352A, Advanced Persistent Threat Compromise of Government Agencies, Critical Infrastructure Entities, and Private Sector Organizations (December 2020); and a CISA Alert on the Kaseya VSA supply chain ransomware incident (July 2021).

Mitigation and Defense

No single control stops a trust-path attack. MITRE states officially that this technique cannot be easily mitigated with preventive controls. MITRE recommends minimizing the amount and sensitivity of data available to external parties. In practice you stack layers. The inventory tells you what exists. Identity and network controls shrink it. Monitoring catches the moment a key is used in the wrong place.

Enterprise defenses

  • Build a vendor inventory with risk tiers. List every vendor with remote access, data access, or code access, and mark the ones that could cause the most damage.
  • Require MFA and PAM jump hosts for all third-party access. Multi-factor authentication stops stolen passwords, and a privileged access management jump host records everything a vendor does.
  • Use time-boxed, least-privilege vendor accounts. Access expires on schedule and opens only what the contract needs.
  • Segment the network. Vendor access reaches only the systems it needs, never the payment floor or the domain controllers.
  • Keep an egress allowlist for vendor channels. Update agents and management tools may call only their approved endpoints, from approved servers, on approved schedules.
  • Baseline vendor traffic and alert on new sources. A vendor login from a new IP, or a new server calling the update channel, becomes a visible, loud event.
  • Put security and incident-notification clauses in contracts. The vendor must tell you quickly, in writing, when they are breached, and must share their security posture.
  • Offboard fast. When a contract ends, accounts, tunnels, and firewall rules are removed the same week.
  • Watch vendor breach news and rotate exposed credentials. When a vendor is breached, reset what they can reach before the attacker tries it.

Home & small business defenses

  • List who you depend on. Write down the outside companies that can reach your systems: your IT provider, your payroll service, your backup vendor.
  • Turn on MFA for every shared account. A stolen password alone should never open your door, especially for accounts shared with an outside provider.
  • Use a unique password for every service. One leaked password should never unlock two doors.
  • Review connected apps and devices twice a year. Remove access for tools and partners you no longer use.
  • Update vendor software from official channels only. Old remote-access tools are the easiest doors on any small network.
  • Ask your provider simple questions. Do you use MFA? Who can reach our data? How fast do you tell us about a breach?
LayerActionWhy it works

Measure the layers, not the effort. Three numbers tell the story. First, how many critical vendors have MFA. Second, how many vendor channels sit inside the egress allowlist. Third, how fast a new vendor source IP reaches a human. When those three numbers look good, the trust map is under control.

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

This section is aimed at SOC analysts and detection engineers in daily work. Every mistake below is common, cheap to fix, and visible in a real breach report.

These mistakes share one root cause: vendor access feels boring. It works for months without attention, so it drifts to the bottom of every backlog. Meanwhile attackers rank vendor access as a top prize, because one quiet login beats ten loud exploits. Budget follows attention, so make vendor risk visible in every quarterly review. The fix is to treat vendor access as production territory, with owners, reviews, and alerts like any other critical system. The good news: none of these fixes needs new budget, only scheduled attention.

MistakeWhy it hurtsBetter way
Vendor accounts without MFAOne stolen or guessed password is enough to walk in through the trust path.Enforce MFA on every third-party identity, no exceptions, and track it as a metric.
Vendor VPN profiles never audited or revokedTunnels from ended contracts stay open for years, unnoticed and unwatched.Review all vendor VPN profiles and firewall rules quarterly and remove what is unused.
Flat network: vendor reaches everythingA single vendor login equals access to the whole internal network.Segment so vendor access reaches only the contracted systems, and test it with a real connection.
Trusting vendor traffic by defaultAlert rules exclude vendor IPs and accounts, so the attacker's favorite lane is blind.Monitor vendor traffic with its own tuned rules instead of wide exclusion lists.
No baseline of vendor trafficStrange vendor logins and new update callers look normal, because nobody knows what normal is.Baseline per vendor: hours, source IPs, destination systems, and volume, then alert on change.
Update channels never monitoredA trojanized update beacons for weeks before anyone asks about its traffic.Log and alert on update endpoint calls per server, with an allowlist and a rhythm check.
Security questionnaires used as the only controlWritten answers age fast and nobody verifies them against reality.Add technical checks: MFA proof, segmentation tests, and monitoring of real vendor activity.
No offboarding checklistAccounts from ended contracts stay alive and nobody owns them anymore.Automate removal on contract end and require sign-off from both IT and procurement.

Fix the table in order of speed. Week one: turn on MFA for every vendor identity and move vendor alerts to a priority queue. Week two: build the vendor inventory and mark the risk tiers. Week three: baseline vendor traffic and add the update channel to the egress allowlist. Week four: review VPN profiles and firewall rules, and remove what is dead. Each step has an owner, so nothing waits for a committee. Thirty days is enough to close the cheapest doors.

Two habits keep the fixes alive. First, give every vendor account a named owner in writing, so nobody asks whose alert it is. Second, rehearse one vendor breach scenario per year with the real people: SOC, IT, procurement, and legal. The first rehearsal is always clumsy, and that is exactly why it matters.

Best practices checklist

Use the checklist below as a quarterly review page. Print it, walk it with IT and procurement together, and record the date for every line. A checklist with dates becomes evidence; a checklist without dates stays a wish. Share the result with leadership in the same week.

  • Every vendor with remote or data access is inventoried and risk-tiered.
  • All third-party logins require MFA and, where privileged, a PAM jump host.
  • Vendor accounts are time-boxed, least-privilege, and reviewed quarterly.
  • Network segmentation limits each vendor to its contracted systems.
  • Vendor traffic is baselined, and new source IPs raise an alert.
  • Vendor update channels are allowlisted and watched for fixed-interval calls.
  • Vendor account alerts go to a priority queue with a short triage target.
  • Offboarding removes accounts, tunnels, and firewall rules within days.
Closing thoughtTrust is an attack surface. Attackers do not break your strongest wall; they walk through the smallest company that holds a key to it. Inventory the keys, shrink them, log every turn of the lock, and the trust map stops being a secret weapon for the attacker. Revisit the whole page after every vendor change, because trust maps move quietly.

Glossary of Key Terms

Every technical term used in this guide, explained in simple words.

Trust dependency
A company, product, or service your systems rely on and accept automatically, without checking each request again.
MSP (managed service provider)
An outside company that runs part of your IT for you, such as servers, backups, or the help desk.
Contractor
A person or company hired to do defined work for you, often with their own tools and some access to your systems.
SaaS (software as a service)
Software you use in the browser while another company hosts and runs it for you.
Trust relationship
An agreed link where two systems or companies accept each other's users, data, or traffic without asking again.
Supply chain
The full chain of companies and products that deliver software, hardware, or services to you.
Update channel
The path a vendor uses to ship new versions of its software to your systems.
Build pipeline
The automated steps that turn a vendor's source code into a finished, installable product.
Code signing
A cryptographic stamp that proves who produced a file and that it was not changed after signing.
Trojanized update
A real software update that an attacker secretly changed to add harmful code before it was signed and shipped.
Beacon
A small, repeated signal from inside a network to an attacker's system, used to report status and receive orders.
Egress traffic
Network traffic that leaves your environment toward the internet. Watching it catches beacons and data theft.
Allowlist
A list of approved items, such as IP addresses or domains. Everything not on the list is blocked or alerted on.
MFA (multi-factor authentication)
A login that needs a password plus a second proof, such as a code from an app.
Identity provider (IdP)
The central service that checks logins for your cloud tools and records where each sign-in came from.
Lateral movement
The attacker's steps from one system to the next inside a network after the first entry.
Sigma
A public rule format for describing detections, so the same logic works in many security tools.
SPL (search processing language)
The search language of the Splunk platform. Each Sigma rule in section 5.5 has an SPL version for quick testing.
