T1608 Stage Capabilities

Modern attacks start long before the first alarm rings. The attacker prepares websites, malicious files, certificates, and links so that everything is ready when the victim arrives. This page explains this preparation step, called Stage Capabilities, and shows defenders how to notice it early.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    This section explains what Stage Capabilities means in plain words. It shows why defenders should care about a phase they can barely see. It also places the technique in the full attack chain.

    What the technique is

    Stage Capabilities means that an attacker prepares everything needed for an attack before touching any victim. The attacker uploads malware to a file host, uploads tools, or installs a TLS certificate on a server. They may also prepare a website that attacks visitors, or place links where future victims will click them.

    Think of it as a burglar preparing tools, printing a fake delivery uniform, and renting a van days before the break-in. Nothing illegal has touched your house yet, but the preparation is real and leaves traces in the outside world.

    Why it matters

    This is the earliest phase a defender can possibly see. If you notice a staged domain or a staged file, you can block the attack before any user clicks anything. That is far cheaper than cleaning an infected network.

    Staging also explains a common puzzle: an incident response team often finds that the "attack" seemed to appear instantly. In reality, infrastructure was staged days or weeks earlier. Teams that watch staging get extra warning time.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Stage Capabilities belongs to the Resource Development tactic. Attackers first acquire infrastructure (T1583) and obtain capabilities (T1588). Staging (T1608) puts those pieces into place so victims can reach them.

    After staging comes Initial Access: a phishing email (T1566), a drive-by visit to a booby-trapped website (T1189), or a user downloading the staged file (T1204). Staging is the bridge between preparation and first contact.

    2. Step 2 — Prepare the server The attacker installs a web server and a fake update page on the rented host. Defender view: nothing yet on victim side; URL scanning services may already fingerprint the page content.
    3. Step 3 — Install a certificate The attacker requests a free TLS certificate, so the site shows the trusted padlock icon. Defender view: the issuance appears immediately in public Certificate Transparency logs; monitoring services can alert within minutes.
    4. Step 4 — Upload payload The attacker uploads the malware installer and a remote-access tool to the staging server. Defender view: malware feeds and sandboxes start reporting the new file hashes within hours.
    5. Step 5 — Plant the link The attacker places the staging URL in a short email that looks like an invoice notice and posts it in a forum. Defender view: the mail gateway sees the URL but has no reputation data for such a young domain.
    6. Step 6 — The victim clicks An employee clicks the link and lands on the fake update page. Defender view: the first internal evidence appears: a proxy and DNS log entry for a very young domain with almost no global traffic.
    7. Step 7 — Payload download The page offers a file; the browser saves installer.zip. Defender view: EDR records the download with the "internet zone" marker, and the proxy log shows an executable or archive from a rare host.
    8. Step 8 — The SOC reacts The analyst joins the dots: fresh domain, fresh certificate, feed-reported hash, one internal click. The domain is blocked, users are checked, and the hosting provider is asked to take the site down. Defender view: hunting finds no other internal systems touched the domain.

    Note

    The simulation is a simplified teaching model. Real staging campaigns may run these steps in parallel, use dozens of domains at once, or hide the staging server behind legitimate cloud services.

    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section looks at staging through the eyes of an attacker. Understanding why and how they prepare infrastructure helps you predict what to look for. Everything here is described for defensive learning; lab examples run only on your own machine.

    When and why attackers stage

    Attackers stage capabilities because preparation pays. A ready-made phishing link, a working download page, and a valid TLS certificate make the later attack faster and more believable. If the staging server is blocked or taken down, only the front door is lost, not the whole operation.

    Staging also brings scale and separation. One staged download page can serve thousands of victims, and if defenders start investigating, the victim never touched anything that points back to the attacker's main systems.

    How it is performed

    The typical preparation run has a clear shape. The attacker registers a domain that sounds harmless or copied, rents cheap cloud hosting, and installs a web server. Then they request a free TLS certificate, upload the payload and tools, and build a page that pretends to be something useful. Finally the attacker places links to it in emails, comments, or search results.

    Each part is cheap and fast. A full staging setup can be online in under an hour, which is why defenders see such high volumes of fresh malicious domains every day.

    Where it applies

    Staging lives in the PRE environment: attacker-owned servers, rented cloud instances, bulletproof hosting providers, compromised legitimate websites, and third-party services such as file hosts, code hosts, and social platforms. The victim's own network is not involved yet.

    Because it happens outside your perimeter, your own firewall or endpoint product cannot log it. The only witnesses are public data sources and, later, the first internal click.

    Prerequisites and conditions

    • A built payload or tool (T1587 / T1588) ready to upload.
    • Acquired or compromised infrastructure (T1583 / T1584): domains, servers, accounts.
    • For SEO or AI poisoning: knowledge of what future victims will search for.
    • For drive-by staging: access to a website victims already trust.
    • Little or no money: most staging needs only free services and cheap hosting.

    Lab only — safe practice setup

    You can rehearse the defensive side by building a harmless staging-like server on your own test machine. This uses no real malware and must never be exposed to the internet.

    lab_stage_setup.sh — runs only on your own test VM
    # LAB ONLY - harmless imitation of a staging setup, run inside your own test VM
mkdir -p ~/lab/stage && cd ~/lab/stage
echo "placeholder file - not malware" > update.zip

# Self-signed certificate so the local server speaks HTTPS (free CA not used in the lab)
openssl req -x509 -newkey rsa:2048 -nodes -keyout key.pem -out cert.pem \
  -days 7 -subj "/CN=localhost"

# Serve the folder locally on port 8443
python3 -c "import ssl,http.server,functools; \
h=functools.partial(http.server.SimpleHTTPRequestHandler); \
srv=http.server.ThreadingHTTPServer(('127.0.0.1',8443),h); \
ctx=ssl.SSLContext(ssl.PROTOCOL_TLS_SERVER); ctx.load_cert_chain('cert.pem','key.pem'); \
srv.socket=ctx.wrap_socket(srv.socket,server_side=True); srv.serve_forever()"

# In a second terminal, check it like a defender would
curl -k https://127.0.0.1:8443/update.zip -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n"

    Common variations

    Attackers rent bulletproof hosting that ignores abuse reports. They abuse free tiers of cloud platforms or hijack legitimate websites to host staged content. Some push payloads through short-link chains that redirect through several services before the final download.

    Attacker mistakes you can catch

    Reused infrastructure across campaigns, long-lived certificates on brand-new domains, decoy pages whose wording does not match the search terms used to reach them, and test clicks from hosting IP ranges. Each slip creates an outside-in detection opportunity.

    Cost of failure

    When staging is discovered early, attackers lose payloads, certificates, accounts, and time. That is why they rotate fast — and why your monitoring must be continuous, not a weekly review of old reports.

    4

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders cannot watch the attacker's desk, but staging still leaves traces in public and in your first-click logs. This section shows what those traces look like, where to find them, and how to triage them quickly.

    How defenders can see it

    Three vantage points matter. First, public telemetry: Certificate Transparency logs, passive DNS databases, WHOIS, and URL scanning services record staging activity worldwide, whether or not you are a target. Second, your own edge: proxy, DNS, and mail logs capture the moment a user touches staged content. Third, threat intelligence: vendors and ISACs report staged infrastructure used against your sector.

    Artifacts staging leaves

    • Domains registered hours or days before first use, often on cheap new extensions.
    • TLS certificates from free authorities issued at odd hours for lookalike names.
    • Files uploaded to file hosts that sandboxes later flag as malicious.
    • Fake forum threads, bogus update pages, or poisoned answers left on public platforms.
    • Inside your logs: one or few clicks to a domain with almost no global reputation.

    Where to look

    Monitor Certificate Transparency feeds for certificate issuances that match your brand or suppliers. Keep a watchlist of "newly observed domains" for your industry keywords. In your SIEM, join proxy and DNS logs against domain-age and feed data. In mail logs, look for URLs whose host is younger than a week.

    Why it matters

    Catching staging means acting before compromise: a blocklist entry instead of a reimage. It also feeds the whole program — every confirmed staging artifact improves your blocklists, your awareness training examples, and your threat-intel picture of who is targeting you.

    Short triage flow for a suspected staged resource

    1. 1 — Receive the signal A CT-log alert, a feed report, a user report, or an internal click to a rare domain arrives in the queue.
    2. 2 — Check age and history Look up domain registration date, certificate issuance date, and first passive-DNS sighting. Anything younger than 7 days deserves attention.
    3. 3 — Look, do not log in Inspect the URL in an online scanning service from outside the network. Never open the staged content from a corporate device.
    4. 4 — Decide severity Harmless parked domain: close with a note. Malicious content: continue. Unclear: watch passively and re-check in 24 hours.
    5. 5 — Contain Block the domain and its IP at proxy, DNS, and mail level. Search historical logs for any earlier internal contact.
    6. 6 — Report and enrich Send the indicator to your blocklist, your ISAC, and the hosting provider. Record which campaign keyword or lure it used.

    Good practice

    Measure "time from staging to block". If your CT-log and newly-observed-domain alerts reach a blocklist decision in under one hour for in-scope keywords, the attacker's staging investment loses most of its value.

    Note

    No regulator demands that you detect staging on the attacker's server. They demand that you operate the monitoring and filtering that makes staged content visible and blockable on your side. Frame your staging program in those words during audits.

    5.2Threat intelligence

    Staging detection lives on external data. This section lists the sources worth feeding into your program. It explains the cycle of collection, analysis, and action. It also shows how to grade confidence before you block something.

    Core sources

    • MITRE ATT&CK — the shared language for staging (T1608 family) and the techniques that follow it.
    • Vendor reporting — Mandiant, Microsoft, ESET, Proofpoint, and others publish staged-infrastructure indicators with campaigns.
    • OSINT services — certificate search sites (for example crt.sh), URL scanning services, WHOIS, passive DNS, abuse.ch feeds such as URLhaus and ThreatFox.
    • Commercial feeds — newly-observed-domain feeds, phishing infrastructure feeds, sandbox detonation feeds.
    • ISACs — sector sharing communities (for example FS-ISAC, H-ISAC, MS-ISAC) exchange staging indicators seen against peers.
    • Internal telemetry — your own proxy, DNS, mail, and EDR history shows which staged resources actually reached users.

    Collection, analysis, application

    Collect by subscribing to two or three feeds and automating CT-log and new-domain monitoring for your brand and industry keywords. Analyse by joining indicators: a fresh domain plus a fresh free-CA certificate plus a reported file hash is a strong staging pattern.

    Apply the intelligence in three ways. Set preventive blocks for high-confidence indicators. Build detection content from the Sigma rules in section 5.5. Write short intel notes that tell leadership who is targeting you and how early you can see them.

    Record every decision. When a blocked domain later appears in a vendor report, that feedback loop proves your pipeline works.

    ConfidenceMeaningTypical staging exampleSafe action
    HIGHMultiple independent sources agree, or you observed the full chain yourself.Sandbox flags the file, two feeds list the domain, CT shows lookalike name.Block immediately at DNS, proxy, and mail; hunt for internal contact.
    MEDIUMOne reliable source or several weak signals combined.Newly-observed domain matching your industry keyword, no content confirmation yet.Watchlist, passive scanning, alert on internal contact; no broad block yet.

    Actors and campaigns known for heavy staging

    Mustang Panda
    The only group MITRE documents on the parent technique itself: it used servers under its control to validate tracking pixels sent to phishing victims (Proofpoint, 2022). Nearly every other named example lives on the sub-technique pages.
    EXOTIC LILY
    Uploaded malicious payloads to file-sharing services including TransferNow, TransferXL, WeTransfer, and OneDrive while brokering initial access (Google TAG, 2022). Documented under T1608.001.
    Saint Bear
    Used the Discord content delivery network to host malicious content referenced in emails and links (Palo Alto Unit 42, 2022). Documented under T1608.001.
    Sea Turtle
    Captured legitimate SSL certificates from victim organizations and installed them on attacker-controlled infrastructure for adversary-in-the-middle operations (Cisco Talos, 2019). Documented under T1608.003.
    Mustard Tempest
    Staged the SocGholish fake-update chain on compromised web infrastructure and fed it with poisoned search results (SentinelOne 2022, Red Canary 2024). Documented under T1608.004 and T1608.006.
    Operation Spalax (C0005)
    Staged malware on legitimate hosting services such as OneDrive and MediaFire during campaigns against Colombian government and military targets (ESET, 2021). Documented by MITRE as a campaign — see the case study below.

    5.3Threats ranking and categorization

    The six threats below are the realistic ways staging turns into harm for an organization. Each has a severity, the platforms it touches, what must be true before it can happen, its impact, and why the severity was chosen. Severity reflects combined likelihood and business damage for a typical mid-size organization.

    IDThreatSeverityPlatformPreconditionsImpactJustification
    TH-01 Malware staged on public file hosts and code platforms — payload uploaded to a file-sharing or code-sharing service, then linked to victims. HIGH CloudNetwork Attacker has a working payload and an anonymous upload path. Initial access, credential theft, ransomware entry. Extremely common across commodity eCrime; hosting on trusted platforms defeats naive filtering.
    TH-02 Fresh domain with free TLS certificate as staged front door — new domain plus certificate installed to look legitimate. MEDIUM CloudNetwork Attacker registers domains at will; free CAs issue automatically. Enables phishing, payload delivery, and C2 that padlock-aware users trust. Very high volume but individually avoidable; strong outside-in telemetry exists.
    TH-03 Drive-by staging on compromised legitimate sites — trusted website prepared to attack its own visitors. HIGH NetworkEndpointCloud Attacker compromises a CMS or hosting account your users already visit. Silent exploitation with zero user clicks beyond normal browsing. Low user suspicion defeats training; browser exploits can be severe, though modern sandboxing lowers success rates.
    TH-04 Staged links distributed at scale — links to staged content planted in email, social media, forums, and hijacked threads. HIGH IdentityNetworkEmail Staged page exists; attacker has a distribution channel (botnet, hijacked account, spam service). Direct path to phishing success and payload delivery. The single most common bridge between staging and a real compromise in 2020s eCrime.
    TH-05 SEO poisoning targeting professional search terms — poisoned results surface fake forums and downloads for document searches. MEDIUM NetworkEndpoint Attacker controls many compromised sites and grooms them into search indexes. High-trust victims download malicious archives for work reasons. Targeted at specific professions (finance, HR, IT), so likelihood varies by sector; impact can reach ransomware.
    TH-06 Tool staging for post-exploitation — attacker stages remote-access and movement tools to be pulled by an implant already inside. MEDIUM NetworkWindowsLinuxCloud Requires an existing foothold, so it applies after initial access. Escalates a small incident into full control and lateral movement. Not an entry threat by itself, but its detection often reveals an otherwise-missed intrusion.

    Note

    Ratings are starting points. Adjust them to your sector. An SEO-poisoning threat jumps to High for a law firm that searches legal templates daily. TH-03 jumps to Critical for a browser-based business that handles sensitive data.

    TH-06 — Tool staging for post-exploitation

    Log sources
    Proxy logs segmented by asset criticality, firewall logs, Linux shell history or auditd, Windows process creation with network correlation, EDR network events
    Key fields
    src_ip (asset role), dest_ip, dest_port, url, bytes_in, Image, CommandLine, ParentImage, User
    Normal
    Servers rarely pull code-host content; workstations fetch from approved mirrors with package managers
    Suspicious
    Server subnet fetching raw payloads from code hosts or file hosts; curl or certutil on Windows fetching executables; implant downloading a second-stage tool minutes after first beacon
    Detection logic
    (src is server/DC subnet) AND (dest in code/file host category) — or (process is LOLBin downloader) AND (target not in approved package mirror list)
    endpoint event — implant pulls staged tool (Sysmon Event ID 1)
    {
  "EventTime": "2026-10-19T11:40:03Z",
  "EventID": 1,

    Real-World Case Study

    Operation Spalax is the cleanest public example of this whole family of staging work in one campaign. ESET researchers documented threat actors staging malware on legitimate hosting services such as OneDrive and MediaFire. Phishing emails then drove victims to those downloads. MITRE tracks the campaign as C0005. The chain below comes from that public reporting; the victim-organization timeline afterwards is a labeled reconstruction for teaching purposes.

    Context

    The campaign ran against Colombian government offices, military units, and private companies from 2019 onward. ESET could not firmly name the group, and noted some tooling overlaps with APT-C-36 (Blind Eagle). The staging choice matters more than the name. The actors hosted their malware inside trusted cloud platforms, so the delivery link pointed at a domain no blocklist wanted to touch.

    Attack chain

    • Prepare lure documents that imitate government communications (staged content).
    • Upload malicious archives to OneDrive and MediaFire accounts (T1608.001 Upload Malware).
    • Send phishing emails with links that point at the staged files (T1608.005 Link Target).
    • Victim downloads and opens the archive; the implant runs (user execution).
    • The chain delivered Verblecon, a loader ESET described in the same report, built for espionage follow-on.
    • Attackers kept new uploads ready so takedowns of one link changed little.

    Timeline of one victim organization (reconstruction)

    1. Day 0, 09:12 An administration employee receives an email that imitates a government notice and clicks a link to a OneDrive-hosted archive.
    2. Day 0, 09:15 The user downloads and opens the archive; the implant starts and calls out over HTTPS.
    3. Day 0, 11:30 The proxy flags the first unusual egress: a workstation posting to a cloud file host that no other user touches, then to a young command domain.
    4. Day 0, 13:00 The SOC isolates the workstation and pulls the OneDrive link from mail history; two more recipients never opened it.
    5. Day 1 The team reports the hosting URL to the platform's abuse system and blocks the command domain; the implant never receives tasking.
    6. Day 2–3 Credentials reachable from the host are rotated, mail sessions revoked, and the affected user returns to a rebuilt machine.

    Detection point

    The winning signal was a join, not a single log. Mail URL telemetry showed a file-host link. Then the proxy saw a download from that host, then rare egress from the same workstation. Any one event alone looked ordinary. The sequence did not.

    Containment and eradication

    The host was isolated within two hours of the first egress alert. Investigators removed the implant and its scheduled tasks, reset the credentials the profile could reach, and revoked web-mail sessions to kill cookie reuse.

    Recovery

    The user returned to a rebuilt machine the next morning. The team added the staging pattern — mail link into a personal cloud file host — to awareness training, and reported the abuse so the platform suspended the uploader account.

    Impact and consequences

    Because the implant never received its follow-on tasking, the loss stayed at response cost: roughly ninety staff-hours, one day of disrupted work, and a precautionary regulatory notification review. The alternative — a quiet espionage foothold — is what the same staging pattern produced at less prepared victims named in the reporting.

    Team response and communication

    The SOC paged IR at detection, and one incident channel coordinated IT, legal, and communications. Daily briefings used three numbers: hosts affected, credentials rotated, and staged URLs reported. Keeping the metric set small prevented both panic and complacency.

    Lessons learned

    • Staging on legitimate cloud platforms defeats pure domain reputation; the control is the sequence (mail link → file host → rare egress), not any one log.
    • Reporting abuse to the hosting platform is a real control: it removes the staged file for every future victim, not just yours.
    • Two more recipients never opened the lure — staging campaigns live or die on the first click, so click-time mail defenses pay off.
    • Share confirmed staging indicators with your ISAC; peers had seen the same file-host pattern weeks earlier.

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop an attacker from preparing. You can make their preparation useless: harden the entry points staging feeds, watch the outside signals, and rehearse the response. Controls below are grouped by layer, with a priority table at the end.

    Official mitigation position

    ATT&CK lists exactly one mitigation for T1608: M1056 Pre-Compromise. Staging runs on infrastructure and accounts the attacker owns, outside your walls, so it cannot be prevented directly. Every control below therefore hardens what staging feeds — users, mail, web, and endpoints — and shortens the time between staging and abuse.

    Policy and people

    Write a policy for software downloads: approved sources only, with a simple request path for exceptions. Train users with real staged-content examples — fake forums, lookalike portals, poisoned search results — and give them a one-click report button that actually gets answered.

    Endpoint

    Run EDR in block mode with MOTW-aware rules, block script interpreters from executing user-downloaded content, keep browsers and OS patched, and remove local admin rights. These controls neutralize most staged payloads even after a user downloads them.

    Network and web

    Enforce proxy categories: block personal file-sharing for office users, allow with logging for IT. Filter DNS with a newly-observed-domain feed. Consider blocking direct IP-hostname access and rare TLDs used heavily by staging (.icu, .top, .click) with an exception process.

    Identity

    Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 keys or platform passkeys) for all remote access and admin accounts. Staged credential pages lose most of their value when a stolen password alone is not enough.

    Cloud and your web estate

    Patch and MFA-protect the CMS platforms you operate so attackers cannot stage on your sites. Add file-integrity monitoring for web roots, WAF rules against injected scripts, and CI egress restrictions so servers cannot fetch staged tools.

    Logging, monitoring, response

    Ship proxy, DNS, mail, and EDR logs to the SIEM. Run the CT-log and new-domain watchlists from section 5. Keep the triage flow from section 4 rehearsed, with a quarterly purple-team test of one staging scenario end to end.

    Home and small-office advice

    Keep the operating system and browser set to auto-update, and add a reputable ad and script blocker. Be suspicious of document downloads from search results that lead to forum-style pages, and never enable macros or open downloaded scripts. Use the built-in protection of your platform, turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it is offered, and back up important files to a service that keeps history. A hardware security key for your main email account is the single best 30-dollar upgrade for a home user.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    These are the failures detection teams actually make with staging. Each card shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from coming back.

    1. Treating staging as out of scope

    Symptom
    No alert exists until a user already clicked.
    Root cause
    The team scopes detection only to endpoint techniques.
    Fix
    Add two outside-in feeds (CT logs, new domains) to the roadmap now.
    Prevention
    Make Resource Development tactics part of the detection charter.

    2. Relying only on file hashes

    Symptom
    Feeds match yesterday's sample; today's variant sails through.
    Root cause
    Attackers rebuild payloads hourly, so hashes age fast.
    Fix
    Detect on behavior chains (host age + file type + referer), not identity.
    Prevention
    Review rules quarterly for hash-only logic.

    3. Ignoring Certificate Transparency

    Symptom
    Phishing/staging domains appear "suddenly" during incidents.
    Root cause
    Nobody consumes the free, real-time CT stream.
    Fix
    Stand up a CT watchlist for brand and supplier keywords.
    Prevention
    Add CT coverage to the logging coverage matrix.

    4. No DNS resolver logging

    Symptom
    Proxy misses traffic that bypassed it (browsers, tools, malware).
    Root cause
    Resolver logs never shipped to the SIEM.
    Fix
    Ship resolver logs with client IP and query name.
    Prevention
    Alert when log volume from a resolver drops to zero.

    5. Blanket blocking of whole platforms

    Symptom
    Business outages after blocking a big CDN; alert fatigue first.
    Root cause
    Blunt category blocks instead of risk-scored decisions.
    Fix
    Block by behavior (file type + age + referer), allowlist the legitimate flows.
    Prevention
    Every block policy lists its intended business exceptions.

    6. No owner for the TI pipeline

    Symptom
    Feeds expire silently; lookups return nothing for weeks.
    Root cause
    Nobody owns feed refresh and rule maintenance.
    Fix
    Assign a named detection owner with a monthly health check.
    Prevention
    Dashboard shows feed freshness and lookup hit rates.

    7. Tuning to silence

    Symptom
    Rules quietly exclude so much that real chains no longer fire.
    Root cause
    Pressure to cut alert volume without fixing root causes.
    Fix
    Re-tune with documented exclusions and review by a second engineer.
    Prevention
    Replay a labeled test set after every tuning change.

    8. Never validating detections

    Symptom
    Rule fires in the vendor demo, never in your tenant.
    Root cause
    No purple-team testing of staging scenarios.
    Fix
    Rehearse one scenario quarterly (see section 7).
    Prevention
    Track detection coverage against the T1608 family.

    9. Missing new TLD abuse

    Symptom
    Staging domains on .icu/.top/.click never match .com-centric rules.
    Root cause
    Watchlists built years ago for legacy TLDs.
    Fix
    Add cheap-abuse TLDs to rules and risk scoring.
    Prevention
    Review TLD statistics twice a year.

    10. Assuming HTTPS means safe

    Symptom
    Users trust any padlock; tickets dismiss malicious sites as "secure".
    Root cause
    Old training equated TLS with trust.
    Fix
    Retrain: padlock means encryption, not honesty.
    Prevention
    Include padlock-abuse examples in annual training.

    11. No historical hunt after a feed hit

    Symptom
    Blocking a domain today but nobody checks who clicked last month.
    Root cause
    Response runbook stops at the block action.
    Fix
    Add "search 90 days of proxy/DNS for the indicator" to the runbook.
    Prevention
    Metrics track hunts run per confirmed indicator.

    12. Ownership gaps on response actions

    Symptom
    Blocklist requests sit in chat with no decision.
    Root cause
    No named approver for outside-in blocks.
    Fix
    Delegate authority: SOC can block staging indicators without a change ticket.
    Prevention
    Pre-approved block criteria written into the IR plan.

    Daily habits

    • Review CT and new-domain alerts for your keywords.
    • Check proxy hits to flagged staging indicators.
    • Confirm feed lookups still refresh.

    Weekly habits

    • Walk the triage flow on one live candidate.
    • Review alert volume and exclusion drift.
    • Share two indicators with your ISAC or peers.

    Monthly habits

    • Replay the test event set against all rules.
    • Review TLD and campaign trends in the news.
    • Report staging metrics to leadership.
