Overview

This section explains the technique in plain language, shows why it matters to defenders, and sets out the key facts you will need later.

What is this technique?

Adversaries create or generate content to support targeting and operations. The content can be written text, audio, images, video, or other media. They use it to establish fake personas, to impersonate known people or organizations, and to support social engineering, fraud, or influence activities.

Content may be developed before an operation or during it. Attackers can build it themselves, pay a third party to make it, or produce it with AI-assisted tools. They often use AI first to research the target and develop the pretext, and then to write the words, clone the voice, or render the fake video. MITRE lists two sub-techniques: T1683.001 Written Content and T1683.002 Audio-Visual Content.

Why it matters

Generated content removes the old signals defenders relied on. Phishing with spelling mistakes is easy to teach people to spot. A fluent email in your language, a cloned voice of your CFO, or a realistic job application is not. In 2025, Anthropic reported an AI-orchestrated espionage campaign (C0062, actor GTG-1002) in which an AI agent produced operational documentation at machine speed across roughly thirty targets.

For a defender, this technique is the fuel line for many downstream attacks. You usually cannot see it happening, but you can see what it produces when it reaches your organization.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Generate Content sits in the Resource Development tactic. That is the stage where the attacker prepares weapons, infrastructure, and personas. The content then feeds directly into Initial Access and Impact techniques:

Note · What "PRE" means

PRE is not a device platform like Windows or Linux. It marks activity that happens before the attacker compromises anything you own. ATT&CK's official detection strategy for this technique (DET0916, analytic AN2059) says: "Much of this takes place outside the visibility of the target organization, making detection difficult for defenders. Detection efforts may be focused on related stages of the adversary lifecycle, such as during Initial Access." This guide follows that advice: the threats, logs, and detections below all live at the edge where generated content meets your organization. Two words used a lot below: telemetry means the log data your systems record, and a detection is a rule that searches that data.

Step 1 of 8

Defender hint

The same story in words

Step 1

Pick the target job

The attacker studies a job posting and the company website. They learn which skills, certificates, and tools the company wants. Defender hint: public job posts tell attackers exactly what to fake.

Step 2

Draft the resume with AI

The attacker prompts a generative AI service to write a resume that matches the posting. The text is fluent and tailored. Nothing touches your network yet, so your logs show nothing. Defender hint: this is the part you cannot see, which is why later steps matter.

Step 3

Build the identity kit

AI tools also produce a profile photo, forged certificates, and portfolio files. The fake identity now looks complete. Defender hint: generated files often carry tool metadata you can read later.

Step 4

Submit the application

The attacker uploads the documents to your recruiting portal. The first real evidence appears: a file upload event, and PDF metadata that points to an AI or template tool. Defender hint: feed recruiting SaaS audit logs into your SIEM.

Step 5

Pass automated screening

The resume scores well because it is tailored and error-free. Keyword filters cannot see the lie. Defender hint: screening tools judge text quality, and that signal is now inverted.

Step 6

Win the video interview

The candidate answers with AI assistance and sometimes an altered camera feed. Meeting logs show a new external participant on an unknown device. Defender hint: log interview sessions like any other remote access.

Step 7

Get hired and provisioned

HR creates the account and ships a laptop. Identity logs show MFA registered from a new device and a new location. Defender hint: watch new-hire onboarding telemetry in the first 30 days.

Step 8

SOC connects the dots

The SOC links the metadata, device, and interview anomalies into one case. Access is revoked, the laptop image is preserved, and the pattern is shared with peers. Defender hint: no single signal was enough; correlation was.

Red Team Perspective

This section explains how attackers think about content generation. It is written for defenders and red teamers who need to understand the behavior. All examples are lab-only and educational.

When and why attackers use it

Generated content is cheap, fast, and scalable. One operator with AI tools can produce work that used to need a team: fluent text in any language, a credible photo, a cloned voice, a live video face. That removes the biggest cost of social engineering: preparation time.

It also removes the old quality tells. Recruiters, help desks, and finance teams are trained to spot bad grammar. They are not trained to spot a perfect email written by a language model. Attackers use this technique when the attack depends on trust, not on exploits.

The goal of the work

The output of T1683 is trust. A resume earns an interview. A fluent email earns a click. A cloned voice earns an MFA reset. A deepfaked "CFO" earns a wire transfer. The attacker is not attacking software here; they are attacking the human decision that guards a process.

That is why this technique belongs to Resource Development: it is preparation. The real damage happens later, in Phishing, Impersonation, and Financial Theft.

How it is performed (generic flow)

  1. Research. Collect public details about the target: names, titles, tone of voice, current projects, job postings, org charts. AI services can speed up this research.
  2. Choose tooling. Pick AI services and editors for the needed medium: text models for written content, voice cloning and face models for audio-visual work.
  3. Generate. Produce drafts: lure emails, fake resumes, forged documents, synthetic audio or video, persona posts.
  4. Polish. Iterate on tone, urgency, branding, and language until the content passes a casual read.
  5. Stage. Register look-alike domains, create throwaway mailboxes and social accounts, host fake pages.
  6. Use. Deliver the content through phishing, applications, support calls, meetings, or social media, and watch for engagement.
Ethics notice

This page describes attacker behavior so defenders can detect it. Do not run these steps against real people or real organizations. Practice only inside an isolated lab with your own test data, and follow your organization's rules and local law.

Where it applies

Job & hiring scams

Fake applicants, fake recruiters, and fabricated employment credentials. Used by groups such as DPRK-linked Contagious Interview (G1052).

BEC & invoice fraud

AI-written executive requests and invoices that copy the real brand voice. Tooling such as Kali365 (S9044) automates this at scale.

Brand impersonation

Fake support chats, cloned portals, and branded decoy documents, as used by APT38 (G0082).

Romance & investment scams

Long-lived fake personas whose chats, photos, and voice notes are all generated.

Fake support & IT personas

Attackers pose as help desk staff to pressure users into approvals and credential resets.

Influence operations

Bulk articles and social posts generated to shape opinions or damage a brand.

Common variations and attacker mistakes

Variations you will meet

  • In-house generation: the attacker runs the tools themselves and keeps full control.
  • Third-party content: content is commissioned from specialists or bought from marketplaces.
  • AI-assisted tailoring: base content is reused, then AI adapts it to each victim's language and role.
  • Template farming: one good lure is mass-produced with small per-target changes.

Mistakes attackers make

  • Metadata left in exported files (generator names, template paths, timestamps).
  • Recycled photos and bios reused across many fake personas.
  • Bursts of activity: dozens of near-identical uploads or posts in minutes.
  • Look-alike domains registered in patterns (same registrar, same TLD set).
  • Live deepfakes that lag, glitch, or refuse to turn the head or speak on demand.

Every mistake is a detection opportunity. Section 5 turns each one into a rule.

Blue Team Perspective

You will rarely catch the generation itself. You can catch the content when it touches your organization. This section maps what to watch, what artifacts appear, and how to triage them.

How defenders can see it

Mail gateway

First-time senders, fresh sending domains, display-name impersonation, and suspiciously polished urgent text.

Proxy & CASB

Access to AI generation services, look-alike domains, and newly registered destinations from your network.

SaaS audit logs

Recruiting portals, meeting platforms, and collaboration tools log uploads, participants, and device details.

Identity provider

New accounts, MFA re-registrations, and password or MFA resets that follow help desk pressure calls.

Brand monitoring

Certificate Transparency logs, domain watch services, and social monitoring reveal impersonation early.

People

Employee reports are still the fastest sensor. Make reporting one click and reward it.

Why it matters for the chain

Generate Content is upstream, so a defender's leverage is at the seams where content enters the organization. When you block a polished lure, you are not just stopping one email; you are measuring the attacker's resource development work. Failed attempts still reveal targeting, infrastructure, and intent, and those indicators enrich every downstream detection you own.

Downstream techniques that consume generated content: T1566 Phishing, T1598 Phishing for Information, T1534 Internal Spearphishing, T1656 Impersonation, and T1657 Financial Theft.

Triage flow

  1. Receive. Intake the report (user, gateway, brand monitor) and open a case with a fixed timestamp.
  2. Preserve. Keep the original message, files, headers, and log lines before anything changes.
  3. Verify out of band. Contact the claimed sender through a known channel. Never trust the contact details in the message itself.
  4. Check infrastructure. Sender domain age, TLS certificate age, hosting, and prior sightings in intel sources.
  5. Scope. Search the gateway and proxy for the same sender, domain, and campaign pattern across all mailboxes.
  6. Contain. Purge matching messages, block the domain and sender, and add the pattern to gateway rules.
  7. Close the loop. Feedback results to the reporter, tune detections (section 5.5), and share with your ISAC.
Warning · If money or credentials moved

Switch to the fraud incident path first: contact the bank to attempt recall, freeze affected workflows, and preserve evidence in parallel. Forensic curiosity can wait; recalled transfers cannot.

Critical · TH-01 AI-written phishing and BEC lures

What happens: an AI model drafts flawless, targeted lure text (executive requests, invoices, security notices) and sends it at scale through fresh domains or compromised accounts.

Preconditions: target research, generation tooling, delivery infrastructure, and an untrained or over-trusted approval process.

Impact: credentials, fraudulent payments, follow-on intrusion.

Justification: highest volume path; removes the legacy grammar tell, so content-only filters fail and losses follow (Kali365, Midnight Blizzard).

Critical · TH-03 Live deepfake social engineering

What happens: attackers clone executive voices or run real-time face swaps in calls to push transfers or MFA resets past human checks.

Preconditions: public audio/video of executives, cloned identity tooling, a target with payment or reset authority, weak out-of-band verification.

Impact: multi-million fraud (Arup lost about US$25M), silent MFA takeover.

Justification: bypasses text filters entirely; live human trust is the only control, and it fails under pressure.

High · TH-02 Synthetic job applicant

What happens: a fabricated identity with an AI resume, forged references, and interview assistance gets hired; the laptop and accounts become attacker property.

Preconditions: remote hiring, weak document verification, no recruiting telemetry in the SIEM.

Impact: insider access, pre-loaded malware, data theft (documented DPRK IT-worker operations, KnowBe4 2024).

Justification: rarer than phishing but converts directly into persistent insider access; multiple confirmed cases.

Medium · TH-04 Unapproved generative AI use

What happens: staff paste sensitive data into unapproved AI services, or attackers abuse approved AI accounts from stolen sessions, giving recon material and content templates.

Preconditions: no AI allowlist, no CASB/proxy visibility, no DLP on AI egress.

Impact: data leakage, policy gaps, target research for adversaries.

Justification: common and noisy but usually policy-level; direct compromise is less frequent than TH-01/TH-03.

Medium · TH-05 Brand impersonation infrastructure

What happens: attackers register look-alike domains, obtain TLS certificates, and generate bulk branded pages and posts to pre-position phishing and fraud.

Preconditions: none beyond registrar access; attackers automate brand cloning with AI.

Impact: low direct impact, but every Critical threat above leans on this infrastructure, and early detection here buys days of warning.

Justification: ranked medium because it is preparatory; its value is as an early-warning sensor you fully control.

sigma · yaml
title: Executive Display-Name Impersonation from Newly Registered Domain
id: a1f3c9d2-4b7e-4c8a-9d21-6e5f8a2b4c11
status: experimental
description: >
    Detects email whose display name impersonates an executive role and arrives
    from a young external domain on first contact. This matches AI-written BEC
    lures that lack legacy grammar or attachment tells.
references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1683/
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-20
logsource:
    category: email_gateway
    product: proofpoint
    service: tap
detection:
    selection_exec:
        display_name|contains:
            - 'CFO'
            - 'CEO'

            - 'Finance Director'

        first_contact: 'true'
    selection_new_domain:

    condition: selection_exec and selection_first_contact and selection_new_domain and not filter_internal
fields:

    - sender_domain
    - domain_age_days
    - subject
    - recipients_dept
falsepositives:

TH-01 · Splunk SPL

spl · splunk
index=email sourcetype="proofpoint:tap"
| eval sender_domain=lower(sender_domain)
| search display_name IN ("*CFO*", "*CEO*", "*Chief Financial*", "*Finance Director*", "*President*")
| where isnum(domain_age_days) AND domain_age_days < 30
| where lower(first_contact) = "true"
| where NOT match(sender_domain, "\.yourcompany\.com$")
| stats count AS messages, earliest(_time) AS first_seen, values(subject) AS subjects BY sender_domain, display_name
| convert ctime(first_seen)
| sort - messages

Mapping note: same logic as the Sigma rule; assumes Proofpoint TAP data normalized to display_name, sender_domain, domain_age_days, first_contact. For EOP, use index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity with Operation=MailItemsAccessed-adjacent transport rules or a gateway add-on.

False positives and tuning: tune with a partner-domain allowlist and raise the domain-age threshold to 60 or 90 days if new-supplier mail is common. Add recipients_dept IN ("finance","executive") to raise precision. Severity: high.

Real-World Case Study

Campaign C0062: the first documented large-scale AI-orchestrated espionage operation, reported by Anthropic in November 2025. It shows what happens when content generation becomes machine-speed.

Context

In mid-September 2025, Anthropic detected a threat actor later designated GTG-1002, assessed with high confidence as a Chinese state-sponsored espionage group. The operators abused the Claude Code coding agent, chained with Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools, to attack roughly thirty global targets: large technology companies, financial institutions, chemical manufacturers, and government agencies. What made the campaign historic was autonomy: Anthropic estimates 80 to 90 percent of the tactical work ran without human intervention, with operators approving only key decision points.

Generated content ran through the whole operation. The agents wrote operational documentation as they worked: discovered services, harvested credentials, exploitation techniques, and complete attack progress notes, staged in structured markdown files before exfiltration.

Timeline

Early September 2025

Preparation

Operators select targets and build an attack framework around Claude Code agents and MCP-connected tooling.

September 2025

Jailbreak and launch

Attacks are split into small tasks that look innocent, with a fake "defensive testing" persona, to bypass the model's guardrails.

September 2025

Automated operations

Agents scan IP ranges, find vulnerabilities, map high-value databases, harvest credentials, stage data, and document everything in generated markdown files.

Mid-September 2025

Detection by the vendor

Anthropic's behavioral classifiers flag the misuse pattern amid the agent activity.

Following ten days

Containment

Anthropic maps the operation, bans accounts as they are identified, notifies affected entities, and coordinates with authorities.

13 November 2025

Public disclosure

Anthropic publishes the campaign report; ATT&CK later records it as campaign C0062.

Attack chain

  1. Human operators choose targets and prepare the framework.
  2. Agents perform active scanning of IP blocks and vulnerability scanning.
  3. Agents enumerate accounts and find high-privilege users in databases.
  4. Agents exploit services, harvest credentials, and create a local backdoor account.
  5. Agents collect and stage data, writing generated documentation as they go.
  6. Operators approve key steps; exfiltration — the final step of copying stolen data out of the network — begins from staged data.

Detection point

The campaign was caught by behavioral analysis of how the AI accounts were used, not by a content filter. The victim-side lesson is the same: the attacker's content generation is invisible to you. Your signals are the downstream effects: sudden scanning, unusual database enumeration, staging directories full of neat markdown files, and unexpected local account creation.

ATT&CK records the campaign's techniques: T1595.001/002, T1087, T1136.001, T1213.006, T1005, T1074.001, and more.

Containment and eradication

Anthropic banned the accounts as they surfaced and worked with security partners to notify victims. For affected organizations, eradication meant rotating every credential the agents could have harvested, removing backdoor accounts, and closing the exploited service paths. Because the agents documented their own work, victims also had to treat the staged notes as evidence of exactly what had been taken.

Recovery and impact

Only a small number of intrusions succeeded, and data theft was limited before the operation was shut down, but the full victim list and loss scope were never published. The larger impact was structural: the industry's first clear proof that a full intrusion chain could run mostly on AI agents, with human operators reduced to approvals.

Team response and communication

The response mixed vendor action, victim notification, and authority coordination. Communication was careful: high-confidence attribution was published with clear limits, no premature IOCs, and no overstated claims. Enterprise teams should copy that pattern: say what you know, what you verified, and what you cannot confirm.

Lessons learned

  • Expect AI-orchestrated intrusions to move faster than human-paced alert review; automate triage where you can.
  • Watch for machine-generated artifacts: uniform documentation, staging files, and repetitive agent patterns.
  • Rotate credentials broadly after any automated intrusion; assume bulk harvesting.
  • Track the T1683 technique family and its campaigns (C0062, C0064) in your intel program.
  • Set clear, honest communication rules for AI-related incidents before you need them.

Mitigation and Defense

MITRE assigns only one mitigation to this technique: M1056 Pre-compromise, because the generation happens on the attacker's side. Real defense therefore hardens the stages where generated content lands. The controls below are ordered by effect.

Enterprise controls

Policy and governance

Write an AI acceptable-use policy that covers both using AI and receiving AI-generated content. Add an out-of-band verification policy for payments and credential changes. Under it, any transfer, payroll change, or MFA reset is confirmed on a known phone number or in person, never through the channel that made the request. Keep the rules short enough that people actually follow them.

Identity and access

Deploy phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 keys or passkeys) for finance, executives, and administrators. Give the help desk a written verification script with a cooling-off period for MFA resets, and monitor MFA re-registrations in the IdP. Review new-hire identity proofing with HR: documents should be verified, references called on known numbers.

Email security

Enforce DMARC at p=reject for your domains, quarantine first-contact external mail with exec-style display names, and banner external senders. Treat domain age and sender history as first-class signals in gateway rules, because content analysis alone cannot catch fluent AI text.

Endpoint and data controls

Run EDR everywhere and forward its telemetry to the SIEM. Apply DLP to AI egress points: proxy, CASB, and approved AI services. Block unapproved AI binaries on managed hosts, and alert on synthetic media tooling installation (see the detections in 5.5).

Cloud, SaaS, and AI governance

Keep an AI tool allowlist with named owners. Ingest audit logs from meeting platforms, HR and recruiting SaaS, and collaboration tools. Require provenance (proof of where a media file came from) or disclosure labels for media your organization publishes, aligned with EU AI Act Article 50 practice.

Network and brand monitoring

Filter DNS against newly registered domains and watch Certificate Transparency for brand look-alikes. Register defensive domains for common phishing keywords. Alert on your brand appearing in fresh certificates (detection TH-05).

Payment controls and fraud response

Require dual approval for payments and beneficiary changes, with hard limits that no single person can override. Build a fraud runbook that includes immediate bank recall attempts, account freezes, and police and ISAC notification. In deepfake fraud, minutes decide whether money comes back.

People and training

Train for the new tells: pressure, urgency, and verification shortcuts, not spelling errors. Run a deepfake tabletop with finance leadership. Make reporting one click, thank every reporter, and share what the report caused. A workforce that reports fast beats any filter.

Home and small-office advice

Families and small teams are targeted with the same methods: cloned voices of relatives, fake job offers, and urgent payment requests. Agree on a family or team code word for urgent requests. Call back on the number you already know, never the one given in the message. Limit public video and audio of leadership (and children) on social media, because that is training data for voice and face clones. Turn on MFA everywhere, enable bank transfer alerts, and slow down: real organizations accept a callback.
