T1657 Financial Theft

Financial Theft is the end goal of many intrusions: wires redirected, payroll diverted, crypto wallets emptied, or a ransom demanded. By the time the money moves, the attacker has usually been inside for weeks. This guide teaches you how to see the theft coming — and how to stop it.

Tactic: Impact Impact type: Availability Platforms: Windows · macOS · Linux · SaaS · Office Suite Version 1.2 No sub-techniques Guide v2.0 · audited

What it is

Stealing money itself: fraud, extortion, and abuse of payment systems, not just data theft.

Main risk

Direct money loss plus fraud liability. Wires and crypto transfers are often irreversible.

Where it happens

Email platforms, finance and ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) SaaS tools, banking endpoints, and crypto wallets.

Detection difficulty

High. Money moves quietly. Strong controls need both process rules and telemetry.

Overview

What Financial Theft means in MITRE ATT&CK, why it sits at the end of most attack chains, and what this guide will teach you.

What is T1657?

MITRE ATT&CK is a public knowledge base that groups real attacker behavior into techniques. T1657 — Financial Theft describes adversaries who steal monetary resources for financial gain. They may use extortion, social engineering, technical theft, or other methods. The technique is listed under the Impact tactic, which is usually the last stage of an attack.

The official ATT&CK entry names several campaign types:

  • Ransomware extortion — encrypt files, threaten to publish stolen data, demand payment.
  • Business Email Compromise (BEC) and fraud — take over or fake a trusted mailbox and trick staff into sending money.
  • “Pig butchering” — a long-con investment scam that ends with the victim sending funds to the attacker.
  • Bank hacking — break into bank payment systems and move money out directly.
  • Cryptocurrency abuse — steal wallets, seed phrases, or crypto-exchange accounts.

Attackers may also compromise accounts to make unauthorized transfers. BEC uses impersonation of trusted people and vendors, so the victim is deceived into sending money to attacker-controlled accounts. One more warning from ATT&CK: fear of theft can be a decoy. Some actors stage fake fraud to hide pure disruption, such as data destruction.

Note ATT&CK lists the impact type of T1657 as Availability. That may look strange for theft. The idea is simple: when money is taken away, the victim loses a resource. Money that leaves an account is no longer available to its owner.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Financial theft is rarely the first step. A typical chain looks like this:

  1. Get in — phishing or stolen credentials (for example T1566 Phishing).
  2. Stay and watch — read email, learn the payment process (for example T1114 Email Collection).
  3. Prepare — change settings, add rules or accounts (for example T1098 Account Manipulation).
  4. Take the money — T1657 Financial Theft. This is the moment that matters most to the attacker.
  5. Cash out — launder funds through mule accounts, casinos, or crypto mixers.

T1657 is therefore the goal, not a single tool. Many other techniques exist to serve it. That is why detection teams watch the steps before the money moves. An inbox rule, a new payee, or a look-alike domain is easier to stop than a wire transfer that has already left the bank.

Frequently asked questions

Is T1657 only about banks?

No. Any organization that pays people or vendors is a target: schools, hospitals, manufacturers, charities. The money path is what matters, not the industry.

Is this the same as “data theft”?

No. Data theft steals information to sell or leak. Financial theft takes money itself, or forces a payment — ransom, fake invoice, diverted payroll. They often travel together, but the goal differs.

Why does ATT&CK put this under Impact?

ATT&CK groups by effect on the victim. Money taken away is a resource the victim no longer has, so ATT&CK records the impact type as Availability. The technique page also notes that fake fear of theft can be a decoy for pure disruption.

Can we just blame the user who clicked?

Please don’t. Every fraud in this guide needed a process gap — a single approver, an unverified bank change, an unmonitored log. Fix the gap and the next phish fails too.

  3. Step 3 · Hidden inbox rule buries invoices. The attacker creates an inbox rule that moves any mail with words like “invoice”, “payment”, or “bank” into a hidden folder. The clerk stops seeing vendor emails. Evidence: a New-InboxRule event in the Microsoft 365 Unified Audit Log (UAL).
  4. Step 4 · Weeks of silent thread reading. The attacker reads real invoice threads and learns names, amounts, tone, and timing. They never send anything yet. Evidence: MailItemsAccessed audit events at unusual hours from the VPS IP.
  5. Step 5 · Look-alike domain and fake bank details. The attacker registers a look-alike domain and emails the treasury team from it, posing as the vendor. The message says the vendor has a new bank account and repeats details from the real thread. Evidence: a DNS query for the look-alike domain, and an email from a newly registered sender domain.
  6. Step 6 · Treasury approves and executes the wire. Treasury compares the mail thread, sees no reason to doubt it, and pays the invoice to the “new” account. The bank confirms the debit. Evidence: a new payee record and a first-time payment that beat the fraud checks.

  8. Step 8 · Recall and response. The SOC calls the bank fraud line to request a recall, removes the inbox rule, resets the clerk’s credentials, blocks the look-alike domain, and re-verifies the vendor’s real bank details by phone. Evidence: recall ticket, rule deletion event, credential reset, and a proxy block for the domain.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains, at a conceptual level, how attackers think about stealing money — so you can predict their moves. It stays defensive: no working fraud instructions, only what a defender or an ethical tester needs to know.

Danger Financial theft is a crime everywhere. Real fraud operations harm real people and companies. Everything here is educational and defensive. Any command example later in this guide is for a lab you own, and the goal is always to build and test detections.

Why attackers chase money

Most financially motivated groups measure success in one number: money moved. Data theft needs a buyer. Ransomware needs negotiation. Stolen money is the payoff itself. This shapes their planning in three ways:

  • Patience. They may stay quiet for weeks, reading email and learning the payment process, before doing anything loud.
  • Timing. They act just before holidays, weekends, or quarter close, when approvers are busy and banks are slower to react.
  • Pressure. They copy the tone of real threads, use real invoice numbers, and create urgency so nobody double-checks.

Playbook A — BEC invoice fraud

Steal or spoof a finance mailbox, watch real invoice threads, then send “updated bank details” from a look-alike domain. The victim sends the wire believing it is routine. This is the most common path and the one shown in the simulation above.

Playbook B — Payment-rail injection

Compromise the small set of endpoints or accounts that talk to a payment system, then submit fraudulent transfers directly. This is the bank-heist pattern seen in the APT38 case study in section 6. It needs deep access, so it is rare but very costly.

Playbook C — Payroll diversion

Take over an employee’s HR self-service account, change the bank account for direct deposit, and wait for the next pay run. Quiet, repetitive, and painful for every affected employee.

Playbook D — Crypto wallet hijack

Trick a user into installing a fake wallet app or a malicious browser extension, or swap copied wallet addresses on the clipboard. Transfers on public blockchains cannot be recalled, so this path is final.

Playbook E — ATM cash-out

After compromising card-processing systems, coordinate withdrawals from many ATMs in a short window (“jackpotting” era tactics reported in the Carbanak/Cobalt Group cases). The money is gone in hours.

Playbook F — Vendor master-data fraud

Instead of faking an email, change the vendor’s bank details inside the ERP or procurement system itself. Every following payment to that vendor goes to the attacker. No spoofed email is ever needed.

Prerequisites an attacker needs

  • Access. A stolen mailbox or portal session is enough for BEC. Payment systems need far more privileged access.
  • Knowledge of the process. Who approves payments? What does a real invoice look like? This comes from reading email or documents.
  • An unmonitored moment. A single-approver process, a busy Friday, or a gap between the finance app and the SIEM.
  • A cash-out path. Mule accounts, crypto addresses, or a cooperating bank insider. Defenders cannot see this part, which is why recall speed matters.

Common variations and attacker mistakes

  • Variation: instead of a look-alike domain, the attacker replies inside the real thread from the compromised mailbox itself. Harder to spot, but it leaves mailbox-audit evidence.
  • Variation: split one large fraud into many small payments to stay under approval limits.
  • Mistake: creating the inbox rule from a residential or data-center IP never seen before — a loud signal on day one.
  • Mistake: reusing the same look-alike domain or wallet address across victims, which lets researchers link campaigns.
  • Mistake: sending the fake bank-details email before any real invoice thread exists, so the story does not match reality.

Timing and psychology

Attackers choose moments when verification is weakest. Examples: Friday afternoons, the last day of a quarter, the first day back from holidays, or the week a real vendor says it is switching banks. They copy real numbers, real signatures, and real urgency. A useful defensive habit: ask “what would make this request feel normal?” and then make sure normal is verified anyway.

Variations by industry

  • Healthcare: payroll diversion plus supplier fraud on medical consumables, where urgent orders are normal.
  • Manufacturing: fake bank-detail changes on long supplier lists; one change hits dozens of purchase orders.
  • Nonprofits: donation redirection and grant-payment fraud, where small teams approve everything.
  • Schools and universities: tuition refund diversion through the student finance portal.

What ethical testers may do instead

An authorized red-team engagement can test the detection and process layers without committing fraud. It may send approved phishing simulations, create rule events in a lab tenant, or run a fake “new payee” request through treasury with prior sign-off. The test target is the control, never the money.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

What defenders actually see, which logs matter, and a seven-step triage flow you can follow when a finance-related alert fires.

How theft looks from the SOC

No log line ever says “money stolen”. Instead, defenders see small, boring-looking events: a new inbox rule, a sign-in from a new country, a changed bank account, a fresh domain lookup. Each one alone can be normal. The craft is in joining them. By the time a payment is flagged by a bank, the money is often already gone — so the SOC’s real advantage is the days or weeks before the wire.

Artifacts financial theft leaves

  • Inbox rules that move or delete mail with finance keywords.
  • Mailbox forwarding to unknown external addresses.
  • Sign-ins from new IPs, countries, or user agents on finance accounts.
  • New OAuth consent grants for unknown mail or finance apps.
  • New payees or changed bank details in ERP, HR, or payment systems.
  • DNS or proxy hits on look-alike domains of vendors and banks.
  • Browser extensions or clipboard tools installed on finance endpoints.

Where to look

  • Email: Microsoft 365 Unified Audit Log, Exchange message trace, Entra ID (Azure AD) sign-in risk logs.
  • Finance apps: ERP, payroll, and procurement audit exports (NetSuite, Workday, SAP or similar).
  • Banking: payment-platform session logs, SWIFT-related logs where applicable, ATM switch monitoring.
  • Network: DNS resolver and proxy logs for new or look-alike domains.
  • Endpoints: EDR console for extensions, clipboard monitors, and script hosts on finance machines.

Why it matters, and where it sits in the chain

Before the theft you will usually find phishing (T1566), account use (T1078-style credential abuse), and email collection (T1114). After the theft comes cash-out, which you rarely see. That means your best detections sit in the middle of the chain — the quiet preparation steps. If you can catch an inbox rule or a new payee on day one, the theft never happens.

Triage flow: finance fraud alert

  1. Confirm the alert scope. Which user, mailbox, app, or payee? Is it finance-related at all? Pull the raw event, not just the alert summary.
  2. Check the identity. Sign-in history for the account in the last 30 days: new IP, new country, new device, MFA (multi-factor authentication) changes?
  3. Look for the pair. An inbox rule plus a sign-in risk event? A new payee plus a look-alike domain query? Search for the companion signal before you judge severity.
  4. Check the money path. For payee changes: who changed it, when, from where, and was it verified? For payments: is it still pending? Pending wires can be recalled; settled ones usually cannot.
  5. Contain first, ask later. If two or more signals align, disable the rule, reset credentials, revoke sessions, and freeze the pending payment — in that order.
  6. Call the bank fast. Use the fraud-line number from your runbook (never from the suspicious email). Minutes matter for recalls.
  7. Write it down. Record timeline, evidence, and control gaps, then feed them back into detections and process rules.
Good practice Keep a printed or offline fraud runbook: bank fraud-line numbers, insurance contact, the IC3 (Internet Crime Complaint Center) reporting link, and legal contacts. In a real incident nobody should be searching for a phone number at 6 p.m. on a Friday.

Four small panels answer most T1657 questions without any new data source. Each one is a search from section 5.5 with a chart or table on top.

  • Finance mailbox tape — every rule change, forwarding change, and risky sign-in for finance-tagged identities, one timeline per user.

  • Payment context strip — for every payment-gateway logon, the approval reference and PAM justification next to it; empty fields glow here.

If a panel stays empty for a week, the feed is broken — fix feeds before you tune rules.

Threat intelligence

Sources and collection

  • MITRE ATT&CK — the technique page plus related ATT&CK groups and software entries give verified actor-to-behavior links.
  • Vendor threat reports — blogs from security vendors describe new BEC kits, crypto drainers, and campaign infrastructure.
  • OSINT — public alerts from the FBI’s IC3, FinCEN advisories, and FS-ISAC (Financial Services Information Sharing and Analysis Center) bulletins.
  • Commercial feeds — indicators for look-alike domains, mule accounts, and drainer wallets.
  • Internal telemetry — your own fraud tickets, chargebacks, and help-desk reports. This is the highest-signal source you own.

From intel to detection: a worked example

Here is the loop end to end. Public warnings from the FBI’s IC3 have repeatedly described fraud waves that use fake “new banking information” emails sent from look-alike vendor domains around holiday weeks.

  1. Collect. You read the IC3 alert on Monday morning (five minutes).
  2. Decide. Does the behavior apply to us? We pay vendors weekly — yes (ten minutes with finance).
  3. Build. You already log DNS queries. You add a rule. Alert when a query name imitates one of your top 100 vendor or bank domains (a 1–2 letter change), and the domain is younger than 14 days (see the DNS samples in section 5.4).
  4. Test. You replay the fictional DNS sample from section 5.4 into the pipeline and confirm the alert fires with the right fields.
  5. Review. After a week: 3 alerts, all SaaS pivot domains, 1 true look-alike that marketing wanted registered anyway. You tune the vendor list, keep the rule, and add the weekly review step from section 8.

Total cost: under two hours. That is what “intel-driven detection” means in practice — a small, honest loop, not a dashboard of feeds nobody reads.

Worked example — from a warning to a rule in one morning

Here is the loop with real words. The IC3 warning describes fake “new banking information” emails sent from look-alike vendor domains around holiday weeks. You run the five steps before lunch.

  1. Extract the behavior. A domain that imitates a known vendor sends payment-detail emails in the days before a holiday.
  2. Map it. The fraud is T1657; the delivery may be T1566 phishing or T1656 impersonation. Your detection point is the DNS query.

TH-01 — BEC invoice fraud Critical

Description. An attacker takes over (or spoofs) a finance mailbox, watches real invoice threads, then sends “updated bank details” from a look-alike domain. Treasury pays a real invoice to a fake account.

Preconditions. Mailbox compromise or domain spoofing; a payment process with a single approver; no out-of-band verification of bank-detail changes.

Impact. Irreversible wire loss, often the full invoice amount. Recovery depends on bank recall speed, which is measured in hours.

Why Critical. Highest frequency of all six threats, near-zero technical skill needed, and direct money loss. Public IC3 reporting puts BEC losses in the billions of dollars per year.

TH-02 — Payment-rail injection Critical

Description. Attackers compromise the small set of endpoints or accounts that operate a payment or SWIFT interface, then submit fraudulent transfer messages themselves (the APT38 pattern).

Preconditions. Weeks of quiet access to the payment environment; weak segregation between payment terminals and the office LAN; operator credentials.

Impact. Direct theft from the institution’s own accounts; losses can reach tens or hundreds of millions.

Why Critical. Lowest frequency, highest single-impact. The Bangladesh Bank case (section 6) shows the full cost when detection depends on luck.

TH-03 — Payroll diversion High

Description. An attacker compromises an employee’s HR self-service portal account, changes the direct-deposit bank account, and waits for the next payroll cycle.

Preconditions. Credential phishing or password reuse; a payroll portal reachable from anywhere without risk-based sign-in checks.

Impact. Stolen wages per affected employee, repeated each cycle until noticed; HR trust damage.

Why High. Very common, easy to attempt at scale, but amounts per victim are smaller than BEC and often recoverable from payroll reserves.

TH-04 — Crypto wallet hijack High

Description. Fake wallet apps, malicious browser extensions (the BeaverTail / Contagious Interview pattern), or clipboard-swapping tools redirect crypto payments or steal seed phrases.

Preconditions. A user who handles crypto; a download from an untrusted source; endpoint policy that allows arbitrary extensions or apps.

Impact. Wallets emptied; blockchain transfers are final, with no recall path at all.

Why High. Active campaigns target job applicants and crypto staff; irreversibility makes any success total for the victim.

TH-05 — ATM cash-out High

Description. After compromising card-processing or switch systems, attackers raise card limits and coordinate withdrawals from many ATMs within minutes (the Carbanak-era pattern).

Preconditions. Access to authorization systems or switch management; knowledge of withdrawal limits and monitoring windows.

Impact. Millions gone in hours; physical cash, so almost nothing is recoverable.

Why High not Critical. Rare outside financial institutions, but if you operate card infrastructure this is a top-three threat. Rate it Critical for banks, High for the general model.

TH-06 — Vendor master-data fraud Medium

Description. A procurement or ERP account is compromised and the vendor’s bank details are changed in the master data itself. All future payments flow to the attacker — no spoofed email needed.

Preconditions. ERP access for procurement users; change workflow without callback verification; ERP audit logs not monitored.

Impact. Repeated loss over multiple invoices until the vendor complains about missing payment.

Why Medium. Requires ERP access and a change window, so it is less common than BEC; but because payments repeat, total loss can still be large.

Logs and patterns

For each threat, this section gives you: where to look, which fields matter, what normal and suspicious look like, and the detection logic in plain words. You also get three fake sample events to test your pipeline. All timestamps use UTC.

TH-01 — BEC invoice fraud

Log sources and fields

  • Microsoft 365 Unified Audit Log (UAL) — operations New-InboxRule, UpdateInboxRules, MailItemsAccessed, Set-Mailbox. Fields: UserId, ClientIP, Parameters. Note: MailItemsAccessed requires Exchange Online Audit (Premium) — E5/G5 or the add-on license; on E3 you will not see it.
  • Entra ID (Azure AD) sign-in logs — fields: userPrincipalName, ipAddress, location, riskLevel.
  • Message trace — sender domains and SPF results for outbound finance mail.

UAL operations cheat sheet

OperationWhat it meansWhy TH-01 cares
New-InboxRuleA mail rule was createdHiding vendor or security mail is the classic prep step
UpdateInboxRulesA rule changed — including via Outlook on the web APIsAttackers edit existing rules to look normal

Add-MailboxPermissionSomeone granted mailbox accessA second identity reading finance mail

Operation names are case-sensitive in the UAL. If a search returns nothing, confirm the exact name on one real event before tuning the rule.

Normal vs suspicious, and logic

Normal. Rules that file newsletters; sign-ins from the office or corporate VPN; mail access during work hours.

Suspicious. A rule containing finance keywords; sign-in from a new country; mail reading at 03:00 from a hosting provider IP.

Detection logic. Alert when New-InboxRule parameters contain invoice, payment, bank, or wire — and the same user had a risky sign-in in the previous 7 days. Severity: high; correlate both events into one case.

Field notes: ClientIP in the UAL is the actor’s address — compare it with the sign-in log. OperationCount over hundreds in minutes means scripted bulk access, not a human reading mail. The rule name rss is a classic camouflage trick.

Benign look-alike · TH-01

The honest noise: a user files newsletters into a folder. The rule name is rss too — the camouflage trick cuts both ways. The quiet version has no finance keywords, and the user’s sign-in history is clean: office IP, no risky sign-in in the last week. This is why the TH-01 rule joins the keyword match with the sign-in risk instead of alerting on rules alone.

TH-02 — Payment-rail transaction injection

Log sources and fields

  • Windows Security log on payment/SWIFT servers — EventCode 4624 (logon), 4648 (explicit credentials). Fields: LogonType, TargetUserName, IpAddress, WorkstationName.
  • SWIFT gateway logs (organization-specific; many sites keep allwt-style audit trails) — message direction, reference, operator ID.
  • PAM (privileged access management) session logs — who opened a privileged session, from which jump host.

Normal vs suspicious, and logic

Normal. Operators reach payment servers only from the jump host, during change windows, with matching approval records.

Suspicious. RDP from any address except the bastion; a payment message with no approval ticket; gateway activity at 02:00 local time.

Detection logic. Alert on interactive or RDP logons to payment servers where the source is not the approved bastion list. Separately, join gateway messages to the approval system and alert on orphans.

Field notes: LogonType 10 is RDP — from anywhere except the bastion it is wrong by definition. approval_ref: null plus an empty PAM justification means the payment had no recorded approval, which is the strongest possible signal on this asset class.

Benign look-alike · TH-02

The honest noise: the payment operations service account signs in to a payment host inside its documented window, with a ticket reference and a PAM justification filled in. Same asset, same log — the difference is the context fields. This is why the rule time-bounds the window and requires the approval reference, not just the logon.

TH-03 — Payroll diversion

Log sources and fields

  • HR SaaS audit logs (Workday, ADP, SuccessFactors or similar; field names vary — see the mapping notes in 5.5) — action direct_deposit_change, actor, employee ID, masked old/new account.
  • IdP sign-in logs for the HR portal — new device, new ASN, MFA re-enrollment.
  • MFA factor-change events — a factor reset right before a bank change is a classic pair.

Normal vs suspicious, and logic

Normal. Employees update their own details around onboarding; changes come from the corporate network during work hours.

Suspicious. Bank change from a new device plus fresh MFA enrollment, days before pay run; several employees’ changes from one IP.

Detection logic. Alert when a direct-deposit change occurs within 10 days of a payroll date and the session had a new device or an MFA factor change in the prior 72 hours. Batch alert when one IP changes two or more employees.

Field notes: an empty deviceId means “never seen this device”. A python-requests user agent on an HR portal is scripting, not an employee typing. The three events line up into one story within 13 minutes.

Benign look-alike · TH-03

The honest noise: January enrollment season, when many employees update direct-deposit accounts on purpose. The quiet version comes from the office network or the corporate VPN, from a real browser session with a registered device, and the HR ticket wave matches the enrollment calendar. Your tuning lever: expect a seasonal bump, keep the new-device and scripting conditions strict, and never exempt the change event itself — only the noise around it.

TH-04 — Crypto wallet hijack

Log sources and fields

  • Sysmon / EDR process events — EventCode 1: Image, CommandLine, User, Hashes.
  • Browser management logs — extension installs: extension_id, extension_name, requested_permissions.
  • DNS and proxy logs — queries to freshly registered drainer domains.

Normal vs suspicious, and logic

Normal. Approved wallet software signed by a known vendor, installed by IT; standard browser extensions.

Suspicious. An unsigned exe with wallet-related names in %TEMP%; an extension asking to “read and change all data on websites” plus clipboard access; a script host spawning from AppData.

Detection logic. Alert when a process image or extension name matches wallet/metamask-like strings and the path is user-writable. Tune with a signed-vendor allowlist (see 5.5, TH-04) because generic keyword matching is noisy by nature.

Field notes: Signed=false plus a Temp path is the core pair. The extension permission list requests clipboard reading — exactly what clipboard-swap theft needs. A one-day-old domain contacted seconds after install suggests the payload also contacts the attacker's server.

Benign look-alike · TH-04

The honest noise: finance and developer staff do install real wallet software — MetaMask, exchange tools, portfolio trackers. The quiet version has three tells: the binary is digitally signed by a known vendor, it runs from Program Files or the user’s chosen install path with a setup history, and the extension requests no clipboard permission. The rule’s core pair — unsigned binary from a Temp path plus clipboard-reading permission — separates almost all of this noise.

TH-05 — ATM cash-out coordination

Log sources and fields

  • ATM switch / authorization host logs — terminal_id, action, response_code, amount, timestamp.
  • Electronic journal (EJ) files from terminals — per-withdrawal records.
  • Network flow logs from switch management — unexpected sessions to non-partner IPs.

Normal vs suspicious, and logic

Normal. A busy terminal handles maybe 5–10 withdrawals per hour; limits hold; partner IPs are stable.

Suspicious. More than 20 approvals on one terminal in 10 minutes; withdrawals on the same card in two cities within an hour; new outbound sessions from the switch host.

Detection logic. Count approved withdrawal authorizations per terminal in a 10-minute window; alert above 20. Add a second rule for same-card geography jumps (“impossible travel” for cards).

Field notes: two approvals 33 seconds apart on one terminal is normal; hundreds per hour is not — the rule counts, not the single line. The limit change with approval=NONE from a batch service account is the preparation step that makes the burst possible.

Benign look-alike · TH-05

The honest noise: payday and holiday weekends push real withdrawal counts up, and a shopping-mall bank of terminals can legitimately process a burst. The quiet version stays under the threshold, spreads across many terminals and many cards, and matches the branch’s cash-demand pattern. Tune with a per-terminal baseline rather than one global number, and re-baseline before holidays on purpose.

TH-06 — Vendor master-data fraud

Log sources and fields

  • ERP audit log (SAP, Oracle, NetSuite or similar; assume a generic erp:audit feed) — action, actor, vendor ID, old/new bank fields.
  • Approval workflow records — ticket ID, callback evidence, approver.
  • Email and DNS logs — a change request from a look-alike domain one or two days earlier.

Normal vs suspicious, and logic

Normal. Changes inside the approved change window, with a ticket and a documented callback to the vendor’s known number.

Suspicious. A bank-detail change with no ticket; an actor who never touches vendor data; a look-alike domain email shortly before.

Detection logic. Alert on any supplier_bank_details_update outside the maintenance window or without an approval reference; enrich with look-alike domain queries in the prior 48 hours.

Field notes: the ERP change and the workflow lookup come from the same user two minutes apart — the user checked that no ticket existed, which shows intent. The look-alike query the day before is the third link in the chain.

Benign look-alike · TH-06

The honest noise: vendors really do change banks. The quiet version runs through the workflow tool with a callback record attached — who called, which contract number, when — and it does not cluster with look-alike domain queries or unusual mail activity. If your workflow tool is the only path for such changes, its audit log is your best evidence: a change with no workflow record is a finding by itself.

TH-01 — BEC inbox rule on finance mail

TH-02 — Unapproved logon to payment infrastructure

Mapping note. Maps to Windows Security event 4624 on the payment servers themselves (collect with Windows Event Forwarding or an agent). LogonType 2 is console, 10 is RDP. Severity: critical on this asset class — page immediately. Join with PAM session records to see whether the session had a ticket.

Assumptions. Windows Security events via the Splunk Add-on for Windows with CIM names (Logon_Type, src_ip, dest) in index=wineventlog; payment hosts are named SWIFT-GW* or PAY-* — rename to your convention. Keep the bastion allowlist in a lookup table in production.

TH-03 — Payroll direct-deposit change

Mapping note. product: hr_saas is a generic category: in reality map it to your vendor’s audit feed (Workday “Direct Deposit Change” business-process events, ADP or SuccessFactors equivalents) and rename the action values to their real strings. Severity: high; raise to critical when a batch of employees changes from one IP.

Assumptions. HR audit data in index=hr with sourcetype=workday:audit (rename for your vendor). The query ends with a batch view: one row per source IP that touched two or more employees. Delete the final two lines to see every change, and schedule both variants — the single-change view for triage, the batch view for campaigns. Time-bound it to the 10 days before a pay run for a tighter rule.

TH-04 — Wallet-related process or extension

Mapping note. Maps to Sysmon event 1 (process creation) collected into your SIEM; with an EDR platform, map Image to the process path field instead. Severity: medium by design — keyword matching on names is noisy, so treat it as a hunt query first and promote to high once allowlists are stable. Pair with browser-extension install events from the same host within minutes.

Assumptions. Sysmon data in index=sysmon with the raw XML sourcetype shown; if you normalize to CIM, use index=edr with process_path and process_name fields instead of Image. Tune the regex to your environment and add a signed-vendor allowlist lookup to cut developer noise.

TH-05 — ATM authorization burst

Mapping note. product: atm_switch is a placeholder category for your switch or authorization platform (Postilion-style auth logs, EJ feeds). The aggregation counts per terminal inside the 10-minute timeframe — the burst shape is the signal, not any single line. Severity: critical for card issuers and processors.

Assumptions. Switch authorization records in index=payments with sourcetype=atm:auth; field names like terminal_id, card, amount, resp are invented stand-ins for your platform’s real names. Add a companion rule: same card, two or more terminals in different cities within 60 minutes.

TH-06 — Vendor bank-details change in ERP

Mapping note. product: erp is generic — map it to SAP change documents, Oracle or NetSuite audit exports, and rename action values to your system’s real ones. Severity: high; a first-time change on a vendor that was just contacted via a look-alike domain (see the 5.4 sample) is effectively critical.

Assumptions. ERP audit export in index=finance with sourcetype=erp:audit; old_value/new_value hold masked bank fields (mask IBANs in the pipeline to keep logs safe). Enrich in production: join vendor_id to a lookup of vendors whose invoices arrived in the last 7 days.

How to test each rule before it goes live

Every rule in this section can be tested with the sample events you already have. The loop takes minutes and saves hours of tuning later.

Real-World Case Study

The Bangladesh Bank SWIFT heist of February 2016 is the most instructive public T1657 incident. It is documented in court records, central-bank reports, and extensive public journalism. This section uses only publicly reported facts.

Case note Publicly documented incident — not composite. Attribution to the Lazarus Group / APT38 is per the United States Department of Justice indictment (September 2018), so we label it High confidence. Amounts are as publicly reported: 35 fraudulent transfer requests worth about $951 million were submitted; about $81 million was actually stolen and largely never recovered.

Context

Bangladesh Bank, the country’s central bank, uses the SWIFT financial messaging network to move billions of dollars in reserves. SWIFT is not a bank itself — it is a messaging system banks use to instruct each other to move money. The bank’s SWIFT terminals sat on a network that was only weakly separated from its general office LAN. Attackers spent weeks or months inside that network, learning how payments were made, when staff worked, and how the SWIFT software behaved. That patience is the signature of this technique.

Timeline

Months before — 2015

Attackers gain a foothold in the bank’s network and move quietly between systems. They study SWIFT operations, payment routines, and staff schedules. No money moves, and no alarm sounds.

January – early February 2016

Malware is deployed on the SWIFT payment environment. It changes the SWIFT software and its database to hide the fraudulent messages. It also deletes the printer output that normally confirms each message. That printout was the operators’ everyday proof that transfers exist.

Thursday 4 – Friday 5 February 2016

It is a Friday — the weekend in Bangladesh, while New York is open. Attackers use the SWIFT messaging interface to submit 35 fraudulent transfer requests totaling about $951 million, aimed mostly at accounts in Southeast Asia.

Friday 5 February 2016

Most requests are stopped, but $81 million succeeds: it lands in four accounts at Rizal Commercial Banking Corporation (RCBC) in the Philippines. Within days it is funnelled through casinos and junket operators, converting it into cash and chips — a classic laundering path.

Friday 5 – Monday 8 February 2016

One request is flagged by an intermediary bank partly because of a spelling error — the beneficiary address misspells “Jupiter” (as in Jupiter Street in Manila). The stop in New York then blocks the remaining requests — roughly $850 million that would otherwise have left.

Monday 8 February 2016 onward

Bank staff find the SWIFT system behaving strangely: messages and confirmations are missing, the printer queue is silent. Because the malware deleted the normal evidence, understanding what happened takes days. The theft is only pieced together later.

February – May 2016

Forensic teams investigate; SWIFT warns all customers and later launches its Customer Security Programme with mandatory controls. Bangladesh Bank’s governor resigns in March. The Philippine central bank fines RCBC heavily; Senate hearings follow.

September 2018 and after

The US Department of Justice indicts a North Korean programmer for the heist, confirming the attribution picture. Only a small fraction of the stolen $81 million is ever returned; most is gone for good.

Attack chain

  1. Get in. Foothold in the office network (public reporting points to phishing and lateral movement over months).
  2. Learn. Study SWIFT operations: who sends transfers, when, and what normal looks like.
  3. Prepare. Deploy malware that manipulates the SWIFT platform and its printer/database outputs so fraudulent activity leaves no visible trace locally.
  4. Execute. Submit 35 transfer requests on a Friday, when local staff are away but the US legs of the payment system are open.
  5. Launder. Move the $81M through RCBC accounts into casinos and junket operators within days.

Detection point

The honest lesson: the scheme was caught by accident. A spelling mistake in one beneficiary address made an intermediary bank suspicious, and a stop in New York froze the rest. The bank’s own monitoring did not catch the theft; worse, the malware hid the usual confirmations, so discovery was delayed by days. Log review would have revealed thousands of lines of tampering — nobody was reviewing them in real time, and the logs themselves were not tamper-evident.

Containment

  • Recall and freeze requests went to RCBC, the Philippine central bank, and intermediary banks. The requests partly worked for blocked funds. They came too late for the $81 million already in the casino pipeline.
  • SWIFT-related credentials and infrastructure were reset, and the affected payment systems were isolated from the rest of the network.
  • Cross-border coordination followed, and some casino-side funds were later frozen in the Philippines.

Eradication

  • The payment environment was rebuilt; attacker malware and tooling were removed and the SWIFT terminals were hardened and re-installed from trusted media.
  • Access to payment systems was re-architected: stricter segregation from the office LAN, dedicated credentials, and better change control.

Recovery

Only a small part of the stolen $81 million was recovered — a few million dollars returned via casino and junket-operator pressure. Roughly $850 million in further requests never left, because of the accidental stop. In fraud terms, blocked beats recalled, which beats never noticed.

Impact and consequences

  • Direct loss: about $81 million, mostly never recovered.
  • People: the central bank governor resigned; RCBC staff faced charges and a large fine from the Philippine regulator.
  • Industry: SWIFT launched its Customer Security Programme, making security controls mandatory for all member institutions.
  • Reputation: years of hearings, lawsuits, and reporting — a permanent reference case in fraud and security teaching.

Team response and communication

Response was slow and painful, which is itself instructive. The malware had destroyed the local evidence trail, so early internal communication was confused: staff could not even prove which messages had been sent. Coordination reached across the US Federal Reserve, SWIFT, Philippine banks, and regulators — across time zones and legal systems. Public statements came before full facts were known, which amplified reputational damage. A modern incident team would recognize three failures: no tamper-evident logging, no rehearsed fraud runbook with bank contacts, and no pre-agreed cross-border communication plan.

Lessons learned

  • Segment payment infrastructure. SWIFT terminals should not live on the same network as office PCs.
  • Verify out of band. Payment instructions and account changes need callback verification to known numbers and dual approval.
  • Make logs tamper-evident. Ship payment and printer logs off-host in real time; WORM-style storage beats local files the attacker can delete.
  • Monitor payment anomalies. Volume, timing, beneficiary, and approval-matching rules should have caught 35 requests on a Friday.
  • Staff the calendar. Weekends and holidays are attack windows; monitoring and approver coverage must match.
  • Rehearse the recall. Know the bank fraud-line, the recall process, and the regulator contacts before you need them.
Good practice The useful reframe: this heist needed both technical access and process gaps. Either strong segmentation of payment systems or a simple anomaly rule on Friday transfer volume would likely have stopped it. Defense in depth is not a slogan — it is how one weak layer survives.

What changed in banking after 2016

  • SWIFT Customer Security Programme (CSP): mandatory security controls for all SWIFT users, with annual attestation — segmentation, access control, and logging of the payment environment are now required, not optional.
  • Independent assessment: CSP controls are checked by assessors, which pushed banks to fund payment-environment security they had deferred for years.
  • Industry shift: fraud and cyber teams were merged in many institutions; payment anomaly monitoring became a first-class SIEM use case.
  • Regulatory tailwind: later frameworks (NIS2, DORA) turned much of this into explicit legal duty for financial entities in Europe.

Detect it like a modern SOC

Map the 2016 events to today’s detections. Watch for logons to payment hosts that skip the bastion (TH-02 rule in 5.5). Watch for payment messages with no approval reference, and for gateway activity at night. Ship gateway and printer logs off-host right away, so an attacker cannot delete them quietly. The heist needed months of quiet access — every one of those months is a chance to catch it now.

What worked, what failed

What worked

  • A spelling error in one beneficiary name raised suspicion at a New York intermediary — the accidental control that stopped about $850 million.
  • Cross-border cooperation eventually traced the money path through the casinos and supported indictments.
  • SWIFT responded industry-wide with mandatory control requirements, raising the floor for every bank after 2016.

What failed

  • Payment infrastructure sat on a weakly segregated network — one compromise reached the SWIFT terminals.
  • Logs existed but nobody watched them in real time, and the malware could hide and delete the local trail.
  • Verification of payment instructions relied on the same compromised channel — there was no out-of-band check.
  • The fraud runbook did not exist: bank contacts, recall steps, and communication plans were invented mid-crisis.

Evidence to preserve early

Money moves fast and evidence fades faster. Before cleanup starts, export and store offline: the full mailbox audit and UAL export for every affected identity; the message trace for the fraud window; the ERP change history for the affected vendor or employee; the payment-gateway log with approval references; and the look-alike domain’s registration record (whois) if one was used. These five exports let auditors, the bank, and the insurer rebuild the story even after credentials are reset.

7

Mitigation and Defense

Controls that actually stop financial theft, arranged by area, then mapped to the six threats, plus home-office advice and a prioritized action list.

Identity and access

  • MFA (multi-factor authentication) everywhere, phishing-resistant for finance roles.
  • Conditional access: finance mailboxes and ERP sign in only from managed devices and expected countries.
  • Few standing privileged accounts on payment systems — use just-in-time access with tickets.

Payment process controls

  • Callback verification for any bank-detail change, using the vendor’s known number — never a number from the email.
  • Dual approval for wires and new payees; cooling-off period plus lower limits for first payments.
  • Approval-matching: every payment must reference an approval record, checked automatically.

Email platform

  • SPF, DKIM, and DMARC enforced; external-sender banners on; look-alike domain alerts.
  • Alert on inbox rules and auto-forwarding in finance mailboxes (see 5.5, TH-01).
  • Report-phish button wired to the SOC, with fast mailbox purge capability.

Logging and monitoring

  • Ingest ERP, HR SaaS, payroll, and payment-platform audit logs into the SIEM — unmonitored logs give you no protection.
  • Ship payment logs off-host in near real time so attackers cannot delete them (Bangladesh Bank lesson).
  • Correlate: inbox rule + risky sign-in; new payee + look-alike DNS; MFA reset + deposit change.

Network and endpoint

  • Segment payment/SWIFT infrastructure; only bastions may reach it; no direct RDP.
  • Register and monitor look-alike domains of your company, banks, and top vendors; block at proxy and DNS.
  • Extension allowlisting on browsers; block wallet-named executables in user-writable paths (see 5.5, TH-04).

Response readiness

  • Fraud runbook: bank fraud lines, insurance, legal, and IC3 (Internet Crime Complaint Center) contacts — offline copy.
  • Know your recall window: pending wires can be recalled, settled ones rarely. Speed decides.
  • Annual BEC and fraud tabletop with treasury, finance, and the SOC.

  • Verify any payment request through a channel you already know — call the person, don’t reply to the email.

  • Turn on MFA for banking, payroll, and email first — that is where the money is.

Vendor onboarding and change checklist (print and post next to the payment desk)

  • ☐ New vendor record created only with contract ID and tax ID on file.
  • ☐ Bank details verified by callback to a number from the signed contract — never from the email that requested them.
  • ☐ Change request recorded in the workflow tool with the callback date and who made the call.
  • ☐ Second approver reviews before the record is saved.
  • ☐ First payment to a new or changed account runs at a reduced limit with a second review.
  • ☐ Annual re-verification for all active vendors; lapsed vendors are blocked from payment.

The one drill that pays for this whole guide

Once a quarter, in a calm hour, run this 30-minute exercise with treasury and the SOC:

  1. Pick the last real vendor bank change and walk it backwards: workflow record, callback evidence, approver. Time it.
  2. Freeze one test payment in the banking platform and prove who can unfreeze it. Write down how long the freeze took.
  3. Call the bank’s fraud line from the runbook number and confirm the recall process is current — names and forms change.
  4. Fix one thing that surprised you before the next drill.

If the freeze takes more than 30 minutes, or the fraud-line contact is stale, you have found a real gap on a quiet day instead of during a fraud.

Money-mule red flags worth training finance to spot

  • A “vendor” whose bank sits in a country unrelated to its address or contract.
  • Urgency that appears exactly at closing windows, month ends, or just before holidays.
  • A request to split one invoice across several new accounts.
  • Bank-detail changes where the new account name differs from the vendor’s legal name.
  • Email threads that suddenly switch to a personal or free-mail address.

None of these is proof alone. Each one is a reason to run the callback step that the process already requires.

Common Mistakes and Best Practices

Fourteen real blue-team mistakes around financial theft, each with the symptom you will notice, the root cause, the fix, and the prevention that keeps it fixed.

1 · Bank-detail changes accepted by email alone

Symptom. A vendor complains that invoices were never paid — the money went somewhere else.

Root cause. The process treats an email as proof. Email is the easiest channel for an attacker to fake.

Fix. Freeze all payments that followed email-only changes; verify every recent change by callback.

Prevention. Written policy: bank-detail changes require a callback to the vendor’s number on file, plus a second approver.

2 · Single approver for wires

Symptom. Payments move even when the approver is on holiday — or when the approver is the attacker.

Root cause. Speed was optimized; control was not. One person can be phished.

Fix. Add a mandatory second approver for new payees and large amounts.

Prevention. Dual approval in the payment platform itself, not in email; exceptions require a documented reason.

3 · No alerting on finance mailbox rules

Symptom. BEC is discovered weeks later, when the vendor asks about an unpaid invoice.

Root cause. The Unified Audit Log is collected but nobody watches rule-creation events.

Fix. Deploy the TH-01 Sigma/SPL rule from 5.5 and alert on finance-keyword rules.

Prevention. Treat finance mailboxes as privileged assets: same monitoring bar as domain admins.

4 · ERP audit logs never ingested

Symptom. After fraud, nobody can say who changed the vendor record, or when.

Root cause. ERP audit logging was off, or the export was too “expensive” to ingest.

Fix. Turn on change-document audit; backfill what is retained; ingest into the SIEM.

Prevention. Payment-relevant systems must appear on the logging checklist with retention times.

5 · New-payee events not monitored

Symptom. First payment to a new account goes out with no review at all.

Root cause. The payment platform tracks payees, but the SOC has no feed of creation events.

Fix. Stream new-payee and bank-change events to the SOC; add the new-payee rate to dashboards.

Prevention. Cooling-off period and lower limits for first payments to any new account.

6 · Look-alike domains not tracked

Symptom. The fraud domain was registered weeks before the attack and nobody noticed.

Root cause. No monitoring of registrations that resemble company, bank, or top-vendor names.

Fix. Register key look-alikes, monitor new registrations, and block them at DNS and proxy.

Prevention. Weekly review of newly registered domains matching your brand and vendor list.

7 · DLP disabled to cut noise

Symptom. Nobody noticed bulk finance-document uploads before the fraud.

Root cause. DLP (data loss prevention) was tuned off after noisy months instead of tuned properly.

Fix. Re-enable with narrow, high-confidence rules for finance data going to personal storage.

Prevention. Review DLP exclusions quarterly; noise is a tuning task, not a reason to stop collecting.

8 · Finance apps outside SSO and conditional access

Symptom. Payroll and ERP logins come from any IP, with passwords only and no device check.

Root cause. Legacy apps kept local accounts “because migration is risky”.

Fix. Put them behind SSO (single sign-on) with MFA; restrict access to managed devices.

Prevention. New finance tools may not go live without identity integration sign-off.

9 · Assuming the bank catches fraud

Symptom. The SOC waits for the bank’s fraud team; the recall window closes.

Root cause. Banks see millions of transactions; your internal signals are the early ones. Banks are also slow on weekends — exactly when fraud happens.

Fix. Escalate on internal signals immediately; call the bank’s fraud line yourself.

Prevention. Runbook states who calls whom, within minutes, with what evidence.

10 · No fraud runbook

Symptom. During an incident, people search the intranet for the bank’s phone number.

Root cause. BEC was treated as “an IT problem”, so no joint runbook with finance exists.

Fix. Write the runbook now: contacts, recall steps, evidence preservation, notification duties.

Prevention. Annual BEC tabletop with treasury, finance, legal, and the SOC.

11 · Impossible-travel alerts ignored for finance users

Symptom. A ticket from last week says “user confirmed travel” — the user never traveled.

Root cause. Risky sign-in alerts are auto-closed for executives and finance staff to avoid friction.

Fix. Re-open and verify the backlog for finance roles; check what else the session did.

Prevention. Finance accounts get a lower auto-close threshold and mandatory human review.

12 · Treating BEC as user error

Symptom. After each fraud, the only action is “remind users to be careful”.

Root cause. Blame ends the investigation; control gaps stay open for the next attempt.

Fix. Post-incident review must produce at least one technical and one process control change.

Prevention. Track fraud incidents to closure like security incidents: owner, deadline, verification.

13 · Payment and printer logs trusted only on the host

Symptom. After an incident, the payment server’s logs are gone or incomplete — exactly the files that mattered.

Root cause. Logs were kept locally where anyone with admin rights on that host could edit or delete them (the Bangladesh Bank pattern).

Fix. Stream payment, gateway, and printer logs off-host in near real time to the SIEM and immutable storage.

Prevention. Treat “can this log be deleted by the attacker?” as a design question — answer it with remote, append-only collection.

14 · Payroll portal reachable from anywhere

Symptom. Deposit changes arrive from consumer IPs and unknown devices at 21:00 — and succeed.

Root cause. The HR portal was left open to the internet with password-only sign-in “for employee convenience”.

Fix. Put the portal behind SSO with MFA; allow sign-in only from managed or known devices; re-authenticate for bank changes.

Prevention. Any system that changes where money goes inherits finance-grade identity controls from day one.

Habits that keep you safe

Daily

  • Review finance-role risky sign-ins and MFA resets.
  • Check new inbox rules and auto-forwarding on finance mailboxes.
  • Confirm overnight payment approvals have matching records.

Weekly

  • Review new payees and bank-detail changes against callbacks.
  • Scan for look-alike domains of company, banks, and top vendors.
  • Check that ERP, HR, and payment audit feeds are still flowing.

Monthly

  • Tune the six rules from 5.5 with real false-positive data.
  • Review finance-app access: leavers gone? MFA coverage?
  • Walk one fraud scenario end-to-end with finance (15-minute drill).

Quick questions

Is BEC a technical hack or a scam?
Both. The best BEC starts as a technical compromise (mailbox takeover) and ends as a human scam (a convincing request). Defend both halves: identity controls plus payment verification.
The bank confirmed the transfer went through. Can we get it back?
Sometimes, if you move within hours. Recall success drops fast after the first day and after funds cross into casinos or mixers. This is why the fraud runbook and bank contacts exist before the incident.
Should finance mailboxes get special monitoring?
Yes. Tag finance, treasury, payroll, and AP mailboxes in the SIEM and apply the rules in section 5.5 with lower thresholds for those identities.
We are a small company without a SOC. What do we do?
Three things: callback verification for every bank-detail change, dual approval for new payees, and MFA on the finance mailbox. They cost almost nothing and stop the most common versions of this technique.
Where do I start with one hour a week?
Deploy the inbox-rule rule for finance mailboxes, turn on new-payee monitoring in the ERP, and write the one-page fraud runbook with bank contacts. Those three cut the most risk per hour spent.
Do we need to watch payroll too?
Yes — the payroll portal is a second money door. Alert on direct-deposit changes from new devices and on MFA resets shortly before them; payroll fraud is quieter than invoice fraud and just as expensive.
What about cryptocurrency payments to vendors?
Treat wallet addresses like bank details: verified by callback through a known channel, stored in the workflow tool, and never accepted by email alone. Clipboard-swapping malware makes copy-paste wallet handling unsafe on any endpoint.
Who should own the fraud runbook?
Treasury owns the money steps, security owns the evidence steps, and one named person owns the document. The owner updates the bank contacts and the freeze steps every quarter — that is when they rot.
Is an approval enough by itself?
No. An approval attached to an unverified bank change just documents the fraud. Approvals and verification are two different controls; this technique defeats approvals alone.
Does insurance change what we should detect?
It changes what you must prove. Insurers and regulators ask for the evidence trail — verification records, approval logs, detection timestamps. The detections in section 5.5 produce exactly that trail as a side effect.
One sentence to remember this technique by?
By the time the money moves, the attacker has been inside for weeks — so watch the quiet steps before the money, not the wire itself.
What is the fastest useful habit?
Read every new inbox rule on a finance mailbox yourself, once a week. Five minutes, and it catches the most common opening move in this entire technique.

Common misconceptions, corrected

  • “Our bank would flag that.” Banks see millions of transactions. Your internal signals — new payee, inbox rule, look-alike query — are earlier and better.
  • “We would notice a big transfer.” Fraud is usually one normal-looking payment inside normal volume, sent when approvers are distracted.
  • “MFA stopped it, so we are safe.” MFA blocks credential replay, not a spoofed vendor email or an ERP master-data change.
  • “Only finance needs to care.” HR, procurement, IT, and the help desk all sit on the money path.
  • “Crypto is not our problem.” Payroll in stablecoins, developer wallet tooling, and job-scam campaigns (Contagious Interview) say otherwise.
