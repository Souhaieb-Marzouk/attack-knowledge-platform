Transmitted Data Manipulation T1565.002

Transmitted data manipulation means changing information while it moves between two systems: the sender believes the message arrived as written, and the receiver believes it arrived as sent. 1. Overview

This section explains what transmitted data manipulation is, quotes the official MITRE ATT&CK description, and shows how this sub-technique relates to its parent and siblings.

Adversaries may alter data en route to storage or other systems in order to manipulate external outcomes or hide activity, thus threatening the integrity of the data. By manipulating transmitted data, adversaries may attempt to affect a business process, organizational understanding, and decision making.

Manipulation may be possible over a network connection or between system processes where there is an opportunity deploy a tool that will intercept and change information. The type of modification and the impact it will have depends on the target transmission mechanism as well as the goals and objectives of the adversary. For complex systems, an adversary would likely need special expertise and possibly access to specialized software related to the system that would typically be gained through a prolonged information gathering campaign in order to have the desired impact.

Source: MITRE ATT&CK T1565.002 — Transmitted Data Manipulation, version 1.1, last modified 2026-05-12. Cited by ATT&CK: FireEye/Mandiant APT38 report, October 2018, and US Department of Justice charging documents, 2018.

What it means in plain words

Two honest systems talk to each other. An attacker sits on the path between them and changes selected parts of the messages. Each side still believes the session is intact. Data in transit is data that is moving — over a network cable, Wi-Fi, a VPN, or even between two programs on one machine.

The attacker does not need to break into either endpoint. A foothold on the same local network, a fake DNS answer, or control of a router can be enough. Once traffic flows through their position, they rewrite chosen fields, such as a payee account number, and forward everything else unchanged. That selectivity is why the attack is hard to notice.

Why it matters

Encryption protects data from readers, but an attacker can sometimes get inside the encryption. They downgrade a session to plain HTTP, present a forged certificate, or own a device that ends TLS anyway, such as a corporate inspection box. Then both sides see a normal encrypted session while the content is altered.

The result is fraud that looks ordinary. The user really approved a payment. The server really received one. Only the details in between were changed. Integrity — the promise that data arrives exactly as sent — is broken without any theft notice, so detection must come from the network path.

Where it fits in the attack chain

T1565.002 belongs to the Impact tactic (TA0040). The attacker must first take a position on the path. That positioning work belongs to earlier tactics: for example, T1557.002 ARP Cache Poisoning or T1584 Compromise Infrastructure. The manipulation itself — rewriting the fields while they travel — is the T1565.002 moment.

Attackers can combine it with the sibling techniques: change data in transit and its stored copy, so reconciliation never finds the difference.

Official procedure example

ATT&CK lists one documented procedure example for this technique:

APT38 has used DYEPACK to manipulate SWIFT messages en route to a printer. A printer is a system too. The messages were correct in the database and wrong on paper, because the attacker changed them on the last hop. (Source: FireEye APT38 report, October 2018.)

    Written version of the attack story

    The same story in text. It needs no JavaScript, so you can read it anywhere.

    Transcript — "Transfer tampered on the wire" (8 steps)
    1. Step 1 — Café Wi-Fi. The customer connects a managed work laptop to the café Wi-Fi. The attacker sits on the same guest network with a small laptop and scans it. Open guest networks have no port control, so any device can join.
    2. Step 2 — ARP poisoning. The attacker sends fake ARP replies. The laptop and the café gateway both learn the attacker's MAC address for each other's IP. Traffic between customer and internet now passes through the attacker.
    3. Step 3 — Transfer request. The customer opens the bank app and starts a payment. The HTTPS request crosses the attacker's laptop before it reaches the internet.
    4. Step 4 — Downgrade and rewrite. The attacker pushes the session toward plain HTTP or presents a forged certificate for the app domain. While the form is submitted, the payee account and amount are rewritten in flight.
    5. Step 5 — Tampered request arrives. The bank receives a valid, signed-in request. The session cookie is genuine. The details are not what the customer approved.
    6. Step 6 — Controls flag the mismatch. HSTS on the app domain refuses the downgrade. The app's certificate pinning rejects the forged certificate and reports the failure. The tampering becomes visible.
    7. Step 7 — SOC correlates the signals. The proxy sees plaintext attempts to the app domain. DNS logs show the app domain resolving to a new address. The laptop reports a Windows IP-conflict event (4199). All three join into one high-priority case.
    8. Step 8 — Evict and re-secure. A step-up challenge blocks the payment. The user moves to mobile data, sessions are reset, and the café hotspot is reported to its provider. The attacker's position is gone.

    3. Red Team Perspective

    This section explains how attackers think about data in transit: the position they seek, the encryption problem they must solve, and the mistakes that expose them. It is for defenders. Practice belongs only in your own lab.

    When and why attackers use it

    Manipulating traffic is attractive when both endpoints are trusted and only the path is weak. A bank customer on shared Wi-Fi, a service-to-service API call, or a payment message on its way to a printer all fit. The attacker does not need to breach either endpoint.

    Compared with stored manipulation, the transit variant leaves no change on disk at the victim's side. After the session ends, there is no altered file and no edited record. The only witnesses are the network devices on the path — which is exactly where defenders must watch.

    APT38's use of DYEPACK shows the idea at its most precise: SWIFT payment messages were manipulated between the payment system and the printer. Both computers kept honest records. The printed output was false.

    Prerequisites: position, crypto, timing

    Three things must be true. Position: the attacker must sit on the path — same LAN with ARP poisoning, a fake DNS answer, a rogue proxy, or a compromised router. Crypto solution: the traffic is already plain, or the attacker downgrades it, or presents a forged certificate, or owns a device that ends TLS anyway. Timing: the rewrite must happen while the target session is active, so precision matters.

    Complex targets need study time. ATT&CK notes that attackers usually gain specialized knowledge through a long information-gathering campaign before they can manipulate a complex system with confidence.

    A safe lab exercise

    This exercise trains defender observation only. On a network you own, learn what normal looks like: which MAC owns the gateway address, how quiet real ARP traffic is, and what a clean certificate chain shows. Later, these baselines are what make poisoning obvious.

    lab-only · build an ARP and certificate baseline
    # 1. Show the local ARP table (Linux) — note the MAC next to your gateway IP
ip neigh show

# 2. Capture ARP traffic for about 60 seconds on your own lab network
sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -n -c 200 arp

# 3. Windows: view the same table
arp -a

# 4. Inspect a certificate chain read-only (no data sent beyond the handshake)
openssl s_client -connect example.test:443 -servername example.test -showcerts </dev/null

# 5. Review notes:
#    - one MAC per IP is normal; two MACs claiming one IP is not
#    - the issuer name of your own services is your baseline
    What you should notice

    Normal ARP is boring: one MAC per IP, few replies. Any excitement — one IP claimed by two MACs, or reply storms — is exactly what the detections in 5.5 watch for. The certificate issuer you saw is the value your TLS rules will compare against.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    Variations you will meet

    Selective rewriting — only chosen fields change, so sessions look healthy. Half-manipulation — only requests are altered, responses pass untouched. Pre-staged certificates — typosquat certificates issued before a hijack, visible in certificate transparency logs. Device-level rewriting — consumer routers owned at scale, changing traffic for many homes at once.

    Mistakes that expose them

    They forget latency, and detoured sessions are measurably slower. They let certificate warnings reach careful users. They poison ARP aggressively, and switches log loudly. They reuse certificates or IP addresses that already appear in threat feeds. And they cannot hide from certificate transparency: new certificates for your domains are public.

    Lab only

    Lab only — never run on systems you do not own. Intercepting or rewriting other people's traffic is illegal almost everywhere, including networks that feel semi-public such as offices, hotels, and campuses. Even "only testing" is a crime in many places.

    4. Blue Team Perspective

    The network is honest even when endpoints are not. This section shows which signals betray transit manipulation, which artifacts to collect, and how to triage fast.

    How defenders can see it

    Four views stack up. Layer-2 honesty: ARP tables and switch messages expose the detour itself. Protocol honesty: DNS answers and TLS certificates expose fake endpoints. Flow honesty: NetFlow shows paths that should not exist. Business honesty: fraud controls compare what the user approved with what the server received.

    No single view is complete. A forged certificate fools protocol checks; only pinning or fraud logic notices. A quiet relay fools fraud checks; only layer-2 telemetry notices. Correlation is the defense.

    What artifacts it leaves

    Expect duplicate MAC claims in ARP tables, switch MAC-flapping messages, Windows IP-conflict events (4199), unexpected DNS answer IPs for known domains, lookups to outside resolvers, self-signed or mismatched certificates on internal SNIs, plaintext requests to TLS ports, old TLS versions, and pinning failures reported by apps.

    Endpoint files stay untouched. That is the trap: if the SOC has no network telemetry, this attack produces almost no endpoint alerts at all.

    Where to look

    PlaceWhat to collectWhy it matters
    Switch and router logsMAC flapping, port-security violations, config changesThe detour physically happens here; switches see it first
    Zeek / Suricatadns.log, ssl.log, http.log, arp events, EVE JSON alertsProtocol-level proof of poisoning, spoofing, and downgrades
    Windows endpointsSystem event 4199 (IP conflict), Sysmon 22 (DNS query)Hosts see the conflict and the fake answers first-hand
    DNS server logsAnswers for protected domains, with client and resolverFake answers arrive before the user ever connects
    Proxy / egressPlaintext attempts to TLS-only domains, CONNECT patternsDowngrade attempts usually leave here
    Certificate transparencyNew certificates for your domains and typosquatsEarly warning before hijacked traffic starts
    Endpoint protectionDefender behavior detections on browser processesCatches man-in-the-browser helpers that rewrite forms

    Why it matters for the SOC

    Endpoint telemetry alone cannot see this attack. The workstation file system is untouched, so a file-focused SOC sees nothing. The good news: four cheap rule families — IP conflicts, DNS answer changes, TLS certificate mismatches, and downgrade attempts — cover most variants. Few techniques give this much detection value per network log source, and the rules are ready in 5.5.

    Short triage flow

    1. 1. Confirm the detour. Pull ARP tables and switch logs for the involved VLAN. Find the two MACs claiming one IP.
    2. 2. Identify the rogue. Map the attacker MAC to a switch port and a DHCP lease. Do not unplug yet if you want packet evidence.
    3. 3. Scope the sessions. List flows through the rogue MAC. Name the users and transactions that passed through it.
    4. 4. Freeze evidence. Export Zeek or Suricata logs, flow records, and switch logs before the port closes.
    5. 5. Contain. Shut the port, clear poisoned ARP caches, and force re-authentication for affected users.
    6. 6. Report and harden. File the fraud case if money moved. Enable dynamic ARP inspection and DHCP snooping if they were off. Review pinning and HSTS posture.
    Warning

    Clearing ARP caches restores service but also destroys a live artifact. Capture first, then clean. On busy networks, evidence lives for minutes.

    5.1 Compliance and Regulation Mapping

    Integrity attacks are a compliance problem even when nothing is stolen. Most frameworks treat the data itself as the protected asset, not only its confidentiality. If a payment record, a patient record, or a customer instruction is changed in transit, the organization is processing wrong data — and often cannot prove what the real data was. Auditors read this as a failure of integrity controls, not as a harmless incident. Several frameworks also require you to detect such changes, so a silent manipulation becomes a double finding: the control failed, and the monitoring that should have raised the alarm was missing.

    For transmitted data manipulation specifically, the requirements cluster around three duties. First, protect data while it moves, usually with strong cryptography on every important flow. Second, monitor transmissions so alteration is noticed, which means network telemetry and log review. Third, respond, because several rules treat a discovered manipulation as a reportable event. The table below maps those duties to the five frameworks that appear most often in audits. Use it to justify network sensors, certificate management, and the detection rules in 5.5.

    RegulationReferenceWhat it requiresHow this technique threatens itDetection / defense hook
    GDPR (EU) Art. 5(1)(f); Art. 32(1)(b); Art. 33 Integrity and confidentiality principle; ability to ensure ongoing integrity of processing; notify personal-data breaches within 72 hours Silent alteration of personal data in transit is itself a breach of the integrity principle, and it can trigger Art. 33 even with no theft TLS policy for all personal-data flows; integrity monitoring; document the incident decision path for altered records
    HIPAA Security Rule (US) §164.312(e)(1); §164.312(e)(2)(ii); §164.312(c)(1); §164.312(b) Transmission security; encryption of ePHI in transit; integrity controls; audit controls ePHI altered on the path breaks both transmission security and the integrity mechanism for electronic protected health information Encrypt ePHI flows end to end; log transmission events and review them; alert on certificate and DNS anomalies

    How auditors test it

    One combined paragraph serves most frameworks. Auditors ask for evidence, and the same artifacts answer all five tables above: file-integrity and configuration reports for network devices, the logging and monitoring configuration with sample alerts, log review records with named reviewers, change tickets that match the device changes, TLS scan results for important flows, and the certificate inventory with issuers and expiry dates. Keep those artifacts in one place and the compliance side of this technique is covered.

    5.2 Threat Intelligence

    Threat intelligence tells you who does this, how they did it, and what to watch first. It only helps if it flows through a simple, repeatable process. Here is one that works for a small team.

    Sources. Start with MITRE ATT&CK and its STIX/TAXII feeds for the technique itself. Add government advisories, such as CISA alerts (they use AA-YY-NNN numbers, for example AA19-131A on Sea Turtle), and vendor reports from FireEye/Mandiant, Cisco Talos, Sygnia, Kaspersky, and ESET. Sector sharing helps a lot here: FS-ISAC circulates banking-specific warnings. Machine-readable sources include MISP feeds, AlienVault OTX, and VirusTotal Collections. Certificate transparency logs are a public feed you can query yourself for your own domains.

    Workflow. Collect from a small number of sources you actually read. De-duplicate, because reports repeat each other. Map each event to a technique ID, so T1565.002 content lands in one queue. Then apply it three ways: backtest your Sigma rules against new indicators, turn reports into hunting hypotheses ("which internal SNIs had a new issuer this month?"), and give your triage team a bias list of the actors below. Brief leadership once a quarter on which actors matter to you and why.

    Cadence. Review actor and campaign relevance quarterly. Infrastructure ages fast: a hijacked domain is fixed, a rogue certificate expires, a router campaign burns out. Keep the entries below dated and retire the ones that stop matching your exposure.

    Actor / campaignWhat they did with this techniqueDetection takeawayConfidence
    APT38 Used DYEPACK to manipulate SWIFT payment messages en route to a printer (FireEye/Mandiant report, October 2018). Bank-theft campaign family active since about 2014. Integrity checks must cover the last hop, including print and internal delivery paths, not just the internet edge. High
    Sea Turtle Compromised DNS registrars and certificate authorities to run man-in-the-middle against targeted organizations (Cisco Talos, April 2019). Watch registrar changes, certificate transparency, and issuer anomalies; pin high-value apps. High
    DigiNotar attacker(s) Fraudulently issued more than 500 certificates, including one for Google domains, and intercepted Gmail traffic of users in Iran (2011 — see the case study). Pinning caught what validation could not; certificate transparency exists because of this case. High

    Brazilian bank DNS hijack crews Hijacked DNS for dozens of bank domains for hours, serving fake pages with valid certificates (Kaspersky, April 2016); reported losses were around the $1M range per public reporting.     How to read the confidence labels

    High means the behavior comes from an official ATT&CK procedure example or the primary vendor report. Medium means several public reports describe the behavior, but attribution or detail is less firm. Year of activity is noted where public reporting gives one. Re-check each entry when you refresh this table.

    Tuning and false positives

    Tune these rules with a two-step rhythm. First, run each rule in search-only mode for two weeks and sort results by count.     6. Real-World Case Study

    DigiNotar, 2011: a trusted certificate authority was compromised, and forged certificates turned into live interception of e-mail traffic. This is the clearest public case of transmitted data manipulation through a broken trust chain. All facts below come from public reporting and the published investigation.

    Context

    DigiNotar was a Dutch certificate authority (CA). A CA signs the certificates that prove a website is genuine. DigiNotar issued certificates for the Dutch government program PKIoverheid and ran a commercial business under its parent, VASCO Data Security.

    Attackers broke into its network. Public audits put the earliest signs at mid-June 2011, and the breach window is reported as June–July 2011. From inside, they fraudulently issued more than 500 certificates — one audit counted 531 — including a wildcard certificate for Google domains (*.google.com) and one for addons.mozilla.org.

    The manipulation

    A forged certificate for Google domains let the attackers run an HTTPS server that browsers trusted for Google. Users in Iran were routed to that fake server — reporting points to DNS manipulation as the likely delivery. The attacker then sat in the middle of the session: the user believed they were on Gmail, Google received a proxied request, and everything in between could be read or changed.

    That is transmitted data manipulation at the TLS layer: messages between two honest endpoints were altered on the path, using trust the attacker never should have had.

    Detection story

    Normal certificate validation passed, because the forged certificates were signed by a trusted CA. What failed was stricter: Chrome uses public-key pinning for Google properties — a hard rule that Google domains must present certificates from Google's own authorities. When the forged certificate appeared, Chrome refused the connection and showed a pinning error. Between 26 and 28 August 2011, a user reported that error publicly. Google investigated, confirmed a man-in-the-middle attack against Gmail users in Iran, and revoked and blacklisted the forged certificate — announced 29 August 2011. The lesson is sharp: endpoint-side trust rules caught what the whole PKI machinery could not.

    How the teams responded

    Google security team: detected the pinning violation through user reports, confirmed the interception, revoked the certificate, and shipped blacklist updates. Browser vendors (Microsoft, Mozilla, Google, Apple, Opera): removed trust in DigiNotar roots in early September, cutting off every fake certificate at once. Fox-IT, hired by the Dutch government: ran the forensic investigation and found the network had weak logging, so a full account was never possible. The Dutch government and its regulator: took over PKIoverheid operations and oversaw the fallout. DigiNotar and VASCO: communicated, cooperated, and filed for bankruptcy.

    Impact and consequences

    The direct victims were the intercepted Gmail users. The business victim was DigiNotar itself: once browsers removed its roots, its entire product — trust — was gone, and the company did not survive the month. Government services had to replace certificates. The industry response outlasted the incident: Certificate Transparency exists largely because a single mis-issued certificate had to be caught by a hard-coded pin. Monitoring certificate transparency logs for your own domains is the direct descendant of this case.

    Lessons learned

    • A CA is crown-jewel infrastructure. Segment it, log it completely, and monitor it like a payment system. DigiNotar could not even say what had happened, because logging was weak.
    • Pinning works. Validation said "trusted"; pinning said "wrong". High-value apps should pin and report failures to the SOC.
    • Trust is binary until it is granular. Any trusted CA could sign your domain. Certificate transparency and issuer allowlists restore visibility.
    • DNS is the delivery path. Interception needs users to arrive at the fake server; watch answers for protected domains.
    • Containment is fast or useless. Revocation plus trust-store removal ended the campaign in days — the same speed your runbook needs.
    • Silent interception is invisible to endpoints. Without pinning and network telemetry, this class of attack leaves nothing for a file-based scanner to find.

    7. Mitigation and Defense

    Defense in transit removes the attacker's position, refuses downgrades, and watches the protocol layers where manipulation must leave a trace. It starts with ATT&CK's official mitigation for this technique.

    Official ATT&CK mitigation

    M1040 — Encrypt Sensitive Information. ATT&CK's guidance for T1565.002 is to "encrypt all important data flows to reduce the impact of tailored modifications on data in transit." Encryption alone does not stop a man-in-the-middle who holds a trusted position, but it removes the easy rewrite paths and shrinks the attack to its hardest variant. Pair encryption with the integrity monitoring below. (ATT&CK M1040)

    Enterprise controls by area

    Policy and identity

    Require registrar locks and multi-factor authentication on all DNS and certificate accounts — the DigiNotar delivery path started with control of names and trust. Keep a cryptographic standard: TLS 1.2 or newer only, HSTS on all web properties, an approved cipher list, and a certificate inventory with owners and expiry dates.

    Network

    Enable dynamic ARP inspection, DHCP snooping, and 802.1X on access switches so a rogue laptop cannot take a LAN position. Segment user, service, and management VLANs. Encrypt internal service-to-service traffic with mutual TLS where you can — the unencrypted API path in TH-07 disappears.

    Endpoint

    Keep operating system and browser trust stores current. Deploy certificate pinning in high-value apps and route pin failures to the SOC — this is the control that caught DigiNotar. Allowlist browser extensions instead of letting users install freely, and keep endpoint protection enabled on browsers.

    Monitoring

    Run Zeek or Suricata at the user and service edges. Baseline DNS answers for protected domains and certificate issuers for your SNIs. Watch certificate transparency logs for your domains. Deploy the five rules in 5.5 and monitor the sensors themselves — a blind sensor hides exactly this attack.

    Cloud and remote work

    Lock DNS provider accounts and enable DNSSEC where the provider supports it. Log cloud load-balancer certificate changes. Treat the VPN concentrator and any TLS-terminating appliance as crown jewels: patch them on schedule and alert on their configuration changes.

    Response

    Keep a man-in-the-middle runbook: capture ARP tables, flows, and switch logs first; identify the rogue MAC and port; scope affected sessions; then close the port, clear caches, rotate credentials, and file fraud reports when money moved. Capture always comes before cleanup.

    Home and small-office advice

    Home users should make transit attacks unprofitable. Keep the router firmware updated and change its default admin password — the router is the home's gate. Prefer mobile data over open public Wi-Fi for banking, or use a reputable VPN. Use sites with HTTPS, and check the name spelling before you enter a password; a padlock alone is not proof. Turn on app-based two-factor authentication, which survives most session tricks better than SMS codes. If your bank app or browser shows a certificate warning, stop and report it — real banks do not break their own certificates, as DigiNotar's users in Iran experienced.

    Healthy habits

    Daily

    Check sensor health. Review IP-conflict, DNS-anomaly, and certificate alerts. Skim new certificate transparency entries for your domains.

    Weekly

    Review DNS answer changes for protected domains. Verify the certificate inventory is current. Check switch port-security events against tickets.

    Monthly

    Replay one man-in-the-middle scenario in the lab. Validate one detection end to end. Review TLS policy exceptions with the app owners.

    9. Cheat Sheet

    The whole page compressed for incident use. Print it, or keep it next to your SIEM.

    Definition and chain

    T1565.002 — Transmitted Data Manipulation: an attacker changes information while it moves between two systems, so both honest endpoints accept altered data. ATT&CK tactic: Impact (TA0040). Official mitigation: M1040 Encrypt Sensitive Information.

    Attack chain in five steps:

    1. Take a position on the path (ARP, DNS, rogue proxy, router).
    2. Solve encryption (plain traffic, downgrade, forged certificate).
    3. Intercept the target session while it is live.
    4. Rewrite chosen fields; forward the rest unchanged.
    5. Both endpoints accept and process the altered data.

    What to collect

    • ARP tables, switch MAC-flapping and port-security logs
    • Zeek or Suricata: dns.log, ssl.log, http.log
    • Windows System 4199 and Sysmon 22 from endpoints
    • Defender Operational 1116
    • Proxy logs and NetFlow paths
    • Device config-change syslog; certificate transparency feeds

    Detection rules (from 5.5)

    • DET-001 — Windows 4199 IP-conflict events (TH-03)
    • DET-002 — untrusted or mismatched TLS certificate on internal SNIs (TH-01)
    • DET-003 — internal DNS names answered with public IPs (TH-02)
    • DET-004 — legacy TLS versions to internal servers (TH-04)
    • DET-005 — Defender behavior detections on browser processes (TH-05)

    First response

    1. Verify with a second source (switch plus host, DNS plus proxy).
    2. Isolate the rogue device or affected segment.
    3. Preserve evidence: ARP tables, flows, sensor logs, certificates.
    4. Compare current data and configs with known-good baselines.
    5. Restore clean state; re-secure affected users and sessions.
    6. Report per policy, including fraud and regulator paths.

    Quick SPL for incident use

    splunk · quick triage — recent certificate failures
    -- Tune index/sourcetype names to your environment
index=network sourcetype="zeek:ssl" server_name="*.example-bank.test"
    validation_status!="ok"
| stats count AS events, values(issuer) AS issuers BY server_name, id.resp_h
    splunk · quick triage — IP conflicts in the last day
    -- Tune index/sourcetype names to your environment
index=win sourcetype="WinEventLog:System" EventCode=4199
| rex field=Message "address conflict for IP address (?<conflict_ip>[0-9.]+) with the system having network hardware address (?<rogue_mac>[0-9A-Fa-f\-:]+)"
| stats count AS events, values(conflict_ip) AS conflict_ips, values(rogue_mac) AS rogue_macs BY Computer
    splunk · quick triage — internal names with unusual answers
    -- Tune index/sourcetype names to your environment
index=edr sourcetype="XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" EventCode=22 QueryName="*.corp.example"
| stats count AS events, values(QueryResults) AS answers BY QueryName, Computer
