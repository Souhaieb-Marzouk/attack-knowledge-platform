    Overview

    A short tour of the technique: what a forced shutdown or reboot is, why attackers use it, and what this guide teaches you.

    System Shutdown/Reboot means an attacker switches a computer off or restarts it on purpose. Every operating system ships commands for this. Windows has shutdown and the PowerShell restart tools. Linux has reboot, shutdown, halt, and poweroff. macOS has shutdown and osascript, the command-line tool that runs AppleScript control commands. Network devices use the reload command. Attackers abuse these normal features. A reboot interrupts everyone who is working, drops remote sessions, and destroys what lives only in memory. A shutdown can even stop a machine from starting again when it is combined with damage to the boot process.

    This technique rarely stands alone. ATT&CK records show it arriving at the end of destructive attacks. Wipers such as NotPetya, Shamoon, and AcidRain wipe disks first and then force a reboot so the damage takes effect. Ransomware crews such as Black Basta and Qilin shut down or restart machines to interrupt response work. Qilin specifically rebooted a backup server to slow recovery. A reboot also destroys the evidence an incident responder needs most. Volatile memory is the working memory that holds running programs and open connections. It is gone the moment power drops.

    Where it sits in the attack chain

    Attackers reach this stage late. They must already control the machine with strong rights, because ordinary users cannot usually restart servers or hypervisors. When you see a forced reboot, the attacker is either finishing a destructive payload or clearing their path.

    Written version of the simulation

    The same story in text, for screen readers and for printing. Each step names the action, the evidence it leaves, and what the defender should notice.

    Step 1 — Get in with a stolen ops account

    The attacker signs in to OPS01, an operations workstation, with an account bought from an infostealer market. The logon comes from an unknown outside address. Evidence: a Security event 4624 logon whose source does not match the jump-host list. The SOC should compare logon sources for operations accounts against known locations.

    Step 2 — Check the power rights

    The attacker runs whoami /priv and sees SeShutdownPrivilege in the list. This user right allows the account to switch the machine off. Evidence: a Sysmon event 1 process creation with a rare parent chain. The SOC should flag privilege checks on workstations and record which rights the caller looked for.

    Step 3 — Schedule a sneaky reboot

    The attacker schedules a delayed reboot on APP01 with shutdown /r /t 300 and the reason text "maintenance". A timer appears, and Windows logs event 1074 at once with the caller and the reason. Evidence: System event 1074 from shutdown.exe outside any change window. The SOC should treat every 1074 as a question, not as noise.

    Step 4 — Force the reboot now

    Tired of waiting, the attacker forces an immediate reboot of APP01 with shutdown /r /t 0 /f. The server goes dark, live sessions drop, and the unclean stop leaves Kernel-Power 41 behind. Evidence: event 1074 again plus Kernel-Power 41 on the next boot. The SOC should page on forced server reboots that have no change ticket.

    Step 5 — Push a reboot wave

    The attacker pushes the same command to many servers through remote execution. SQL01 and WEB01 power off one after another, and users see errors across the company. Evidence: Sysmon event 1 for shutdown.exe with a remote-service parent, one account on many hosts. The SOC should correlate shutdown commands across hosts by the same user.

    Step 6 — Turn the VMs off

    On the hypervisor, the attacker runs power-off commands against virtual machines. Several VM icons go dark at once, and whole services disappear even though their physical hosts are fine. Evidence: ESXi hostd log entries and shell history for power-off commands. The SOC should require an approved admin name behind every hypervisor power action.

    Step 7 — The SOC connects the dots

    The SIEM — short for security information and event management — links events 1074, 6008, and Kernel-Power 41. It links them to one unusual account across many hosts and raises a single critical incident for T1529. The reboot wave becomes one clear attack instead of scattered noise. Evidence: a correlated alert with the shared user and host fields. The SOC should build this correlation before the attack, not during it.

    Step 8 — Contain and recover

    The SOC isolates OPS01, blocks the stolen account, and boots the affected hosts from a known-good state. Services return and the attacker is locked out. Evidence: incident ticket with the timeline and the restore records. The lesson: alert on event 1074 with unexpected callers, because that single event announced this whole attack.

    3

    Red team / hacker perspective

    Understanding the attacker's choices helps you predict the move and place your sensors. Everything here is described for defense and for labs you own.

    When and why attackers use it

    Timing is the whole point. Wiper teams force the reboot at the moment their damage must take effect. NotPetya rebooted each infected machine about one hour after infection. APT37 ran shutdown /r /t 1 one second after wiping the master boot record, so the machine woke up broken. AcidRain and AcidPour rebooted their victim systems once wiping finished, and Shamoon restarted machines after its own wipe. The reboot is what turns disk damage into an outage.

    Ransomware crews use the same move for a different reason: control. Black Basta used ShellExecuteA to shut down and restart the victim system. ShrinkLocker forced a shutdown after encrypting drives. Qilin rebooted a backup server so recovery work stopped while the encryption spread. Maze once shut machines down so its ransomware would run inside a virtual machine after the reboot. A reboot also drops the defender's live response sessions and erases memory. That is exactly what an attacker wants while they move to the next target.

    Some attackers use the command in their own favor. A delayed reboot with a friendly reason string, such as a fake maintenance message, buys them time and cover. The machine logs the shutdown as planned work, and the timer lets them leave the network before it fires. This is why the caller behind event 1074 matters more than the reason text the caller typed.

    How it is performed

    The commands are short, built-in, and quiet. On Windows, the attacker needs the shutdown user right, which admins hold by default. Then the attacker runs a small set of native tools. The commands below are shown for lab study only. Run them exclusively inside a disposable virtual machine that you own, and take a snapshot before you start.

    Two more paths matter. Attackers with cloud keys call APIs such as StopInstances, RebootInstances, or TerminateInstances to switch off fleets at once. And destructive malware can skip the shutdown command entirely. It calls the Windows function NtRaiseHardError (or ZwRaiseHardError) with the shutdown option, which forces a blue screen crash. The machine reboots anyway, but the crash leaves only unclean-stop events behind instead of a tidy 1074 with a caller name.

    Prerequisites and conditions

    What the attacker needs first

    • A local account that holds the shutdown user right, or admin rights on the host.
    • Remote shutdown rights on the target, or a remote execution path such as a service, scheduled task, or WMI (Windows Management Instrumentation, the built-in remote management system).
    • For hypervisors: root or shell access to the ESXi host.
    • For cloud: standing IAM permissions (identity and access management rights) such as ec2:StopInstances.
    • For network devices: enable or admin access through AAA-managed logins (AAA means authentication, authorization, and accounting).

    What limits them

    • Windows logs event 1074 for API-based shutdowns, with the caller named.
    • Hard crashes still leave Kernel-Power 41 and 6008 events behind.
    • AAA command accounting records every device reload.
    • Cloud audit trails name the identity behind every stop or reboot call.
    • Change windows make unexpected reboots stand out from planned work.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    Variations are wide because every platform has a native switch. KillDisk rebooted machines by terminating critical processes instead of calling a shutdown command, which produced a different trail. Olympic Destroyer shut systems down after changing their configuration, and HermeticWiper simply initiated a shutdown once its damage was done. WhisperGate used the Windows ExitWindowsEx function with the EXW_SHUTDOWN flag, so no shutdown.exe process ever appeared on the screen or in the logs.

    Attacker mistakes are useful to know. Asking for a shutdown without the right privilege fails, and the failed access attempt is itself a signal. Using a delayed timer does not hide anything, because 1074 is written the moment the command runs, not when the timer ends. Reusing the stolen operations account across many hosts turns a small incident into an obvious pattern. And rebooting does not clear logs, so attackers who reboot and walk away leave a complete record for the SOC.

    Danger · lab-only

    Never run the commands above on a production or shared system. They interrupt real work, drop other people's sessions, and can leave a machine unable to start if other damage is present. Use an isolated virtual machine with a fresh snapshot, and treat every command as if a real SOC were watching it.

    4

    Blue team / SOC perspective

    Shutdowns happen every day for honest reasons, so the job is to separate planned reboots from attacker actions quickly. Here is what the technique leaves behind and how to triage it fast.

    How defenders can see it

    Windows System log

    Event 1074 names the process and user that requested a shutdown or restart, plus the reason text. Events 6005 and 6006 mark the event log starting and stopping cleanly. Event 6008 marks an unexpected shutdown. Kernel-Power 41 marks a reboot without a clean shutdown. This is the strongest single source.

    Process telemetry

    Sysmon event 1 or Security event 4688 with command-line auditing shows shutdown.exe, Restart-Computer, Stop-Computer, and wmic calls with their parent processes. The parent often reveals remote execution.

    Linux, ESXi, network, cloud

    auditd records the command and user on Linux. hostd logs record power actions on ESXi. AAA accounting records device commands. CloudTrail or Azure logs name the identity behind instance power actions.

    Artifacts the technique leaves behind

    Why it matters and how it fits the chain

    A reboot is not just downtime; it is an evidence problem. The moment power drops, you lose the memory image that would show the malware, the injected code, and the live connections. Every remote response session dies with it. Attackers know this and reboot specifically to slow your investigation. In the worst case the machine never comes back, because the reboot activated a bootkit or completed a wipe. Treat any unexpected power action on a server as a possible last step of a destructive attack. Then check what else that account has touched.

    Short triage flow

    1. Open the 1074 event

      Read the caller process, the user, and the reason text. Compare them with your change calendar and with the patch schedule before you decide anything.

    2. Check who and from where

      Pull the logon events for that account around the same minute. A server reboot driven from a workstation instead of a jump host is a strong warning.

    3. Scope across the fleet

      Search the last 24 hours for shutdown commands and 1074 events by the same account. Many hosts mean a campaign, not a mistake.

    4. Look for the follow-up damage

      Check for wipe or encryption signals on the affected hosts: mass file renames, shadow copy deletion, boot configuration changes, or a crash storm.

    5. Contain without powering off

      Isolate hosts through the EDR (endpoint detection and response) tool or the network layer. Do not switch them off, so you keep memory evidence and logs. Then reset the involved account and escalate.

    Good practice

    Write the triage steps above into your incident runbook before you need them. Teams that capture memory before any restart keep the evidence that proves how the attack moved. Teams that reboot first often never learn what they lost.

    Detection engineering deep dive

    This is the practical core of the guide. It covers regulation mapping, threat intelligence, ranked threats, and real log examples. It ends with five Sigma rules and matching Splunk queries you can adapt today.

    How ATT&CK detects this technique

    The official ATT&CK detection strategy is DET0559, named Multi-Platform Shutdown or Reboot Detection via Execution and Host Status Events. It has five analytics, and each one pairs a command with a host status signal. AN1538 pairs Windows shutdown and restart commands with event 1074 and 6006 records. AN1539 pairs Linux shutdown, reboot, and systemctl poweroff execution with auditd and syslog. AN1540 pairs macOS shutdown, reboot, and osascript calls with the unified log. AN1541 pairs ESXi commands such as esxcli system shutdown and vim-cmd vmsvc/power.shutdown with hostd and shell logs outside maintenance windows. AN1542 pairs network device reload commands with TACACS+/AAA privilege records.

    5.1 Regulation mapping

    Forced shutdowns and reboots attack the availability of your services, so they map to continuity and monitoring controls. Auditors expect proof that planned power actions are controlled, that unexpected ones raise alerts, and that recovery works after an unplanned stop. The table below shows what each framework expects and the evidence a detection program provides.

    Framework and controlWhy it appliesExpected evidence
    GDPR — Article 32 (security of processing) Requires the ability to restore the availability of and access to personal data after an incident. A forced reboot that destroys memory evidence and prolongs an outage makes a breach harder to contain and longer to report. Availability incident records, alerts on unexpected shutdowns, incident reports that show recovery time and evidence handling.
    HIPAA Security Rule — §164.308(a)(7) contingency plan Covered entities must plan for emergencies that take critical systems offline, which is exactly what this technique does to servers and hypervisors. Contingency plan covering forced outages, downtime procedures, records of unexpected shutdown alerts, and recovery test results.
    ISO/IEC 27001:2022 — A.8.14 (redundancy), A.8.16 (monitoring activities) Redundant capacity only helps if the loss of a node is noticed. Annex A expects anomalies such as unexplained reboots to be monitored and investigated. Monitoring rules for shutdown events, tickets for reboot storms, uptime and availability dashboards with investigated gaps.
    SOC 2 — CC7.2 (anomaly detection), A1.2 (recovery infrastructure) Trust services criteria expect detection of unusual system behavior and tested recovery of the processing infrastructure after interruptions. Detection rule documentation, alert and response records for power actions, recovery drills that include unclean shutdowns.
    PCI DSS — 12.10.x (incident response plan and testing) Organizations handling cardholder data must prepare for attacks that disable systems, including destructive malware that reboots hosts to complete its work. IR plan covering outage and wipe scenarios, staff drills, monitoring evidence that cardholder-data hosts restart only inside approved windows.
    NIST SP 800-53 Rev.5 — AC-6 (least privilege), SI-4 (system monitoring), CP-10 (recovery) The shutdown right should be restricted to approved admins, and power actions should be monitored. Recovery from organization-level outages must be reconstituted and tested. User rights assignment records, SIEM monitoring coverage for shutdown events, documented and tested recovery procedures.

    5.2 Threat intelligence

    Intelligence makes your detection rules stronger and your priorities honest. For this technique, the picture is clear, because destructive campaigns since 2016 have reused the same small set of commands. Collect from several source types, then record how confident you are in each finding before you change a rule or a runbook.

    MITRE ATT&CK

    Start with the technique page and its procedure examples. The list of malware and groups that use T1529 spans wipers, ransomware crews, and state-linked teams, which makes rule coverage easy to justify.

    Vendor reporting

    Security vendor blogs publish exact commands, timelines, and API calls for each campaign. Turn their incident timelines into your detection logic and tuning notes.

    OSINT and ISACs

    Free sources such as CISA advisories and sector ISAC bulletins share indicators and tactics early. Sector-specific advice tells you which crews target organizations like yours.

    Commercial feeds

    Paid feeds add actor tracking, infrastructure, and malware families. Use them for context and prioritization rather than as a replacement for your own telemetry.

    Internal telemetry

    Your own logs are the most valuable source. Reboots without tickets, failed shutdown attempts, and near misses show what attackers actually try in your environment.

    High means several independent reports plus your own sightings. Medium means one solid public report. Low means a hypothesis that still needs testing. Write the level next to every intelligence note.

    5.3 Threats ranking and categorization

    Five realistic threats cover this technique well. The rank reflects how likely the threat is and how much damage it causes when it works. Preconditions describe what an attacker must already have; the justification explains the rank so you can defend it in planning meetings.

    ThreatRankPlatformDescription and preconditionsImpact and justification
    THR-01 — Forced shutdown or reboot of servers via command line High Windows One command drops every session on a server. Examples are shutdown /r /t 0 /f and Restart-Computer -Force. Preconditions: an account with the shutdown right and command execution. Interrupts service and destroys memory evidence on the most valuable hosts. Very common across campaigns, but single-host scope keeps it below Critical.
    THR-02 — Mass reboot across many hosts via remote execution Critical Windows A reboot wave pushed through PsExec, WMI, scheduled tasks, or GPO (Group Policy), or forced with a delayed timer. Preconditions: domain credentials with remote shutdown rights. Takes out many servers at once and is the classic shape of a destructive campaign. The fleet-wide blast radius earns the Critical rank.
    THR-03 — Unexpected reboot storm with unclean shutdowns High Windows Spikes of Kernel-Power 41 and event 6008, including malware that forces a blue screen through NtRaiseHardError. Preconditions: code execution, no special rights needed to crash a host. Signals either a crash-forcing payload or coordinated power abuse, and it destroys evidence without leaving a named caller. Harder to attribute, so the rank is High.
    THR-04 — Linux reboot, halt, or poweroff by unexpected user or outside maintenance Medium Linux Commands such as systemctl reboot or poweroff run outside the change window. The account sits outside the ops group. Preconditions: root, sudo, or a privileged service account. Disrupts services and can hide cleanup activity, but Linux estates usually need root, which lowers likelihood compared with Windows.
    THR-05 — Cloud, hypervisor, and network device power actions High Cloud / ESXi / Network / macOS StopInstances in the cloud. vim-cmd vmsvc/power.off or esxcli vm process kill on ESXi. reload on network devices. macOS shutdown -h now is a lower-priority variant of the same move.     Each threat below lists the log sources that see it and the fields that matter. It also shows what normal and suspicious behavior look like, plus the detection logic in plain words. A realistic event example follows each table so you can validate your pipeline against real shapes.

    Threat 1 (THR-01) — Forced server shutdown or reboot via command line

    AspectDetails
    Log sourcesWindows System log event 1074 records the request. It names the caller process, user, and reason. Sysmon event 1 or Security event 4688 shows shutdown.exe, Restart-Computer, Stop-Computer, and wmic.

    Detection logicAlert when event 1074 names a shutdown utility and the caller, time, or reason does not match an approved change. Also alert on forced shutdown commands on servers from Sysmon or 4688.

    Threat 2 (THR-02) — Mass reboot across many hosts via remote execution

    Detection logicCount 6008 and Kernel-Power 41 events in 15-minute buckets per host and across the fleet. Alert when the fleet count or the per-host count passes your baseline.

    AspectDetails

    5.5 Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    Five complete Sigma rules follow, one per ranked threat, each with a matching Splunk query. The rules target Windows Sysmon, the Windows System log, Linux auditd, and AWS CloudTrail. If you collect Security 4688 instead of Sysmon, swap the logsource and keep the field logic. The Splunk queries assume the index and sourcetype conventions listed below. Adjust the index names to your environment and document the change.

    Field assumptions for Splunk

    index=sysmon holds Sysmon XML events with fields like Image, CommandLine, and ParentImage. index=wineventlog holds the System channel as XmlWinEventLog:System. index=linux holds auditd data with fields like a0 and type. index=aws holds aws:cloudtrail data with JSON fields like eventName. Field extraction must be enabled for XML inputs before these queries work.

    Rule 1 — Forced shutdown or reboot via command line

    Mapping note: each Sigma endswith becomes a wildcard path match in Splunk. Each contains modifier becomes a repeated CommandLine token joined by AND. False positives: planned reboots in a change window. Tuning: join the results with your change calendar lookup, drop the known patch-window accounts, and raise severity when the caller is not a named administrator.

    Rule 2 — Mass reboot across many hosts via remote execution

    Mapping note: Sigma cannot count hosts by itself. The volume check moves into the SPL dc(host) aggregation. The three selections collapse into one OR block over ParentImage. False positives: bulk reboots by platform automation. Tuning: allow-list the automation accounts, and keep the host threshold at three or more in an hour. Page immediately when the caller is interactive rather than a service.

    Rule 3 — Burst of unclean shutdowns and critical reboots

    Mapping note: the System log selection maps to the XmlWinEventLog:System sourcetype. The time-window logic that Sigma leaves to the alert engine becomes the bin plus stats pair here. False positives: genuine power failures and hardware maintenance. Tuning: learn your baseline first, and exclude known maintenance nights. Add event 1001 BugCheck records to the same search when you hunt for crash-forcing malware.

    Rule 4 — Linux reboot, halt, or poweroff outside maintenance

    Mapping note: Sigma lists map to the Splunk IN clause. The separate systemctl selection becomes a bracketed OR group, because it matches on two fields at once. False positives: patch automation and scheduled maintenance. Tuning: allow-list the automation accounts and window, and watch uid versus auid to catch sudo misuse. Add last reboot checks to quarterly reviews to find hosts that rebooted silently.

    Rule 5 — Cloud instance stop, reboot, or terminate by unusual identity

    Mapping note: CloudTrail JSON fields map directly to Splunk search-time extractions. The Sigma list of event names becomes the IN clause. False positives: scheduled lifecycle actions under fixed role ARNs. Tuning: allow-list those automation roles. Page on any interactive identity, any new source IP, and any call that touches a backup or domain-controller instance. Add ESXi hostd and AAA command records to the same incident review for the hypervisor and device variants.

    6

    Real-world case study

    Petya, 2016: the attack that made the forced reboot the weapon itself. The payload was harmless-looking until the machine restarted, and that single power action is the T1529 moment.

    Context

    In 2016, researchers at ESET and Kaspersky documented Petya, a ransomware family delivered through fake job-application emails. This case study is a training reconstruction based on that public reporting. The attachments looked like ordinary CV documents, and opening them launched the payload with the user's own rights. Instead of encrypting files one by one, Petya took a harder path. It overwrote the master boot record, the small boot program area at the start of the disk. It wrote its own bootkit there, a small malicious loader that starts before Windows. Then it forced a reboot. The reboot was not a side effect. It was the switch that activated the whole attack.

    Attack chain mapping

    Petya followed the same chain this guide describes for T1529. The technique arrived last and flipped the attack from stealth to destruction.

    Phishing email→ User execution→ MBR overwrite→ Forced reboot (T1529)→ MFT encryption and extortion

    Timeline

    1. Day 0 · morning
      The bait arrives

      A fake job application lands in an inbox. The victim opens the attachment, a dropper runs, and the Petya payload is released on the workstation. Admin rights arrive through the user confirmation dialog.

    2. Seconds later
      Boot record overwritten

      The payload writes its bootkit over the master boot record. Nothing visible changes on screen, and the machine still works normally, which is exactly why the victim keeps using it.

    3. Right after the overwrite
      Forced reboot (T1529)

      The payload forces a system reboot. The moment power cycles, the tampered boot program starts instead of Windows, and the attacker's code takes control of the boot process.

    4. After the reboot
      Fake repair screen

      A screen that looks like a normal CHKDSK disk check appears, claiming to repair the drive. While the user waits patiently, the fake check is encrypting the master file table in the background. That table is the NTFS index of every file.

    5. When encryption ends
      The ransom note

      The repair screen is replaced by a red ransom note demanding payment. The system can no longer start normally, and without specialist recovery the files stay out of reach.

    6. Response phase
      Recovery is painful

      Because the file index itself was encrypted, normal restore tools failed. Researchers later published ways to recover the encryption keys from memory, but many victims simply rebuilt their machines from scratch.

    Detection point

    The best moment was the forced reboot itself. Event 1074 appeared in the System log immediately before the machine restarted. It named the malicious process as the caller. Process logging showed the payload writing to disk boot structures. A defender watching 1074 with an unexpected caller had a clean early warning. The warning came while the machine still worked, minutes after an office document ran.

    Containment

    Teams isolated affected machines from the network before they rebooted, where they could. They blocked the sender domains. They warned users not to open new job-application attachments. The attack encrypted the file index only after the reboot. So every hour of warning saved machines that would otherwise have been lost with the next restart.

    Eradication

    Cleaning meant rewriting the boot record from trusted media, removing the bootkit, and scanning for the dropper everywhere. Machines that had already rebooted into the fake repair screen needed full reimaging. The file index could not be restored without the specialist key-recovery methods published later.

    Recovery

    Organizations with clean backups restored their data after rebuilding the operating systems. Those without backups weighed the ransom demand against data loss. Response teams also named their tools from then on: memory capture with a tool such as Volatility came before any restart. The event pushed many teams to treat boot-level protection features as part of ransomware defense, not as hardware settings. Those features include secure boot and measured boot.

    Impact and consequences

    Petya 2016 was small compared with its 2017 successor NotPetya, but its design shaped everything that followed. It proved that a single forced reboot could move an attack from the user session into the boot path. Antivirus tools of that era were blind there. Security teams learned to care about events 1074 and 6008, and ransomware authors copied the reboot-activated pattern in later families. The fake CHKDSK screen also became a reminder that even a familiar-looking screen during startup deserves suspicion.

    Team response and communication

    Response teams sorted machines into three groups: never rebooted, rebooted but not yet encrypted, and already encrypted. That ordering made the work tractable, because the first group only needed cleanup and the last group needed rebuilds. Help desk staff were briefed with one instruction: do not restart infected machines. The message spread faster through the service desk than through any technical control.

    Lessons learned

    • Alert on event 1074 with unexpected callers; the forced reboot announced this attack before any encryption started.
    • Never power off or restart a suspicious machine by reflex; you may be executing the attacker's final step for them.
    • Capture memory first, because Petya's keys could later be recovered from it, not from disk.
    • Protect the boot path with secure boot, and monitor for boot configuration changes.
    • Treat office-document processes that touch disk boot structures as critical alerts, not curiosities.

    Mitigation and defense

    Be honest about this one: ATT&CK states that this technique cannot be easily mitigated with preventive controls. It abuses the normal power features of every system. The defense is to reduce who can do it, make every action leave a trace, and recover fast.

    What ATT&CK says

    The technique page notes that shutdown and reboot are legitimate system features, so there is no simple block that stops abuse without breaking operations. The controls below therefore focus on least privilege, change control, tamper-resistant logging, and fast detection and response, which together remove most of the risk.

    Controls that actually reduce the risk

    Least privilege for power actions

    Use User Rights Assignment to reserve the two Windows shutdown rights for approved admins. The rights are Shut down the system and Force shutdown from a remote system. Apply this on servers first. It removes the SeShutdownPrivilege that attackers check for with whoami /priv. This follows the least-privilege principle that ATT&CK mitigation guidance promotes across its technique pages.

    Change-controlled maintenance windows

    Make every planned reboot carry a change ticket and a window, and feed that calendar into the SIEM as a lookup. A reboot with a ticket is noise; a reboot without one is a signal. This single habit powers the tuning for every rule in section 5.

    Network device AAA and command authorization

    Put devices behind AAA with TACACS+. That protocol centralizes device logins and command checks. Authorize commands, so reload requires an approved role. Command accounting then records who reloaded what, which is the only visibility many devices offer.

    Cloud and hypervisor guardrails

    Use cloud service control policies to deny Stop and Terminate calls outside automation roles. Keep ESXi in lockdown mode with a small, named root group. Locking down who can press the power switch protects every machine that lives behind it.

    Tamper-resistant, remote logging

    Ship System log, Sysmon, auditd, hostd, and AAA records off the host to the SIEM in near real time. A reboot cannot hide a trail that already left the machine. Remote shipping keeps evidence safe when the attacker forces a restart to cover their work.

    Fast detection and response

    Deploy the five rules from section 5, write a reboot-storm runbook, and rehearse it. The runbook should say clearly: isolate through EDR or network controls, never power off first, capture memory, and reset the involved account.

    Home and small office advice

    Protect your own machines

    • Use a standard (non-admin) account for daily work, so malware cannot restart or damage the system freely.
    • Turn on File History (Windows) or Time Machine (macOS) to an external drive you unplug after backups.
    • Read before clicking restart dialogs that appear right after opening an email attachment.
    • Keep the recovery USB or installer media for each computer in a safe place, made while the machine was healthy.

    Small-office quick wins

    • Schedule updates and restarts at night from one central place, so daytime reboots stand out.
    • Keep one admin account and one daily account per person, and never share the admin one.
    • Use a small UPS (battery backup) for the server, and test that a power cut does not corrupt it.
    • Write down who may restart the server, and treat any other restart as an incident to review.
    Good practice

    You cannot remove the power switch from a computer. But you can decide who is allowed to press it. You can record every press off-box and notice the ones you did not plan. Teams with rights restrictions, a change calendar in the SIEM, and a rehearsed runbook turn this technique from an outage into an alert.

    • Walk one reboot alert end-to-end and time the triage.
    • Review new suppressions and tuning changes with a second reviewer.

    • Open the raw 1074 event: caller process, user, reason text, and time.

    One-line summary

    A reboot can be the last step of an attack, not the end of one. Watch the caller behind event 1074, treat unexplained unclean stops as a storm warning, and never press the power switch yourself until memory is captured. The team that knows its planned reboots spots the unplanned one in minutes.
