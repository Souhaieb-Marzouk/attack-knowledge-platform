T1133 – External Remote Services

🎯Tactic: Persistence · Initial Access 💻Platforms: Windows · Linux · macOS · Containers 🎚Attacker complexity: Low 📊Detection difficulty: Medium

Every company needs a front door for remote work. A VPN, a Citrix gateway, an RDP server, or an SSH box sits on the internet so staff can log in from home. Attackers love this door because it is open on purpose. In this technique, they log into remote services that the company itself exposed. They steal passwords, buy them on criminal markets, phish them, or exploit a bug in the remote-access appliance. Sometimes they skip passwords completely and steal an active session token from the appliance memory — the well-documented "Citrix Bleed" bug from 2023 did exactly this and hit companies like Boeing and Comcast. Once the attacker is inside, they look like a normal remote worker: valid username, valid VPN client, normal IP from an allowed country. That is why this technique is hard to catch and why it opens the door to ransomware. ATT&CK procedure examples include APT29 using compromised VPN and Citrix identities, APT41 abusing VPN links through third parties, APT28 routing brute-force attempts through VPNs and Tor, and Volt Typhoon using VPNs to reach critical infrastructure. In this guide you will learn how the attack works, what logs it leaves behind, and how to write detections that catch it.

Think like the attacker

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

When attackers use this

External remote services are a first-choice entry point for two very different kinds of attackers. Ransomware groups such as Wizard Spider and LockBit affiliates use them to get instant, quiet access before deploying ransomware. Espionage groups such as APT29, APT41, and Volt Typhoon use them to walk in like remote staff and stay for months. The attack usually starts after a credential leak from another breach, a phishing win, or a public exploit for the remote-access appliance itself. Timing matters little: the service is open day and night. Many real incidents begin at night or on weekends, when nobody is watching the VPN logs and IT staff cannot call back a suspicious user.

How attackers do it, step by step

  1. Find the door. They scan the internet (or use search engines for exposed services) to find VPN portals, Citrix gateways, RDP, and SSH. They note the exact product and version from page fingerprints and error strings.
  2. Check the version. An old appliance version means known bugs. A Citrix, Fortinet, Ivanti, Pulse Secure, or SonicWall box that missed a patch is an easy way in.
  3. Get credentials. They buy or reuse passwords from past breaches, phish them, or guess them. Many people reuse work passwords on other sites.
  4. Try the passwords quietly. They spray a few common passwords across many accounts — or one correct password across many VPNs — so no single account shows a loud lockout storm.
  5. Log in as a real user. A valid login produces a normal session. If MFA pushes arrive, some attackers spam them until a tired employee approves (MFA fatigue).
  6. Steal a session instead of a password. If credentials fail, they exploit the appliance. Bugs like CVE-2023-4966 ("Citrix Bleed") leak active session tokens from memory. A stolen token replays the real user's session without password or MFA.
  7. Land inside. The VPN tunnel or Citrix desktop gives them a workstation-like presence inside. They enumerate drives, shares, and accounts as any remote employee would.
  8. Stay and expand. They add a rogue VPN account or certificate, install a backdoor (Sandworm used a modified Dropbear SSH), and move laterally toward valuable data and domain controllers.

Where it works best

🏢Hybrid-work companies

Large VPN estates with thousands of remote users. One more login looks normal. Mergers often leave parallel, forgotten gateways online.

🏥Healthcare, schools, public sector

Long appliance lifecycles and small IT teams. Patch backlogs of months are common, so old CVEs still work.

🏭Critical infrastructure

Volt Typhoon used VPNs to enter energy and communications networks. Flat networks make the first login very valuable.

☁️Cloud desktops and vendors

VDI platforms, MSP-managed remote tools, and third-party VPN links (as in the APT41 billing-service case) widen the doors beyond your own gateway.

Why attackers like this technique

  • No exploit needed. A stolen password is cheaper, quieter, and more reliable than any malware.
  • They inherit your trust. Traffic comes from your own VPN pool, inside your geo-allow rules and firewall policy.
  • It beats the perimeter. Email filters and web proxies never see this path; the attacker logs in over an encrypted tunnel you provide.
  • It survives cleanup. If you reset one password but keep sessions alive, a stolen token still works — as Citrix Bleed victims learned.
  • Deniability. Failed attempts blend into normal remote-user noise, and success looks like exactly what the service was built for.

What attackers need first

  • Recon: the target's VPN/Citrix/RDP/SSH footprint and product versions.
  • Credentials from breaches, phishing, infostealer logs, or bought on criminal markets.
  • A brute-force or password-spray toolkit (hydra, medusa) for weak-password labs.
  • A working exploit for the appliance when credentials fail (public PoCs exist for most edge CVEs).
  • Patience and proxy/Tor infrastructure to spread attempts across IPs.

Tools and example commands

These commands are for authorized labs only — your own test VMs or a written permission scope. They show defenders what the malicious traffic looks like. First, map remote services and weak TLS on a lab appliance:

recon_lab.sh
# Lab only: fingerprint remote services on your own test box
nmap -sV --script ssl-enum-ciphers -p 22,443,3389,5900 10.20.30.40
# Check SSH server hardening
ssh-audit 10.20.30.40:22

Next, a controlled brute-force test against a lab RDP target to verify lockout and alerting works. Run it against your own lab host and watch your SIEM fill with 4625 events:

spray_lab.sh
# Authorized lab: 4 tries, stop at first success, custom user+password lists
hydra -L lab-users.txt -P lab-passwords.txt -t 4 -f 10.20.30.40 rdp
Ethics and law — read this first

Running these commands against systems you do not own or have written permission to test is a crime under computer misuse laws (for example the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the UK Computer Misuse Act, and similar laws worldwide). Even guessing passwords without permission is illegal in most countries. Use isolated lab machines, keep evidence of your authorization, and share findings only with the system owner.

Think like the defender

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

What defenders can see

You cannot stop the front door from existing, but every knock is logged. Remote-access appliances (FortiGate, Palo Alto GlobalProtect, Citrix/NetScaler, Cisco AnyConnect), VPN concentrators, identity providers (Entra ID, Okta), RDP hosts, and SSH servers all produce authentication logs. The winning pattern is a chain: failures followed by a success, a success from a new country or a new device, a login at 03:00 local time, two sessions for one user at once, or a session that starts without a fresh authentication event. Each signal alone can be noise. Together, in one timeline, they are an incident.

Where to look

  • VPN/remote-access appliance event logs (auth success + failure + admin actions).
  • Identity provider sign-in and risk logs (Entra ID, Okta) for the accounts behind the VPN.
  • Windows Security log on RDP servers and domain controllers (4624/4625 type 10, 4720).
  • Linux auth.log / journald on SSH bastions; check Accepted password vs Accepted publickey.
  • MFA provider logs (Duo, Entra MFA) for push storms and fraud reports.
  • Firewall/flow data: what the VPN user touched after login (SMB, RDP, LDAP).
  • EDR on VPN-terminated endpoints: new processes right after remote login.

Why this matters to the SOC

A T1133 compromise is invisible to classic perimeter tools: there is no malware at the gate, just an approved login. The blast radius is everything the VPN can reach — usually files, email, domain admin paths, and the whole internal network. Most ransomware cases that started this way show days of quiet "normal user" activity before encryption. That dwell time is your window: catching the login chain (T1/T4/T5/T6 below) hours earlier is the difference between a blocked account and a full IR engagement.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Before: Reconnaissance (active scanning, search engines for exposed services) and credential access (phishing T1566, credentials from password stores T1555, infostealer logs). Alongside: Valid Accounts T1078 (the account is the weapon) and Brute Force T1110. After: System Discovery, Remote Services for lateral movement (T1021), Account Manipulation for persistence (T1098), then Collection, Exfiltration, and Impact (ransomware T1486). Watching T1133 alerts is therefore watching the start of every worse thing that follows.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

Where threat intel comes from

📰OSINT reports and advisories

CISA joint advisories (#StopRansomware Citrix Bleed AA23-325A, Volt Typhoon AA24-038A), vendor blogs (Mandiant, Microsoft, Check Point, Recorded Future), and NVD/CVE pages for edge-appliance bugs. These give you IoCs, timelines, and exploit paths for free.

🎯ATT&CK-based intel

The MITRE group pages for T1133 (APT29 G0016, APT41 G0096, APT28 G0007, Sandworm G0034, FIN5 G0053, Wizard Spider G0102, Volt Typhoon G1017, and more) document exactly how each actor used this technique. Map your detections to them.

🌐Exposure and infra feeds

Shodan/Censys queries for your own netblocks (find forgotten VPN portals before attackers do), abuse.ch feeds, AlienVault OTX pulses, and KEV (CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) for appliance CVEs like CVE-2023-4966.

🏭Commercial feeds and internal telemetry

Commercial vendors add actor attribution and fresh infrastructure. Your best source is your own logs: prior spray campaigns, recurring source ASNs, and credential-stuffing patterns become baselines for hunts.

The intel cycle for this technique

  1. Define priorities. Decide which risks matter most: appliance CVEs on your fleet (T3), spray on your VPN (T1), token theft (T2).
  2. Collect. Pull CISA/vendor advisories, KEV feed, actor TTPs from ATT&CK pages, and your own auth telemetry.
  3. Process. Tag items by product and threat number (T1–T10); extract IPs, ASNs, user-agent strings, exploit paths.
  4. Analyze. Ask: which of our exposures match this? Which log fields would show it? Which accounts are targeted?
  5. Produce. Turn findings into Sigma rules, hunt queries, patch tickets, and blocklist changes.
  6. Feedback. Review alert hits and misses monthly; update thresholds and the threat list below.

5.4 Logs & Patterns

One pattern per threat number. Each block shows what the attack looks like in the raw log and a checklist of fields, thresholds, and joins that make a good alert.

Threat #1 — VPN credential brute force / password spray

The attacker tries a small set of passwords against many VPN usernames (or many passwords against one lucky account) from one or a few source IPs. FortiGate records each miss as ssl-login-fail. The giveaway is the join: the same remip hitting many user values — normal users mistype their own password, they do not type other people's.

  • Watch reason values: ssl_login_fail vs account-locked messages; lockout storms matter too.
  • Correlate with IdP: user logs in from VPN pool IP but IdP never saw MFA for that session.
Threat #2 — Session token theft + hijack (Citrix Bleed style)

Against a vulnerable NetScaler/ADC appliance, the attacker sends malformed HTTP requests that make the appliance leak about 1 KB of memory containing valid session tokens (CVE-2023-4966). Replaying a token resumes a logged-in user's session — no password, no MFA. In ns.log this appears as odd requests against the VPN vServer and later as activity under a session that never had a matching fresh authentication.

  • Join with firewall flows: hijacked session touching new internal subnets within minutes.

Threat #4 — Internet-exposed RDP brute force + type-10 logon

RDP facing the internet gets thousands of guesses per day. Windows writes every miss as Event 4625 and every success as 4624 with LogonType 10 (RemoteInteractive). A short failure storm from one external IP ending in a type-10 success is a foothold, even if the account was a valid one the attacker already knew.

Threat #5 — SSH brute force on internet-facing servers

Linux servers with SSH on the internet see constant guessing in auth.log. Most attempts fail on made-up usernames (invalid user). With any access at all, attackers often create their own way back in: a new account added to the VPN user group on a domain controller, or a new client certificate issued on the appliance. Learn from a real incident

Real-World Case Study

Every case in this guide is a T1133 case. The one below shows how a single unpatched appliance turned into three corporate breaches and a CISA advisory.

Citrix Bleed (CVE-2023-4966) — session-token theft at scale, 2023

In mid-2023, attackers found a very weak spot in a device almost every company trusts: the Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway — the appliance that terminates corporate VPN and app-delivery sessions. A critical information-disclosure bug (CVE-2023-4966, nicknamed Citrix Bleed) let anyone send a few malformed HTTP requests and receive about a kilobyte of the appliance's memory. That memory dump contained valid session tokens. Replaying a token resumed a logged-in user's session directly — no password, no MFA, no login event in the IdP. Mandiant responded to a Boeing intrusion that public reporting tied to the bug; Comcast disclosed an incident affecting Xfinity customer records; DP World Australia shut port operations; and CISA's joint #StopRansomware advisory (AA23-325A) tied LockBit 3.0 affiliates to the technique. A related NetScaler bug, CVE-2023-3519 (remote code execution on ADC/AAA), was used in the same period against critical infrastructure.

How the attack chain worked

  1. Target. Attackers picked companies running exposed NetScaler ADC/Gateway appliances as their corporate VPN door.
  2. Exploit. A few crafted requests triggered the memory leak — no authentication needed.
  3. Harvest. The response contained active session tokens for currently logged-in users.
  4. Hijack. Replaying a token recreated the user's session on the appliance — bypassing MFA completely.
  5. Land. The attacker was now "inside" with the user's access: Citrix apps, VPN routes, internal sites.
  6. Expand. Credential theft, share enumeration, and lateral movement followed (LockBit 3.0 affiliates moved to ransomware).

How it was detected

The decisive signal was what was missing: activity under a session that had no fresh authentication. Defenders saw ns.log sessions for users whose IdP had no matching sign-in, HEAD requests with absurd Host headers in appliance access logs, and users with two concurrent sessions from two countries. Mandiant's investigation of the Boeing incident and CISA's advisory then gave every SOC the exact exploit pattern to hunt for.

Containment

  1. Patch every NetScaler ADC/Gateway immediately — CISA's KEV listing forces a two-week federal patch clock.
  2. Kill all active and persistent sessions after patching ("kill switch"). Patching alone left stolen tokens valid.
  3. Check appliance logs for the malformed-request pattern; any hit means sessions were already stolen.
  4. Force password resets and re-enroll MFA for exposed users; review new accounts and certs (T7).
  5. Hunt backward for hijacked sessions: logins without auth events, concurrent sessions, new internal paths touched.

Recovery

  1. Rebuild trust in the remote-access path: re-issue certificates, update IdP integrations, verify conditional access.
  2. Monitor for re-entry for 30+ days with the Sigma rules from 5.5 enabled at low thresholds.
  3. Confirm exfiltration scope with legal/privacy teams — data exposure determined notification duties.
  4. Report to authorities (CISA/FBI for US victims) and regulators where personal data was involved.

Lessons learned

  • Edge appliances are P0 patch targets — give them the fastest SLA you have.
  • Patching is not eviction: always kill sessions and tokens after an appliance patch.
  • MFA is not magic: session-token theft (T1528-style) walks straight past it.
  • Appliance logs must reach the SIEM — the first evidence lived only in ns.log.
  • Join IdP + appliance + EDR in one timeline; no single source showed the attack.
  • Exploitation began before disclosure: KEV speed matters, and Shodan-style exposure checks help find forgotten portals.
  • Rehearse this scenario: a tabletop with "VPN compromised, no malware" finds process gaps fast.
Back to T1133

Citrix Bleed is T1133 in its purest form: the attackers did not break the door — they copied a key to a session someone else had opened. Every mitigation below either hardens the door (M1032, M1035, patching) or makes a stolen key useless fast (session revocation, monitoring). Re-read the T2 threat pattern in 5.3/5.4: this case is exactly that rule firing in real life.

Layered defenses that work

Mitigation & Defense

Defense in depth

No single control stops external remote service abuse. Layer identity, patching, network, and monitoring so that when one layer fails — and it will — the next one slows the attacker down enough for your detections to fire.

Enterprise defenses

🔑Access control (M1032, M1026)

Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 keys, platform passkeys) for every VPN/Citrix login — push-based MFA can be fatigued (T8). Wrap admin accounts in PAM with JIT access and kill standing VPN admin rights.

🩹Hardening & patching (M1051)

Treat edge appliances as P0: a 24–48 h patch SLA for Fortinet, Ivanti, Pulse, SonicWall, Citrix, and F5. Subscribe to vendor bulletins and the CISA KEV feed. After every appliance patch, kill all active sessions — Citrix Bleed proved tokens survive patches.

🧱Network controls (M1030, M1035)

Segment so a VPN session reaches only what its owner needs. Concentrate remote access in managed gateways, block direct RDP/VNC/SSH from the internet, and deny Tor traffic per M1021. Route vendor access through a brokered, recorded tunnel.

👀Monitoring & detection (AC-17(1), SI-4)

Ship appliance, IdP, MFA, and VPN logs to the SIEM. Deploy the 5.5 rules and watch the chains: failures→success, risky sign-ins, sessions without fresh auth. Test detections with purple-team exercises that replay the T1–T8 patterns.

🧾Policy & governance (M1042)

Inventory every remote service — including forgotten gateways and third-party tunnels (the APT41 pattern). Disable what has no owner; a service nobody patches is a door nobody locks. Annual access reviews for VPN groups.

🚫Identity hygiene & ZTNA

Block password reuse with breached-password screening, use geo/ASN conditional access (T6), and prefer ZTNA over full-tunnel VPN so a stolen session sees two apps, not the network. Disable accounts within hours of offboarding.

🔐Session & token security (M1041)

Short session lifetimes, device-binding of tokens where the platform supports it, and TLS everywhere in transit. Encrypt logs and secrets on the appliances themselves so a leak like T2/T3 yields less.

🧯Response readiness

Keep a kill-sessions playbook per appliance type, rehearse the Citrix Bleed tabletop, and pre-agree who can force password resets tenant-wide. Backup and restoration plans cover the ransomware that often follows (T1486).

Home user defenses

  • Use a unique, long password for your work VPN account — stored in a password manager, never reused.
  • Turn on MFA everywhere; prefer app-based or key-based over SMS.
  • Install updates for your router, laptop, and phone promptly — home gear is part of the remote path.
  • Never approve an MFA push you did not trigger; deny and report it to IT.
  • Do not run company work on shared or public computers; a stolen session starts there.
  • Keep your company's endpoint protection (EDR/AV) active and updated on the device you VPN from.
  • Be careful with remote-job offers and "IT support" calls asking for VPN credentials — classic pretexts.
  • Report a lost laptop or phone used for MFA within minutes, not days.

Best practices for day-to-day SOC work

  1. Hunt on a hypothesis. Every month pick one threat (T1–T10), write the hunt query, run it over 90 days, and record what you found or proved clean.
  2. Purple-test the Sigma rules. Replay the 5.4 log samples through your pipeline and confirm each rule fires; keep the results as audit evidence (ISO A.8.16, SOC 2 CC7.2).
  3. Close the feedback loop. When an analyst rejects an alert, capture why; tune the rule within the week or retire it.
  4. Document your baseline. Known office IPs, expected countries, standard VPN hours — detection quality is a data problem first.
  5. Track coverage metrics. Which of T1–T10 have a deployed detection? MTTD for the front-door technique should be minutes, not days.
  6. Drill the kill-switch. Practice killing all sessions on your VPN in a maintenance window until it is boring and fast.
  7. Keep the actor map current. When ATT&CK updates T1133 procedure examples, check whether your rules would see that actor's exact steps.
You can win the front door

External remote services are the most predictable attack path in your environment — which makes them the most detectable. Layer MFA, fast patching, and the chains from section 5, and a stolen password becomes a 20-minute alert instead of a 30-day breach. Every login you can explain is one an attacker cannot hide in.
