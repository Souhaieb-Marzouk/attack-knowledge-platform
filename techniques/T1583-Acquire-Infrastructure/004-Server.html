MITRE ATT&CK · SUB-TECHNIQUE OF T1583

T1583.004 Server

The adversary buys or rents whole servers: dedicated bare-metal machines, full cloud servers, or space at a bulletproof hosting provider (a hoster that ignores abuse complaints for a premium fee). These big, long-lived machines later run botnet panels, malware repositories, spam campaigns, DDoS control, criminal forums, and leak sites. This guide explains the pattern in plain language and shows the reputation-based detections that work in a SOC.

Overview

What "acquiring a server" means in ATT&CK terms, who does it, and why a rented machine can be the quiet start of a major incident.

ATT&CK T1583.004 covers the moment an adversary buys, leases, or rents whole servers that they configure and run themselves. ATT&CK describes it as obtaining "physical servers that can be used during targeting", for staging, launching, and executing operations. The server may host a watering-hole page, send phishing mail, or act as command and control. In practice, the machines fall into three groups. The first is dedicated bare-metal servers rented by the month. The second is full cloud servers, sometimes abused through free trial periods, as ATT&CK notes. The third is capacity at bulletproof hosting providers.

A bulletproof hoster is a hosting company that, for a higher price, promises not to act on abuse complaints. When a victim, a researcher, or a security company reports a malicious server, the hoster ignores the ticket, asks for "proof", or quietly moves the customer to a new IP range. This makes bulletproof capacity attractive for botnet panels, malware distribution, spam operations, DDoS control, criminal forums, and leak sites — anything that draws reports fast.

T1583.003 vs T1583.004 — why the split matters

The parent technique T1583 has one sub-technique for small rented slices and this one for whole machines. The difference is size, lifetime, and intent.

AspectT1583.003 Virtual Private ServerT1583.004 Server (this page)
What is rentedA small VM slice on a shared hostA whole machine, a full cloud server, or bulletproof capacity
Typical lifetimeHours to days, then discardedWeeks to months, sometimes years
Typical roleScanner, proxy hop, single payload dropC2 panel, malware repository, spam hub, DDoS controller, forum, leak site
Abuse handlingMainstream hoster, abuses get actionedAbuse-tolerant or fully bulletproof
Detection angleDomain/VM churn analyticsReputation feeds, ASN intel, long-lived IOC tracking

What the server is used for

  • C2 panels — the console that sends commands to botnets and backdoors.
  • Malware repositories — second-stage payloads, tooling, and updates served at scale.
  • Spam operations — mail-out infrastructure with rotation across IPs.
  • DDoS command — controllers that steer attack traffic at targets.
  • Criminal forums and leak sites — marketplaces and extortion pages that must stay online.
  • Staging and testing — a private lab where the adversary builds and tests tooling.

In the ATT&CK attack chain this is Resource Development (the tactic where attackers build capabilities before touching victims). A server acquired here later enables Command and Control (T1071 family), Phishing (T1566), Drive-by Compromise (T1189), and Network Denial of Service (T1498). The purchase itself leaves no trace inside your network. Detection therefore leans on outside intelligence: reputation feeds, abuse reports from the community, and ASN enrichment. An ASN (autonomous system number) is the ID of the network operator that owns an IP range.

Note

Not every rented server is malicious. Cloud providers, game servers, VPN endpoints, and CDNs all live in hosting IP space. The goal of this page is never "block all hosting ASNs" — that breaks the business. The goal is to know which hosting ranges carry bad reputation today, and to watch egress to them with context.

Animated Simulation

Watch the full bulletproof-hosting story: renting the machine, abusing it, and the reputation-hit moment the SOC can act on.

Step 1 of 8: The attacker asks on a criminal forum for a bulletproof hoster and gets three offers. The selling point: abuse tickets get ignored.

SOC view

Nothing is visible inside your estate yet. The deal, the forum thread, and the hosting order all sit outside your visibility; dark-web monitoring is the only early sensor.

Evidence left behind: forum contact; hosting offer thread
Written version of all steps (accessibility)
  1. 1. Find hoster. The attacker asks on a criminal forum for a bulletproof hoster and gets offers. The selling point: abuse tickets get ignored.
  2. 2. Pay in crypto. They pay in cryptocurrency for a dedicated server under an abuse-tolerant policy. The hoster asks no business questions.
  3. 3. Deploy panel. The hoster provisions the machine and the attacker installs a C2 panel (the console that commands infected machines) and a malware repository.
  4. 4. Beacons arrive. Infected machines inside the victim organization connect to the panel on a schedule. Each short session is a beacon.
  5. 5. Launch campaign. The attacker uses the same server to launch a spam and download campaign against the victim organization.
  6. 6. Payload download. A user downloads the payload that the server hosts. The file is served straight from the dedicated machine.
  7. 7. Reputation hit. The SOC’s reputation feed flags the server IP as part of a Spamhaus- or CINS-listed range. The egress block alert fires.
  8. 8. Share and watch. The SOC shares the indicators with the community, files an abuse report (the hoster ignores it), and keeps the whole IP range under watch.

The players

  • Attacker — shops for capacity on criminal forums, pays in crypto, and runs everything from one control host.
  • Bulletproof hoster — sells dedicated servers and, for a premium, does not act on abuse complaints.
  • Dedicated server — the rented bare-metal machine. It runs the C2 panel and serves the malware repository.
  • Victim org — the target company. Infected hosts beacon out; a user downloads the payload.
  • SOC — the security operations center. It joins egress events with reputation feeds and blocks what comes back dirty.

What to watch

  • Egress to hosting IP space — especially servers, raw ports, and odd user agents, from workstations.
  • Reputation-feed joins — any IP on your egress path that appears in Spamhaus, CINS Army, or FireHOL lists is worth an alert.
  • Mail origin analytics — bulk inbound mail whose sending IPs sit in dedicated-hosting ASNs with poor reputation.
  • Long-lived single IPs — a dedicated server keeps one IP for months, unlike a VPS slice; stable dirty IPs are high-value IOCs (indicators of compromise).
  • Abuse silence — if an abuse ticket to a hoster never gets answered, the range itself deserves a watchlist entry.

Red Team Perspective

How operators think about whole-server acquisition: what they buy, why, and what it costs them. Content here is conceptual, plus one lab-only example.

When and why attackers use whole servers

Small VPS slices are disposable. Some jobs need more: a botnet panel must stay reachable for months; a leak site must survive takedown pressure; a spam operation needs stable IPs that major providers have not yet listed. A dedicated or bulletproof server offers capacity, a stable address, and — most valuable of all — an operator who will not act on the reports that these services always attract.

Paying with cryptocurrency or prepaid cards, as ATT&CK notes for Kimsuky, keeps the purchase away from the attacker’s identity. Some groups lease through resellers instead of touching the upstream hoster, which adds a layer between them and the infrastructure record (a pattern ATT&CK attributes to Sandworm Team).

How the acquisition works (conceptual)

  1. Find capacity. Ask on a criminal forum, check "bulletproof" classified listings, or simply buy from a mainstream provider that does not verify customers.
  2. Pay anonymously. Crypto, prepaid vouchers, or a rented account from a reseller.
  3. Configure. Install the panel or repository, front it with a domain and TLS certificate, and set up rotation between IPs if the hoster offers several ranges.
  4. Harden against takedown. Spread roles across machines, keep backups of the panel database, and be ready to migrate to a fresh range when the old one gets listed.

Common variations

Bare-metal dedicated

A full physical machine from a hosting company. Best performance and a stable IP; the go-to for C2 panels and malware repositories. Kimsuky reportedly paid with virtual currency and prepaid cards for exactly this kind of hosting.

Bulletproof bundle

Capacity sold specifically as abuse-tolerant, often bundled with a friendly registrar. Used for spam hubs, DDoS-for-hire control, phishing kits, and leak sites. The hoster ignores complaints or demands unpayable "proof".

Reseller and free-trial abuse

Lease through a small reseller so the upstream hoster never sees the end customer, or run servers on free cloud trial periods, as the ATT&CK description points out. Cheaper, shorter-lived, but harder to attribute.

Warning

Ethical use only. Everything above describes adversary tradecraft so defenders can recognize it. Red teams may simulate the detection side — for example, confirming that their own reputation-feed join fires — but must never rent real bulletproof capacity, run real botnet panels, or touch systems they do not own. Lab simulations run on isolated hardware with synthetic data only.

Prerequisites an attacker needs

  • Funds and anonymity — crypto or vouchers, plus a persona that matches the forum seller’s expectations.
  • Operational skill — the ability to harden Linux, run a panel, manage domains and certificates, and keep the machine patched enough to avoid casual takeover.
  • OpSec discipline — separate control host, no reuse of personal identities or payment methods across servers.
  • Patience for cost — bulletproof capacity is far more expensive per month than a VPS slice; the operator expects the server to earn it back.

Lab-only example — inventory your own lab server

Red and blue teams both benefit from one honest picture of a lab machine’s exposed services. The snippet below runs on a server you own and simply lists listening sockets, their processes, and the owning users. Defenders can flip it: the same view from an unknown host would be a fine starting point for triage.

bash: lab_server_inventory.sh (run only on hosts you own)
# Lab-only example - never use against systems you do not own.
# List every listening socket with the process and user that owns it.
ss -tulpn
# Show which of those ports are reachable from outside (from a second lab host):
nmap -p- --open 192.168.56.20
# Record the result in the lab register so every exposed service has an owner:
ss -tulpn | awk 'NR>1 {print $5, $7}' | sort -u >> lab_register.txt

Blue Team Perspective

What the acquisition of a whole server looks like from inside the estate, which artifacts it leaves, and a triage flow that ends in a decision.

You will almost never see the purchase. The hoster, the payment, and the forum thread all happen outside your estate. What reaches your logs is the use of the server: egress sessions from an infected host, payload downloads through the proxy, inbound spam from its range, or scan traffic from its IP. That means the blue-team entry point is normally a join — your own network and mail telemetry enriched with outside knowledge about who owns an IP range and what its reputation is.

Artifacts the server leaves in your telemetry

ArtifactWhere you see itWhy it matters
Egress flow to a hosting IP on an unusual portFirewall and NetFlow recordsWorkstations that talk to raw hosting IPs on admin-style ports rarely do so for a good reason
Proxy download of an executable from the serverProxy / Zeek HTTP logsThe delivery moment of the campaign; the URL and hash become IOCs
Beacon pattern to one stable hosting IPEgress logs over hours or daysDedicated servers keep one IP for months, so the pattern is stable enough to alert and block
Inbound bulk mail from the same rangeMail gateway logsSpam operations reuse ranges; sending-IP reputation joins catch whole campaigns
Reputation-feed hit on the IPIntel platform, feed dashboardsSpamhaus, CINS Army, or FireHOL listings are community proof of abuse
Abuse ticket with no hoster replyYour abuse workflowSilence is a signal: the range behaves like bulletproof space

Where to look

  • Egress and firewall logs — join destination IPs against Spamhaus SBL/DROP-style lists, CINS Army, and FireHOL level1 every hour.
  • Proxy logs — flag executable downloads whose host resolves into dedicated-hosting ASNs, and bare-IP hosts without a domain.
  • Mail gateway logs — count inbound messages per sending IP; senders in hosting ASNs that exceed normal bulk volumes deserve review.
  • EDR network events — which process opened the connection matters more than which host; browsers downloading an .exe from raw IP space is a classic.
  • Intel platform — your own curated lists: bulletproof ASNs, listed ranges, forum-advertised hosters, and leaked panel domains from vendor reporting.

Why it matters

A single dedicated server can host the panel that commands every infected machine in your estate and the repository that serves their second stages. Blocking one IP at the egress firewall can, in that situation, cut the attacker’s control channel and stop re-infection at the same time. That is why reputation-based egress defense is one of the highest-value cheap controls a SOC can own — and why the mistakes section later warns against doing it crudely.

Triage flow

  1. Confirm the join. Check that the flagged IP is really on the feed version you ingested, and note the list name (SBL, CINS, FireHOL, abuse.ch) — the list type tells you the abuse class.
  2. Find the process. Pull the EDR network event behind the flow. A browser or Office process reaching a hosting IP on port 8080 is a different problem from a backup agent reaching its storage vendor.
  3. Scope the pattern. Query 7–30 days of flows to that IP and its /24. Beacons repeat; look for the same interval and similar byte counts.
  4. Check the mail lane. Ask whether the same range sent you mail recently. A spam hit and a beacon from one range is a strong campaign signal.
  5. Decide the block. Block the IP (or range, with an allowlist review) at the egress firewall and the proxy; document which business services share the range so the block is safe.
  6. Report and share. File the abuse report even if the hoster is expected to ignore it — reports feed the listing systems — and push the IP, domain, and hash to your intel platform and your ISAC.
Good practice

Join first, block second. The most reliable reputation-driven detections are joins: your own telemetry matched against a maintained feed, with the list name and age attached to the alert. Pure feed ingestion without context produces alert floods; pure tuning without feeds produces blindness.

Danger

Do not blanket-block whole ASNs on day one. Large hosting networks carry your backups, your SaaS vendors, and half the internet’s software downloads. Block listed ranges and IPs with allowlists and a documented rollback, and reserve whole-ASN decisions for a formal risk review.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

Because the acquisition happens outside your estate, intelligence is your primary sensor. The sources below are ordered from free and automatic to curated and paid; a mature setup uses several at once and joins them against internal telemetry.

Reputation feeds

Spamhaus SBL/DBL/DROP-style listings, CINS Army, and FireHOL community blocklists aggregate observed abuse per IP and range. They are the backbone of the joins in this page.

abuse.ch and Shadowserver

abuse.ch tracks botnet C2 and payload URLs (URLhaus, ThreatFox, Feodo Tracker); Shadowserver reports reachable services and sinkhole telemetry. Both catch panel and repository servers directly.

Vendor research

Unit 42, Talos, and similar teams publish studies of bulletproof-hosting ecosystems, including which providers tolerate which abuse classes. Their IOC appendices feed watchlists for months.

Dark-web monitoring

Services (or your own analysts) watch criminal forums and marketplaces where hosters advertise. A fresh "bulletproof dedicated" thread is an early, cheap indicator of new ranges.

ISACs and peers

Sector ISACs and trusted peer groups exchange IPs and panel domains that were seen live. Sector context matters: the same range can spam one sector while serving a targeted campaign in another.

Internal reputation joins

Your own history is a source: IPs you blocked, ranges with repeated abuse tickets, and downloaded hashes. Joins of internal egress against external feeds catch what vendors have not yet listed.

Collect, analyze, apply

1. Collect

Ingest feeds on a schedule (hourly for IP lists, daily for research IOCs). Keep the source name, list type, and first-seen date on every record so alerts can explain themselves.

2. Analyze

Enrich: resolve ASN, country, and hosting class for each IP; group by range; compare against your allowlists. Age matters — a listing from yesterday is hot, a listing from last year may be recycled.

3. Apply

Push into the proxy, firewall, and SIEM lookups; alert on joins with internal telemetry; block the worst class immediately; share what you confirm with your ISAC.

Example event 1 — Zeek HTTP record of a payload download from a hosting IP

A workstation fetched an executable straight from a bare hosting IP. The proxy log below is the delivery moment of the whole campaign; the fields worth reading are called out underneath.

log: zeek http record (JSON)
{
  "ts": 1767139200.412,
  "uid": "Cz8fHQ3xYvP2mKdL1e",
  "id.orig_h": "10.22.7.41",
  "id.orig_p": 51344,
  "id.resp_h": "185.220.101.44",
  "id.resp_p": 8080,
  "host": "185.220.101.44:8080",
  "uri": "/dl/loader_v2.exe",
  "method": "GET",
  "user_agent": "Mozilla/5.0 (Windows NT 10.0; Win64; x64)",
  "status_code": 200,
  "resp_body_len": 2457600,
  "resp_mime_types": ["application/x-msdownload"]
}
  • id.orig_h 10.22.7.41 — an internal workstation, not a server; it had no business reason to fetch software from raw IP space.
  • id.resp_h 185.220.101.44 — the dedicated server. In this lab scenario the IP is on a CINS-listed range; the field becomes the join key against the reputation feed.
  • id.resp_p 8080 — a non-standard web port, common for panel and repository servers that avoid corporate-friendly ports.
  • uri /dl/loader_v2.exe — an executable path on a bare-IP host; combined with the port this alone justifies a proxy alert.
  • resp_mime_types application/x-msdownload — the server confirmed the body really is a Windows executable, not an HTML error page.

  • EventID 3 — Sysmon’s network-connection event; every outbound TCP session from a process appears here once.

5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

One complete Sigma rule and one matching Splunk SPL query for each of the top six threats. Every pair follows the same logic so you can port either direction. Field names marked as enrichment fields (for example DestinationAsnClass) are added by your intel pipeline before indexing; each pair states its assumptions.

T1 — Egress connection to a bulletproof or dedicated-hosting ASN

The anchor detection of this page. It fires when any host process opens a connection whose destination ASN has been classified as bulletproof or dedicated hosting, and the destination is not internal address space.

sigma: egress_bulletproof_asn_connection.yml
title: Windows Host Connecting to Bulletproof or Dedicated-Hosting ASN
id: 3f2a9c14-7b6d-4e52-9a41-c8d0f3b2e7a9
status: stable
description: >
  Detects an endpoint process opening a network connection whose destination IP
  belongs to an ASN classified as bulletproof or dedicated hosting by the intel
  enrichment pipeline. Such infrastructure is commonly acquired under MITRE
  ATT&CK T1583.004 to run C2 panels and malware repositories.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1583/004/
  - https://www.spamhaus.org/sbl/
  - https://cinsscore.com/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2025-11-20
logsource:
  category: network_connection
  product: windows
  service: sysmon
detection:
  selection:
    EventID: 3
    DestinationAsnClass|contains:
      - 'bulletproof-hosting'
      - 'dedicated-hosting'
  filter_private:
    DestinationIp|startswith:
      - '10.'
      - '192.168.'
      - '172.16.'
      - '127.'
  condition: selection and not filter_private
fields:
  - EventID
  - Image
  - User
  - DestinationIp
  - DestinationPort
  - DestinationAsnClass
falsepositives:
  - Approved backup, replication, or RMM tooling that runs in the same hosting ASN
  - A business partner SaaS hosted on dedicated servers without its own ASN
level: high
tags:
  - attack.resource_development
  - attack.t1583
  - attack.t1583.004
spl: egress_bulletproof_asn_connection.spl
index=edr sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational EventID=3
  DestinationAsnClass IN ("bulletproof-hosting", "dedicated-hosting")
  NOT (DestinationIp=10.* OR DestinationIp=192.168.* OR DestinationIp=172.16.* OR DestinationIp=127.*)
| stats count AS sessions, dc(DestinationIp) AS dest_ips,
        earliest(_time) AS first_seen, latest(_time) AS last_seen,
        values(DestinationPort) AS ports
  by Image, DestinationIp, DestinationAsnClass
| sort - sessions

Field assumptions: Sysmon events are indexed as XML in index=edr; DestinationAsnClass is written by an enrichment pipeline that tags each destination IP with its ASN class using CINS Army, FireHOL level1, and Spamhaus exports.

  • Mapping: ATT&CK T1583.004 (Resource Development) observed through its operational consequence; pair with T1071 for the C2 use.
  • False positives: sanctioned tools hosted in the same ASN; keep a reviewed allowlist of business ASNs and ports in the enrichment field.
  • Tuning: add DestinationPort context (8080/8443/raw ports are noisier) and raise to Critical when Image is a user-writable path.

T2 — Executable download from a raw hosting IP or hosting ASN

The delivery moment. The proxy sees a fetch of an executable either straight from a bare IP or from a host whose ASN class is hosting. Either branch alone justifies a look.

sigma: proxy_exec_from_hosting_ip.yml
title: Executable Download from Raw Hosting IP via Proxy
id: 8c51d0e6-2f4a-4b7d-8e93-5a6c1d2b4f08
status: stable
description: >
  Detects a proxy transaction that downloads an executable either from a bare
  IP address host or from a host whose destination ASN is classified as
  bulletproof or dedicated hosting. Malware distribution servers rented under
  ATT&CK T1583.004 serve payloads exactly this way.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1583/004/
  - https://urlhaus.abuse.ch/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2025-11-27
logsource:
  category: proxy
  product: zeek
  service: http
detection:
  selection_exec:
    uri|endswith:
      - '.exe'
      - '.msi'
      - '.scr'
      - '.vbs'
      - '.hta'
  selection_bare_ip:
    host|re: '^[0-9]{1,3}\.[0-9]{1,3}\.[0-9]{1,3}\.[0-9]{1,3}(:[0-9]+)?$'
  selection_asn:
    DestinationAsnClass|contains:
      - 'bulletproof-hosting'
      - 'dedicated-hosting'
  filter_status:
    status_code:
      - 200
      - 206
  condition: selection_exec and (selection_bare_ip or selection_asn) and filter_status
fields:
  - id.orig_h
  - host
  - uri
  - method
  - user_agent
  - status_code
  - resp_body_len
falsepositives:
  - Internal update servers published by bare IP; allowlist them explicitly
  - Developer or QA tooling that fetches builds from team VMs on hosting ASNs
level: high
tags:
  - attack.resource_development
  - attack.t1583
  - attack.t1583.004
spl: proxy_exec_from_hosting_ip.spl
index=proxy sourcetype=zeek:http.log
| eval bare_ip=if(match(host, "^[0-9]{1,3}\.[0-9]{1,3}\.[0-9]{1,3}\.[0-9]{1,3}"), 1, 0)
| where bare_ip=1 OR DestinationAsnClass IN ("bulletproof-hosting", "dedicated-hosting")
| where match(uri, "(?i)\.(exe|msi|scr|vbs|hta)$") AND status_code IN (200, 206)
| stats count AS downloads, dc(id.orig_h) AS src_hosts, values(id.orig_h) AS hosts,
        max(resp_body_len) AS max_bytes
  by host, uri
| sort - downloads

Field assumptions: Zeek HTTP data in index=proxy sourcetype=zeek:http.log; host carries the Host header; DestinationAsnClass is enrichment added at ingest. If your proxy lacks ASN enrichment, replace that branch with a lookup: | lookup hosting_asn.csv dest_ip AS id.resp_h OUTPUT asn_class, assuming a maintained lookup exported from your intel platform.

  • Mapping: the payload-delivery half of T1583.004 abuse; combine with T1204 (user execution) for the chain.
  • False positives: bare-IP update servers and IT-run software mirrors; maintain an explicit allowlist of those hosts.
  • Tuning: add resp_mime_types equals application/x-msdownload as a stronger signal, and alert separately on first-seen hosts.

T3 — Bulk inbound mail from poor-reputation hosting ASNs

Spam infrastructure rented under T1583.004 shows up as volume: many messages per sending IP, from IP space classified as dedicated or bulletproof hosting. The mail gateway is the natural sensor.

sigma: esa_bulk_mail_from_hosting_asn.yml
title: Bulk Inbound Mail from Sending IPs in Poor-Reputation Hosting ASNs
id: a1b2c3d4-e5f6-47a8-9b0c-1d2e3f4a5b6c
status: test
description: >
  Detects high message volumes from individual sending IPs whose ASN is
  classified as bulletproof or dedicated hosting. Servers bought under ATT&CK
  T1583.004 are a standard origin for phishing and malspam campaigns.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1583/004/
  - https://www.spamhaus.org/dbl/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2025-12-04
logsource:
  category: email
  product: cisco
  service: esa
detection:
  selection_asn:
    sender_asn_class|contains:
      - 'bulletproof-hosting'
      - 'dedicated-hosting'
  filter_spf:
    spf_result: 'pass'
  condition: selection_asn and not filter_spf | count(sender_ip) by sender_ip >= 100
  timeframe: 1h
fields:
  - sender_ip
  - envelope_from
  - sender_asn_class
  - spf_result
  - verdict
falsepositives:
  - Legitimate marketing platforms that sit in hosting ASNs without dedicated mail infrastructure
  - Partner bulk notifications such as invoices or status digests sent from rented servers
level: high
tags:
  - attack.resource_development
  - attack.t1583
  - attack.t1583.004
spl: esa_bulk_mail_from_hosting_asn.spl
index=email sourcetype=cisco:esa
| lookup hosting_asn.csv sender_ip OUTPUT asn_class
| where asn_class IN ("bulletproof-hosting", "dedicated-hosting") AND spf_result!="pass"
| bin _time span=1h
| stats count AS messages, dc(recipient) AS recipients,
        values(envelope_from) AS senders
  by _time, sender_ip, asn_class
| where messages >= 100
| sort - messages

Field assumptions: Cisco ESA mail logs in index=email sourcetype=cisco:esa; sender_asn_class in Sigma equals the result of the same enrichment the SPL does with lookup hosting_asn.csv, a maintained lookup exported from your intel platform that maps IPs to ASN class.

  • Mapping: T1583.004 as the acquisition of the sending infrastructure; the delivered mail maps to T1566 (Phishing).
  • False positives: bulk senders on shared hosting with clean SPF (filtered by the SPF pass check); still review any survivor.
  • Tuning: lower the threshold for first-seen sending IPs, and add a DKIM fail condition for ranges that rotate rapidly.

T4 — Scan patterns from dedicated-hosting ranges on the perimeter firewall

Scanners and DDoS controllers live on exactly this kind of hosting. A burst of denied ports from one external source in hosting space is the cheap early warning.

T6 — Failed logon bursts from dedicated-hosting sources

Password spraying and brute force from rented servers is a daily event on exposed endpoints. The burst, not the single failure, is the signal; the hosting-ASN class is what removes the office and VPN noise.

Real-World Case Study

FinFisher / FinSpy: commercial spyware whose command servers Citizen Lab mapped worldwide through scanning studies published in 2013 and 2014 — a public picture of the T1583.004 pattern.

FinFisher (also known as FinSpy) is a commercial intrusion and surveillance suite. A company produced it and sold it to government clients. Citizen Lab documented its use against civil society: activists, journalists, and opposition figures — including, as reported, members of the Ethiopian diaspora. In global scanning studies published in 2013 and 2014, Citizen Lab mapped the spyware’s command-and-control presence. Its 2013 scan identified 36 active FinSpy command-and-control servers; taken together with earlier scans, servers had appeared in 25 countries. The follow-up study reported 33 likely government users across 32 countries, with many servers standing on ordinary ISP and dedicated hosting, some fronted by proxies. The case is a public demonstration of T1583.004 behavior: infrastructure bought per server, per role, per country, and kept alive because abuse reports did not stick.

  • Background

    A commercial spyware market

    FinFisher was sold as a "lawful intercept" product. Citizen Lab and partners had already reported FinSpy samples against Bahraini and Ethiopian-linked targets in earlier years, so a research community existed that knew what to look for.

  • 2013–2014

    Attack chain with dedicated hosting

    FinSpy implants were delivered (as reported) through phishing emails and fake software updates, then implants beaconed out to command servers. Citizen Lab scanned the family’s known ports and banners and surfaced 36 active command servers. Servers had appeared in 25 countries in total — a presence only possible with rented, dedicated, per-role machines, the T1583.004 pattern.

  • 2013–2014

    Detection point — researchers publish

    Citizen Lab published the scan results in 2013 and 2014. The 2013 report was You Only Click Twice; the follow-up study was Pay No Attention to the Server Behind the Proxy. Both included server lists, fingerprints, and scan methodology. The publication itself was the detection: any defender could now hunt for connections to the listed command IPs.

  • After publication

    Containment at targeted orgs

    Organizations and service providers that ingested the report could block the listed IPs, search egress logs for past connections, and submit the indicators to reputation feeds and blocklists.

  • After disclosure

    Eradication and hoster action

    Later scans showed some command servers going dark or moving after the reports (as reported), which is the ecosystem’s version of eradication. Operators responded by migrating to fresh ranges — illustrating both the value and the limits of one-time blocks.

  • Ongoing

    Recovery and monitoring

    For targeted civil-society organizations, "recovery" meant re-imaging devices, rotating credentials, and watching for re-infection; for the research community it meant keeping the C2 IP watchlist current as servers moved.

Impact and consequences

The impact was not measured in encrypted files but in people: surveillance of journalists, activists, and diaspora members, with the operational security of whole networks compromised. The financial engine — a commercial vendor selling capability — meant the infrastructure was professionally maintained and quickly replaced. That is the sober part for defenders: when the buyer treats servers as consumables, reputation-based detection has to be continuous, not a one-time project.

Team response and communication

Citizen Lab coordinated with affected parties, published reproducible scan evidence, and shared indicators broadly rather than keeping them private. Vendors and newsrooms amplified the findings; some providers were contacted with evidence. The communication pattern is worth copying inside your own organization: publish the indicator list, the detection logic, and the verification method together.

Lessons learned
  • Hosting-reputation monitoring matters even for targeted orgs. The servers were "out there", but the beacons from victims were not.
  • Intel sharing exposes hidden infrastructure. One research report turned private beacons into blockable, huntable events worldwide.
  • Keep C2 IP watchlists current. Operators migrate; a stale list quietly stops firing. Tie every list to an owner and a refresh date.
  • Abuse-tolerant hosting is a system property. Plan for the hoster to ignore the report, and make feed listings — not takedowns — your first defensive move.

Mitigation and Defense

You cannot stop an adversary from renting a server, but you can make their server useless against you: reputation-aware filtering, enriched monitoring, and a working response loop.

Policy

Write down who may block which infrastructure ranges, and require an allowlist review plus a rollback step for every ASN-level decision. Name an owner for threat-intel triage so reputation hits never wait in a shared mailbox.

Network

Wire reputation feeds (Spamhaus SBL/DROP-class data, CINS Army, FireHOL level1) into the proxy and firewall, with a maintained allowlist. Prefer blocking listed IPs and /24s over whole ASNs.

Threat intel

Enrich every egress, proxy, mail, and auth event with ASN class and reputation labels at ingest. Vendor research on bulletproof ecosystems (Unit 42, Talos) feeds the watchlists; abuse.ch and Shadowserver cover live C2.

Monitoring

Run the 5.5 detections continuously. Add dark-web and panel monitoring (own capability or a vendor) so a new "bulletproof dedicated" offer becomes a watchlist entry before it becomes an incident.

Email

Gate inbound mail on sending-IP reputation and ASN class, not only on content scores. Bulk volume from hosting ASNs with failing SPF belongs in quarantine by default.

Cloud and identity

Treat VPN and SaaS logins as the brute-force target they are: rate-limit by source ASN, alert on failure bursts from hosting ranges, and prefer phishing-resistant MFA so a sprayed password is not enough.

Logging

Keep Zeek or equivalent flow data, proxy logs, firewall deny logs, and Sysmon EID 3 for at least 90 days hot. Reputation joins are retroactive — yesterday’s logs answer today’s feed listing.

DDoS readiness

Controllers for availability attacks live on this kind of hosting. Hold a scrubbing retainer before the incident and test runbook steps quarterly; buying scrubbing during an attack costs hours you will not have.

Response

Maintain an abuse-report workflow that files, tracks, and records hoster replies. Silence from a hoster is itself intelligence — mark the range and share it. Keep IOC expiry dates so watchlists stay honest.

Home and small office

Small networks meet the same servers through spam and malicious downloads rather than targeted campaigns. Standard protections carry most of the weight. Keep the router firmware current, and use the security features of your DNS resolver or security suite. Let your operating system’s built-in reputation filtering block known-bad downloads. Turn on multi-factor authentication for email and banking. Back up important files to a service that keeps versions. If a device behaves strangely — slow traffic to unknown services, or pop-ups from odd sites — disconnect it and ask for help before paying anyone anything.

Key facts

ATT&CK ID
T1583.004 — Acquire Infrastructure: Server (sub-technique of T1583)
One-liner
The adversary buys or rents whole servers — dedicated bare-metal, full cloud, or bulletproof hosting — to stage and run operations
Typical roles
C2 panels, malware repositories, spam hubs, DDoS controllers, criminal forums, leak sites
Key distinction
T1583.003 = small disposable VPS slice; T1583.004 = whole, longer-lived, higher-capacity machines

Response checklist

  • Validate the feed hit: list name, list type, and listing age.
  • Identify the process and user behind the egress event (Sysmon EID 3).
  • Scope 7–30 days of traffic to the IP and its /24 for beacon patterns.
  • Check the mail lane for campaigns from the same range.
  • Block the IP or /24 at proxy and firewall with an allowlist review and rollback note.
  • Search endpoint telemetry for payloads downloaded from the server; hash and quarantine.
  • File the abuse report and record the hoster’s reply (or silence).
  • Share confirmed indicators with your ISAC and update internal watchlists with expiry dates.
