MITRE ATT&CK · INITIAL ACCESS

T1200 Hardware Additions

Not every intrusion starts over the Internet. With brief physical access, an attacker can plug in a tiny computer, a fake keyboard, or a keylogger - and the network simply sees a new device. This page explains hardware-based entry and how to catch it.

  • Tactic: Initial Access
  • Platforms: Windows, Linux, macOS
  • Added: 2018 (v2) · checked vs ATT&CK v20
  • Guide version: 2.0
  • Data sources: Device/USB events, DHCP, NAC/switch logs
  • Detection difficulty: Hard (physical vector)

What it is

Malicious hardware introduced with physical access: implants, BadUSB, keyloggers, rogue access points.

Primary risk

Inside-the-network access that bypasses every Internet-facing defense.

Where it happens

Lobbies, conference rooms, shared desks, data centers, hotels, supply deliveries.

Detection difficulty

The network sees an unknown MAC; hosts see a new USB device - if those logs exist.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    What hardware additions are, why they bypass the perimeter, and what this page teaches.

    The idea in plain language

    Hardware additions are devices an attacker physically introduces to gain access. Four families matter. A network implant is a tiny computer (Raspberry Pi class) hidden near a wall port that tunnels into the network from inside. A BadUSB / HID emulator is a USB stick that pretends to be a keyboard and types commands in seconds. A hardware keylogger sits between keyboard and PC and records keystrokes. A rogue access point is a small Wi-Fi box that offers its own, attacker-controlled network (which connects to T1669 Wi-Fi Networks).

    All four need one thing that cyber defenses cannot grant or revoke: physical access. That access can come from an insider, a visitor, a contractor, a cleaning window, or a supply delivery.

    Why it matters

    Firewalls, email filters, and phishing training do not apply. The implant starts inside, where the network is flat and trusting. Detection depends on operational telemetry most organizations neglect: DHCP leases, switch port security, NAC denials, and USB device events on hosts. These controls are cheap compared to the risk they close.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    T1200 is an Initial Access technique. After planting, attackers usually establish C2, capture credentials, or pivot to Wi-Fi attacks. It pairs with T1091 when media replication is used, and with T1056.001 when keyloggers capture input.

    Animated simulation

    A visitor plants a matchbox-sized network implant. Every step creates network evidence - if anyone is watching.

    Step 1/8
    What is happening

    Defender hint

    The same story in text

    1. Entry. The attacker walks in with a visitor badge and friendly confidence. Defender hint: visitor policy matters - escorts and badge-scoped zones shrink the attack surface.
    2. Planting. In an unattended conference room, a matchbox-sized implant goes into a live wall port. It takes ten seconds. Defender hint: unattended, always-on ports in meeting rooms are the classic target - disable them when idle.
    3. DHCP lease. The implant requests an address; the DHCP server logs a new MAC with an odd hostname. Defender hint: a DHCP log with asset-inventory joins is the single best hardware-implant detector.
    4. Beacon out. The implant checks in with its C2 over HTTPS from an unknown MAC. Defender hint: correlate firewall sessions with MAC identity - "unknown MAC to fresh VPS" is a red flag.
    5. Internal scan. The implant sweeps the local subnet for live hosts and shares. Defender hint: NAC and port-security telemetry will show unauthenticated device activity.
    6. Traffic capture. Unencrypted management protocols leak credentials to the implant. Defender hint: eliminate Telnet/HTTP management on internal devices; encrypt everything.
    7. Persistent tunnel. A reverse SSH tunnel opens from the implant to attacker infrastructure. Defender hint: outbound connections from wired clients that never talk out are anomalies worth alerting on.
    8. Detection and removal. Port security flags the MAC; the port is disabled, the room searched, the device removed. Defender hint: sweep for more devices - professionals plant in pairs.
    3

    Red team / hacker perspective

    The device classes attackers use, what each needs, and the mistakes they make. Conceptual and lab-only.

    When and why attackers use hardware

    They appear in espionage: targeted hotels and conferences. In organized crime: insiders planting devices for ransomware access. And in fraud: skimmers and keyloggers in retail and hospitality (see the FIN7 entry in section 5.2).

    Device classes

    Network implants: SBC-class computers (Raspberry Pi class) in cable-sized cases; tunnel out and bridge inside. HID emulators: USB sticks that type scripted keystrokes (Rubber Ducky and Bash Bunny are the well-known commercial examples - named here for education). Hardware keyloggers: inline recorders or malicious cables (for example "OMG cable" style). Rogue access points: pocket Wi-Fi bridging outsiders into the LAN. Skimmers: payment-focused implants.

    Prerequisites

    Physical access: a visitor, an insider, a contractor, or a cleaning window. An unattended live port, or an insertable USB moment. And - critically - weak network hygiene: no NAC, no port security, no device control, and logs nobody reads.

    LAB-ONLY: build the detection, not the device
    # You do not need an implant to test your defenses. Use any spare machine.

# 1) Plug a Raspberry Pi (or any lab mini-PC) into a TEST bench switch port
#    that mirrors your office configuration.
# 2) Watch what your stack produces:
tail -f /var/log/syslog          # DHCP lease with new MAC (on the DHCP box)
#    - switch: port-security / 802.1X EAPOL failure messages
#    - NAC: unauthenticated device event
# 3) Confirm each event reaches your SIEM and fires the "unknown MAC" rule.
# 4) Then enable port security + NAC and repeat. The second run should fail fast.
# Never plant devices in buildings or networks you do not own.

    Common variations

    Insider-delivered USB handed to a target or dropped in a parking lot. Conference implants in hotels and event venues. Supply-delivered peripherals with tampered firmware. Maintenance impersonation ("IT hardware refresh"). Plant pairs: one device up front, one hidden deeper as a spare.

    Attacker mistakes that help defenders

    Devices that must get a DHCP lease - an instant log trail. Beacons from MACs with no inventory history. HID devices with no matching mouse. Implants left powered for weeks (heat and RF signatures). And generic hostnames like raspberrypi that DHCP logs happily display.

    Danger

    Building or planting attack hardware against facilities you do not own is a crime - and physical tampering carries the same legal weight as hacking. This guide exists to help defenders; test only on your own bench and your own spaces.

    4

    Blue team / SOC perspective

    Hardware attacks leave physical-world and network-world traces. Both are collectable.

    How defenders can see it

    The network is honest: every planted device must announce itself somewhere. It needs an IP (DHCP logs), it traverses a port (switch/NAC logs), it talks outward (firewall logs), or it emulates input (host USB events). The defender's job is to make "new device" a measurable, alertable event - which requires an asset inventory to compare against.

    Network artifacts

    DHCP leases for unknown MACs (often with hostnames like raspberrypi), 802.1X EAPOL failures, port-security violations, new ARP entries, first-ever outbound sessions from wired ports.

    Host artifacts

    Windows Security 6416 (new device recognized) for keyboard-class or composite devices; EDR device-control events; sudden "typed" input launching shells; new HID device with no matching mouse.

    Physical artifacts

    Unknown objects near ports and desks, tape marks or zip ties, unfamiliar wall-warts or cable adapters, badge data showing odd access patterns.

    Where to look (log sources)

    • DHCP server logs: leases with MAC, hostname, and scope - the core implant detector.
    • Switch port security / NAC: 802.1X events, MAC violations, SNMP traps (Cisco ISE, Aruba ClearPass, FortiNAC).
    • Windows Security 6416 and EDR device-control events for USB device inserts.
    • Firewall logs: outbound sessions correlated to switch port / MAC identity.
    • WIPS: rogue access point and rogue client alerts for the wireless variant.

    Short triage flow

    1. Identify the device. From the MAC: OUI lookup, DHCP hostname, switch port location.
    2. Contain instantly. Err-disable or quarantine the port; block its external access.
    3. Locate physically. Map the port to a room; send staff (with security) to inspect.
    4. Preserve. Photograph in place; if safe, power off and bag the device for forensics.
    5. Scope. Review DHCP/NAC history for the MAC elsewhere; sweep nearby rooms; review badge data for the window.
    6. Harden. Close how it got in: port disablement policy, NAC enforcement, visitor process.
    Warning

    An implant that is found and removed is not the end of the incident: assume it captured credentials and mapped the network. Rotate what it could reach, and hunt the accounts and protocols it touched before it was planted - the attackers may have used its data already.

    5.3

    Threats ranking and categorization

    Five realistic threats built on T1200, ranked and categorized. These five are reused in sections 5.4 and 5.5.

    RankThreatSeverityPlatform
    1Planted network implant on the internal LANCriticalNetwork
    2HID keyboard emulator executing scripted commandsHighWindows / Linux / macOS
    3Hardware keylogger inline on keyboard or cableMediumEndpoint / Identity
    4Rogue Wi-Fi access point planted on premisesMediumNetwork
    5Infected USB drive as one-shot initial vectorLowWindows

    Threat 1 - Planted network implant Critical

    Description: a hidden mini-computer on a live wall port tunnels into the network from inside.

    Preconditions: physical access plus an unauthenticated, always-on port.

    Impact: internal foothold for scanning, credential capture, and remote tunnels.

    Justification: bypasses the entire Internet perimeter; persists until physically found.

    Threat 2 - HID keyboard emulator High

    Description: a "USB stick" types an attack script (open terminal, download, execute) in seconds.

    Preconditions: a moment of access to an unlocked machine.

    Impact: full user-level execution, often with persistence added.

    Justification: seconds of exposure suffice; detection depends on device events.

    Threat 3 - Hardware keylogger Medium

    Description: an inline recorder captures keystrokes (passwords, MFA approvals) for later retrieval.

    Preconditions: brief physical access to a target workstation.

    Impact: credential and MFA approval theft; no network trace at all.

    Justification: high value per target but needs retrieval or radio exfil; device events can expose it.

    Threat 4 - Rogue access point Medium

    Description: a planted Wi-Fi box bridges outsiders into the LAN or captures clients (bridges to T1669).

    Preconditions: a powered hiding spot and weak WIPS coverage.

    Impact: remote LAN access or client credential capture.

    Justification: WIPS and RF surveys make it discoverable; still common in weakly monitored sites.

    Threat 5 - Infected USB as initial vector Low

    Description: a malware-laden drive given or dropped to a target; execution needs a user click.

    Preconditions: social engineering plus autorun-adjacent habits.

    Impact: standard malware foothold (chains to T1091 for replication).

    Justification: device control and awareness reduce it to a low-residual risk.

    5.4

    Logs and patterns

    For each of the five threats: log sources, key fields, normal vs suspicious behavior, detection logic, and a realistic event example.

    Threat 1 - Planted network implant Critical

    Log sources: DHCP server logs; NAC/802.1X events; switch port-security logs; firewall.

    Key fields: mac, ip, hostname, port, auth_result.

    Normal: every MAC on the network matches a known asset and owner; 802.1X passes for managed devices only.

    Suspicious: a lease for a MAC that is not in the inventory, especially with a generic hostname or hobbyist hardware OUI.

    Detection logic: join DHCP leases against the asset inventory; alert on unknown MACs anywhere, immediately on server/management VLANs.

    Example event - ISC dhcpd lease log
    Mar 17 09:41:22 dhcp-01 dhcpd[812]: DHCPDISCOVER from de:ad:be:ef:11:22 via 10.30.8.1
Mar 17 09:41:22 dhcp-01 dhcpd[812]: DHCPOFFER on 10.30.8.99 to de:ad:be:ef:11:22 (raspberrypi) via 10.30.8.1

    The MAC de:ad:be:ef:11:22 appears nowhere in the asset inventory, and the client announced the hostname raspberrypi. Either field alone is a strong alert; together they are a confirmed unknown device on an internal subnet.

    Threat 2 - HID keyboard emulator High

    Log sources: Windows Security 6416 (device recognized); Sysmon EID 1 (resulting process); EDR device control.

    Key fields: DeviceDescription, DeviceId, ClassId, then Image/CommandLine.

    Normal: keyboards appear when docked or paired; they come with matching mice and known hardware IDs.

    Suspicious: a new keyboard/composite device followed within seconds by a shell or downloader - typed by the "keyboard".

    Detection logic: correlate new keyboard-class device events with process creation on the same host within 60 seconds.

    Example event - Windows Security 6416 (XML, abridged)
    <Event><System><EventID>6416</EventID>
  <TimeCreated SystemTime="2025-03-17T10:14:03.220Z"/></System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="SubjectUserName">j.reyes</Data>
    <Data Name="DeviceDescription">USB Composite Device</Data>
    <Data Name="DeviceId">USB\VID_2107&amp;PID_0107\0123456789</Data>
    <Data Name="ClassId">{36fc9e60-c465-11cf-8056-444553540000}</Data>
  </EventData></Event>
<!-- 11 seconds later on the same host -->
<Event><System><EventID>4688</EventID>
  <TimeCreated SystemTime="2025-03-17T10:14:14.002Z"/></System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="NewProcessName">C:\Windows\System32\cmd.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="CommandLine">cmd.exe /c powershell -w hidden -enc SQBFAFgA...</Data>
  </EventData></Event>

    A new USB composite device (the ClassId shown is the USB hub/composite class) appears at 10:14:03; a hidden encoded PowerShell launches at 10:14:14. The user never typed that. Correlation across the two events is the detection.

    Threat 3 - Hardware keylogger Medium

    Log sources: Windows Security 6416 on servers/kiosks; EDR device control; physical inspection records.

    Key fields: DeviceDescription, DeviceId, LocationInformation.

    Normal: server-class and kiosk systems never see new human-interface devices; thin clients have fixed hardware.

    Suspicious: any new keyboard-class device on a host that should never gain one.

    Detection logic: alert on keyboard-class device events on servers, kiosks, and thin clients - hosts where keyboards are rare by design.

    Example event - Windows Security 6416 (XML, abridged)
    <Event><System><EventID>6416</EventID>
  <TimeCreated SystemTime="2025-03-17T22:31:55.101Z"/></System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="SubjectUserName">SVC-KIOSK$</Data>
    <Data Name="DeviceDescription">USB Input Device</Data>
    <Data Name="DeviceId">USB\VID_1A2B&amp;PID_3C4D\6&amp;1F0E2A11&amp;0&amp;1</Data>
    <Data Name="LocationInformation">Port_#0003.Hub_#0005</Data>
  </EventData></Event>

    The host is a kiosk running a dedicated service account (SVC-KIOSK$) - nobody should be plugging input devices into it. A new USB input device at 22:31, off-hours, is either maintenance (check the ticket) or a keylogger/HID.

    Threat 4 - Rogue Wi-Fi access point Medium

    Log sources: WIPS alerts; wireless controller logs; spectrum-monitoring reports.

    Key fields: ssid, bssid, classification (rogue/friendly/external), channel.

    Normal: only company-managed BSSIDs broadcast corporate SSIDs; neighbors are classified external.

    Suspicious: corporate SSID advertised from an unknown BSSID (evil twin) or any new BSSID inside the building's RF footprint.

    Detection logic: alert on WIPS rogue classification and on corporate-SSID SSID/BSSID mismatches.

    Example event - WIPS syslog (rogue AP)
    Mar 17 11:02:19 wlc-01 wips: ROGUE_AP_CONTAINED bssid=de:ad:be:ef:88:90 ssid="Corp-Secure" channel=6 classification=rogue rssi=-45 containment=active

    A device is broadcasting your corporate SSID from a BSSID (de:ad:be:ef:88:90) that is not one of your access points, with a strong signal (RSSI -45 means close) - an evil twin inside or right at the building. The WIPS has contained it; security staff should now find the hardware.

    Threat 5 - Infected USB as initial vector Low

    Log sources: Sysmon EID 1 (process from removable path); EDR device control; Windows 6416.

    Key fields: Image, CommandLine, ParentImage, device ID.

    Normal: approved, inventoried USB storage; execution from user-writable paths is restricted by policy.

    Suspicious: processes launched from a newly inserted drive letter (E:\setup.exe), especially right after insertion.

    Detection logic: alert on process execution whose command line or image path starts on a removable drive letter.

    Example event - Sysmon Event ID 1 (XML, abridged)
    <Event><System><EventID>1</EventID>
  <TimeCreated SystemTime="2025-03-17T10:22:18.441Z"/></System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="ParentImage">C:\Windows\explorer.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="Image">E:\setup.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="CommandLine">"E:\setup.exe" /quiet</Data>
    <Data Name="User">CORP\j.reyes</Data>
  </EventData></Event>

    Explorer (a double-click) launched setup.exe directly from drive E: - a removable drive on a fleet where storage is restricted. The executable hash and the drive's device ID link this to the inserted media recorded in 6416.

    5.5

    Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    One complete Sigma rule and one matching Splunk SPL query per threat, with assumptions, false positives, and tuning notes.

    Threat 1 - Planted network implant Rule level: high

    Sigma rule (YAML)
    title: DHCP Lease for Unknown or Generic Hostname
id: 3e8a1c52-70b4-4d69-9f13-6a2c8e0b5d74
status: experimental
description: Detects DHCP leases granted to hostnames that indicate non-managed
  devices - generic Linux installer names such as raspberrypi. Join against your
  asset inventory in the SIEM; any MAC without an owner is an incident.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1200/
  - https://www.mandiant.com/resources/blog/apt28-windows-networks
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2024-11-05
logsource:
  category: network
  product: linux
  service: dhcpd
detection:
  selection_generic:
    hostname:
      - 'raspberrypi'
      - 'ubuntu'
      - 'debian'
      - 'localhost'
  condition: selection_generic
fields:
  - mac
  - ip
  - hostname
falsepositives:
  - Legitimate lab and IoT devices with unmanaged names (inventory them)
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1200
  - attack.tactic.initial-access
    Splunk SPL
    index=network sourcetype=dhcpd
| stats latest(_time) as last_seen values(ip) as ip by mac, hostname
| lookup corp_assets mac OUTPUTNEW owner, asset_type
| where isnull(owner) OR match(hostname, "(?i)raspberry|^ubuntu$|^debian$|localhost")
| sort - last_seen

    Field assumptions: dhcpd logs parsed to mac/hostname/ip; corp_assets is your inventory lookup (clearly environment-specific). False positives: labs and IoT - inventory them, do not filter blindly. Tuning: severity up on server VLANs, management ranges, and ports near exec areas.

    Threat 2 - HID keyboard emulator Rule level: high

    Sigma rule (YAML)
    title: New Keyboard-Class USB Device Followed by Shell Execution
id: 8d4f2b69-15a7-4c08-9e73-4b6d0f1a2c85
status: experimental
description: Detects a new keyboard or composite USB device registration followed
  within a short window by shell or script interpreter execution - the footprint
  of an HID emulator typing an attack script.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1200/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2024-11-05
logsource:
  product: windows
  service: security
detection:
  selection_device:
    EventID: 6416
    DeviceDescription|contains:
      - 'USB Composite Device'
      - 'USB Input Device'
  selection_shell:
    EventID: 4688
    NewProcessName|endswith:
      - '\cmd.exe'
      - '\powershell.exe'
      - '\wscript.exe'
      - '\mshta.exe'
  condition: selection_device near selection_shell
fields:
  - DeviceDescription
  - DeviceId
  - NewProcessName
  - CommandLine
falsepositives:
  - Docking stations and new peripherals paired with legitimate typing
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1200
  - attack.tactic.initial-access
    Splunk SPL
    index=wineventlog sourcetype=WinEventLog:Security (EventCode=6416 DeviceDescription IN ("*USB Composite Device*","*USB Input Device*"))
| transaction host maxspan=60s startswith=EventCode=6416 endswith=(EventCode=4688 NewProcessName IN ("*\\cmd.exe","*\\powershell.exe","*\\wscript.exe","*\\mshta.exe"))
| where eventcount > 1
| table _time host, DeviceDescription, DeviceId, NewProcessName, CommandLine

    Field assumptions: 6416 device events and 4688 process events in the same index; transaction correlates on host within 60 seconds (swap for streamstats at scale). False positives: docking stations - baseline their device IDs and allow-list them. Tuning: require the shell to be short-lived or encoded for high fidelity.

    Threat 3 - Hardware keylogger Rule level: medium

    Sigma rule (YAML)
    title: Keyboard-Class Device on Server or Kiosk Host
id: b19e6c70-2a83-4f15-8d24-7c3e9a5f0b62
status: experimental
description: Detects keyboard-class USB device registrations on hosts where
  keyboards are rare by design - servers, kiosks, and thin clients. New input
  devices on such hosts indicate keyloggers or HID implants.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1200/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2024-11-05
logsource:
  product: windows
  service: security
detection:
  selection:
    EventID: 6416
    DeviceDescription|contains: 'USB Input Device'
  filter_known:
    Computer|endswith:
      - '-WS'
      - '-LAP'
  condition: selection and not filter_known
fields:
  - Computer
  - DeviceDescription
  - DeviceId
  - LocationInformation
falsepositives:
  - Legitimate maintenance sessions using local input (ticket-linked)
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.t1200
  - attack.tactic.initial-access
    Splunk SPL
    index=wineventlog sourcetype=WinEventLog:Security EventCode=6416 DeviceDescription="*USB Input Device*"
| lookup server_roles host OUTPUTNEW role
| where role IN ("server","kiosk","thinclient")
| table _time host, role, DeviceDescription, DeviceId, LocationInformation | sort -_time

    Field assumptions: a server_roles lookup classifies hosts (env-specific; a naming-convention filter works as fallback). False positives: documented maintenance - require a change ticket reference. Tuning: on kiosks, allow-list the exact device IDs of the fixed keyboard.

    Threat 4 - Rogue Wi-Fi access point Rule level: medium

  point as rogue - including evil twins broadcasting corporate SSIDs from unknown
  BSSIDs.

  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1200/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1669/

date: 2024-11-05

  category: network
detection:
  selection:
    message|contains:
      - 'ROGUE_AP'
      - 'rogue ap'
      - 'RogueAP'

    Real-world case study

    DarkVishnya: small computers physically planted inside bank offices to reach the network from within. Based on public reporting by Securelist/Kaspersky (December 2018) and the ATT&CK entry for group G0105.

    Context

    Between 2017 and 2018, at least eight banks in Eastern Europe - most reports center on Ukraine - were breached in an unusual way. Attackers physically entered the buildings and left small computing devices behind: Bash Bunny USB devices, Raspberry Pi boards, netbooks, and cheap laptops. The devices were planted overnight or with the help of insiders, then connected to the internal network like any new employee laptop. Once online, they intercepted traffic, captured credentials, and hosted phishing pages inside the bank's own intranet. ATT&CK lists this operation as the work of DarkVishnya (group G0105) under T1200 Hardware Additions.

    Timeline

    1. 2017-2018Attackers scout target bank offices and prepare small implant hardware from open-source components.
    2. 2017-2018Implants are planted inside buildings - overnight access or insider help - and left powered in work areas.
    3. Hours laterDevices request DHCP leases, join the internal network, and begin intercepting traffic and capturing credentials.
    4. 2018Bank IT teams chase "unknown device" alerts; physical sweeps and access-control reviews locate some implants.
    5. December 2018Securelist (Kaspersky) publishes the DarkVishnya analysis, naming the toolset and the physical-access pattern.

    Attack chain

    1. Recon. The attacker learns the office layout, network zones, and which areas stay unwatched at night.
    2. Plant. A small device is placed in a meeting room, wall socket area, or shared space - low cost, easy to hide.
    3. Join. The implant gets a DHCP lease on the internal network and starts to scan what it can reach.
    4. Intercept. Internal traffic is captured for credentials, and phishing pages are served from inside the intranet.
    5. Expand. Captured credentials open further systems; the attacker works toward payment infrastructure.

    Detection point

    The best early signal was a new network identity: an unknown MAC or hostname getting a DHCP lease, a switch port coming alive in an area that should be empty, or an 802.1X failure on a port nobody should use. Some banks noticed only after users reported odd intranet pages. Physical review - cameras, door logs, and a walk-through - turned the network anomaly into a found device.

    Containment

    The response starts on the switch: shut the port, block the device's MAC, and isolate the affected segment. Then the hunt moves into the physical world - sweep the floor, pull access-control and camera records for the planting window, and check neighboring offices for more implants.

    Eradication

    Remove every found device and verify nothing similar remains. Rebuild systems that the implant touched, and rotate credentials that could have been captured - including service accounts reachable from the implant's segment. Hunt the fleet for the capture and phishing indicators the device produced.

    Recovery

    Bring the network back with the gap closed: NAC or 802.1X on user-facing ports, port lockdown in meeting rooms and lobbies, and device control on endpoints. Restore normal operations and keep the "unknown device" detections running, because this attacker returns to the same door.

    Impact and consequences

    The implants gave attackers a standing position inside bank intranets: credentials captured, traffic observed, and phishing pages served from trusted internal space. Securelist did not publish confirmed loss figures, but the goal was clearly financial, and the operations ran across many institutions for a year or more. The deeper cost was trust: the network boundary held, and the building was the entry point.

    Team response and communication

    Responses involved bank IT and security teams, physical security, and researchers - the pattern was only fully understood when Securelist connected the dots across victims in December 2018. For a bank SOC, the playbook pairs network containment with a physical sweep, and it must involve facilities and HR early: implants depend on uncontrolled space and uncontrolled people.

    Lessons learned

    • Physical access is cyber access. A planted device inside the building bypasses every perimeter control at once.
    • Authenticate devices, not just users. NAC and 802.1X would have blocked most of these implants from ever getting a lease.
    • Lock down idle ports. Meeting rooms, lobbies, and reception areas need ports disabled or heavily restricted.
    • Alert on new identities. Unknown MACs and hostnames on the corporate network deserve the same urgency as malware.
    • Control visitors and contractors. Escort policies, badges, and camera coverage shrink the planting window.
    • Pair network alerts with the floor plan. A new MAC plus an empty room is a physical-security incident, not a helpdesk ticket.

    Mitigation and defense

    Hardware attacks fail against layered physical and network controls.

    Policy

    Visitor escort and badge-scoped zones. Port disablement policy for unattended rooms. Physical security walkthroughs with security staff, including RF sweeps for sensitive areas.

    Identity

    802.1X everywhere so unknown devices fail authentication. Certificate-based supplicants managed by IT. No shared pre-shared keys for wired ports.

    Endpoint

    Device control (USB allow-listing by VID/PID and device class; block HID anomalies). Lock screens enforced. Full-disk encryption so a planted keyboard cannot unlock a sleeping machine.

    Network

    Switch port security (sticky MAC, max MAC count), unused ports administratively shut, DHCP snooping and dynamic ARP inspection, NAC quarantine VLANs.

    Cloud / SaaS

    Conditional Access device-compliance checks so an implant-relayed session still needs a managed device. Short-lived credentials limit captured-token value.

    Logging & monitoring

    DHCP, NAC, port-security, WIPS, firewall, and 6416 device events in the SIEM. Unknown-MAC alerting with inventory joins (section 5.5).

    Backup & resilience

    Not the main lever here - but reimage capability matters if an HID attack compromises an endpoint.

    Response

    A planted-device runbook: contain the port, locate physically, preserve evidence, sweep for pairs, review badge data, rotate what the device could capture.

    Good practice - home and small office

    Do not plug in USB devices you did not buy. Lock your computer whenever you step away. Keep doors to home offices locked. In cafes and hotels, use your phone's hotspot rather than unknown wired ports or Wi-Fi. Full-disk encryption (BitLocker/FileVault) protects a stolen or tampered laptop.

    Common mistakes and best practices

    Ten blue-team mistakes around hardware threats, with symptom, root cause, fix, and prevention.

    1. No asset inventory for MAC addresses

    Symptom Unknown-device alerts are meaningless because "we don't know what's normal".

    Root cause Inventory never included network identities.

    Fix Build a MAC/owner inventory from DHCP history and managed-device exports.

    Prevention Inventory freshness as a KPI with quarterly reconciliation.

    2. Wall ports always live

    Symptom Implant gets a lease in an empty conference room at 02:00.

    Root cause "Install once, forget forever" port philosophy.

    Fix Disable ports by default; enable per booking or task.

    Prevention Port audit joined with room and desk assignments.

    3. 802.1X partially deployed

    Symptom Printers and cameras on the guest VLAN "because 1X was hard".

    Root cause Migration fatigue leaves MAB bypasses everywhere.

    Fix Complete deployment with device profiles; quarantine unknowns.

    Prevention New-device onboarding flow that grants 1X profiles.

    4. USB blocking without HID thinking

    Symptom Storage blocked, but a "keyboard" works perfectly.

    Root cause Policy models only mass storage.

    Fix Class-based device control: allow-list keyboards, block new HID on servers.

    Prevention Device-class matrix in the control policy.

    5. Device events not logged

    Symptom No way to prove what was plugged into a kiosk last month.

    Root cause 6416/device-control auditing off by default.

    Fix Enable device auditing and ship to SIEM.

    Prevention Baseline log checklist includes device channels.

    6. WIPS only at headquarters

    Symptom Rogue AP found at a branch by accident, not telemetry.

    Root cause Wireless defense budget concentrated at HQ.

    Fix WIPS coverage plan for all sites with sensitive staff.

    Prevention Site risk tiering drives coverage.

    7. Ignoring "boring" badges data

    Symptom Implant window unreconstructable after the fact.

    Root cause Badge logs never joined with network events.

    Fix Correlate badge access with DHCP/port events in investigations.

    Prevention Physical-cyber correlation in the SIEM data model.

    8. Removing devices without forensics

    Symptom Implanted device discarded; its capabilities unknown.

    Root cause "Found it, problem solved" response.

    Fix Photograph, bag, and image the device; rotate captured material.

    Prevention Planted-device runbook with evidence steps.

    9. Sweep only where found

    Symptom Second implant discovered months later nearby.

    Root cause Single-device assumption.

    Fix Sweep the floor/building; check DHCP history for sibling MACs.

    Prevention Assume-pairs doctrine in the runbook.

    10. Detection rules without inventory joins

    Symptom Flood of printer MACs turns the unknown-device rule off.

    Root cause Rule fires on raw unknowns, not inventory deltas.

    Fix Join against inventory; alert only on truly unowned MACs.

    Prevention Inventory pipeline maintained as detection dependency.

    Daily habits

    • Review unknown-MAC and NAC quarantine alerts.
    • Check device-control blocks for HID anomalies.
    • Verify no new always-on ports appeared in audits.

    Weekly habits

    • Review WIPS rogue classifications across sites.
    • Reconcile new DHCP MACs against inventory.
    • Walk one sensitive area with physical security.

    Monthly habits

    • Port audit: disabled vs booking data.
    • Device-control policy review (classes, exceptions).
    • Test one section 5.5 rule with replayed events.
