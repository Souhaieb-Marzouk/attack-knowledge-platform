Compromise Accounts T1586

Attackers do not always create new accounts. More often, they take over accounts that already exist — email inboxes, social media profiles, or cloud logins — and reuse the trust attached to those accounts to prepare and run attacks. This guide explains the technique in plain language, shows an animated attack story, and gives defenders working detections, including ready-to-adapt Sigma rules and Splunk queries. ATT&CK labels the platform for this technique as PRE (pre-compromise): the activity happens before an attack touches the target's own systems.

Contents

    1Overview

    Compromise Accounts means taking over an account that already belongs to someone else — an email inbox, a social media profile, or a cloud login — and using the trust attached to that account to prepare and run attacks. This page explains the technique in plain language and shows how to detect it.

    What is this technique?

    People trust an account because of its history. An email address that has written to you for years, a social profile with many real followers, a cloud login that already works — all of these carry trust. Instead of making a new account, an attacker takes an existing one over.

    Ways attackers get the account include buying leaked passwords, tricking users with phishing, reusing passwords from old data breaches, and wearing down users with repeated approval requests. After the takeover, the attacker reads mail, sends trusted messages, or reaches company systems. The account itself becomes a tool for the next stage of the attack.

    Why it matters to defenders

    There is no single alert that says "account stolen". The takeover often looks like a normal sign-in, because the attacker uses the correct password and sometimes even the correct second check.

    Detection therefore depends on changes in behavior: a new country, a new device, a new MFA method, or a new mail rule. This page shows where those changes leave traces, and how to turn the traces into working alerts.

    Words to know

    • Identity provider (IdP) — the service that checks usernames and passwords and decides who may sign in. Okta and Microsoft Entra ID are common examples.
    • Single sign-on (SSO) — one company login that opens many apps, so the user signs in once.
    • Multi-factor authentication (MFA) — a second check after the password, such as an approval on a phone or a security key.
    • Session — the time window in which you stay signed in after one login. Attackers try to steal or reuse an open session instead of logging in again.
    • Credential stuffing — trying many leaked username and password pairs on a login page until one works.
    • Stealer log — a file of passwords and cookies taken from an infected computer and sold or shared in criminal markets.
    • MFA fatigue — sending many approval prompts so a tired user taps Approve just to stop the noise.
    • Spearphishing — a targeted fake message that pretends to come from a trusted person or a trusted account.

    Where this fits in the attack chain

    T1586 sits in the Resource Development stage (tactic TA0042). Before touching the victim's systems, the attacker prepares the resources the attack needs. A compromised account is one of the most useful resources, because it opens doors later.

    Before: gather and build

    Attackers buy or find infrastructure (T1583 Acquire Infrastructure), take over servers (T1584 Compromise Infrastructure), and create fresh accounts (T1585 Establish Accounts). T1586 is the shortcut: use an account that already exists.

    This technique: take over an account

    The attacker gains access to a social media profile (T1586.001), an email account (T1586.002), or a cloud account (T1586.003). The parent page covers the shared idea; each sub-technique page goes deeper.

    After: use the trust

    The stolen account feeds T1078 Valid Accounts (logging in), T1566 Phishing and T1598 Phishing for Information, T1656 Impersonation, and T1534 Internal Spearphishing (phishing coworkers from inside).

    Note

    Official definition, paraphrased: adversaries may compromise accounts with services that can be used during targeting. For operations that use social engineering, an established online persona is important, so rather than creating and cultivating accounts (T1585 Establish Accounts), they may take over existing ones. Methods include gathering credentials by phishing for information, buying credentials from third-party sites, brute forcing credentials (for example, password reuse from breach credential dumps), and paying employees, suppliers, or business partners for access. Before this, adversaries may research the people and services around the target. See the full text on the official ATT&CK page for T1586.

    Written version of the simulation

    The same story as the animation above, in plain text, for screen readers and printing.

    1. Step 1 — Buy stolen credentials. The attacker buys a list of stolen usernames and passwords from a stealer-log market. The data comes from computers infected with password-stealing malware. What defenders see: nothing yet. The sale happens in criminal forums, outside company systems.
    2. Step 2 — Test passwords on the login page. The attacker tries the stolen passwords against the company sign-in page, spread over days and many IP addresses (an IP address identifies a device on the internet). What defenders see: a burst of failed sign-ins appears in IdP logs. The burst is often spread over days and many IP addresses.
    3. Step 3 — Push MFA prompts until someone approves. One password works, but a second check is still needed. The attacker keeps sending approval requests to the user's phone. What defenders see: a storm of MFA push requests shows up. Many users approve just to stop the noise (MFA fatigue).
    4. Step 4 — The user approves — attacker gets a session. The user taps approve by mistake. The attacker now holds a valid session. What defenders see: a sign-in from a new country and device appears next to the user's normal pattern (impossible travel).
    5. Step 5 — Enroll a rogue MFA device and set an inbox rule. The attacker adds their own MFA device and creates a hidden inbox rule, so the access survives the next password change. What defenders see: a new MFA device is registered and an inbox rule is created. These are high-value alerts.
    6. Step 6 — Send phishing from the trusted account. The attacker uses the trusted mailbox to phish the user's colleagues. What defenders see: unusual send volume and new external recipients from a trusted mailbox.
    7. Step 7 — Reset passwords on other services. Password-reset emails from other services land in the mailbox and are used to widen the takeover to shopping sites, social networks, and other linked accounts. What defenders see: password-reset emails and notices leave the mailbox for other services.
    8. Step 8 — SOC responds. The SOC revokes sessions, resets credentials, and removes the rogue device. What defenders see: sessions are revoked, MFA is re-enrolled, and the user is re-educated. Evidence is kept for review.
    Note

    Every step above creates log events somewhere. Steps 1 to 3 create weak signals, step 4 creates the key signal, and steps 5 to 7 create the strongest signals. Section 5 turns each of these into detections.

    3Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section explains, at a conceptual level, why attackers take over accounts and how the approach works. Everything here is for defenders and for authorized lab exercises only. There are no instructions for attacking real systems.

    Warning

    All red-team content on this page is conceptual and lab-only. Run any test against accounts and tenants that you own or are authorized to test, with written approval, on test users that you create for the exercise.

    Why take over an account instead of making one?

    • Trust. A message from a known address gets opened. A message from a new address often does not.
    • History. The account has real conversations, real followers, and a real profile. It does not look new.
    • Fewer checks. New accounts face sign-up checks, limits, and scrutiny. An existing account already passed them.
    • Reach. A company inbox gives contacts, calendars, documents, and password-reset mail for many other services.

    Where the technique applies

    Account types an attacker wants, and what each one gives.
    Account typeExampleWhat it gives the attacker
    Email (Office Suite)Work mailboxTrusted sending, password-reset mail, documents, contacts
    Cloud / SaaSIdP, file storage, customer databasesCompany data, internal tools, ways to persist access
    Social mediaPublic persona pageA believable voice for scams and information gathering
    IaaS consoleCloud platform loginServers to rent under someone else's name and budget

    How attackers get the account (conceptual stages)

    1. Acquire the credentials

    Buy passwords from stealer-log markets, collect them with phishing, reuse passwords from old public breaches, or obtain them through someone inside the organization. This is Resource Development work that happens before the victim sees anything.

    2. Test quietly

    The attacker checks the password on the real login page, in small numbers, often through many addresses and devices. The goal is one valid login with as little noise as possible.

    3. Pass the second check

    If MFA blocks the login, the attacker applies pressure: repeated approval prompts (MFA fatigue), fake help-desk calls, or tricks that copy the approval flow. Number matching and security keys block most of this.

    4. Persist and expand

    Inside the account, the attacker adds their own MFA device, grants an extra app access, hides mail with rules, and uses reset emails to reach the user's other services. Then the account becomes a base for the real attack.

    Prerequisites an attacker needs

    • A valid username and password pair for the target account.
    • A reachable login page, or a session token that still works.
    • A way to pass or remove the second check (approval prompt, phone call, or help desk).
    • Patience: the strongest attackers move slowly and copy the user's normal behavior.

    Variation: social persona

    For scams and information gathering, the attacker wants a social profile with an aged look: posts, connections, photos. A taken-over profile gives all of this on day one (see T1586.001).

    Variation: email account

    For business email attacks, the mailbox is the goal. The attacker reads threads, joins an ongoing conversation, and changes payment details or sends malicious links (see T1586.002).

    Variation: cloud account

    For infrastructure, the attacker wants an IdP, file storage, or cloud platform login. Here they can read data, create extra access, or rent servers quietly (see T1586.003).

    Attacker mistakes that help defenders

    Real attackers make errors, and every error creates noise:

    • Testing too fast. Hundreds of failures in minutes from one address is easy to spot.
    • Shared infrastructure. Cheap hosting and public VPN exits are reused by many criminals, so they carry reputation.
    • Unnatural rhythm. Activity at 04:00 local time, or typing speed and timing that never match the user, stands out.
    • Greedy persistence. Adding MFA devices, apps, and mail rules in one burst creates several rare events at once.
    • Skipping the story. Sending from the trusted mailbox about topics that user never writes about fools nobody for long.
    Danger

    The same playbook works against individuals, not only companies. It needs no malware and no software weakness — only leaked passwords and human tiredness. Treat compromised accounts as a top-tier threat, not as a rare event.

    Note

    Red teams may reproduce parts of this story in a lab to test their own detections. Use test tenants, test users, and approved windows. Never point these techniques at real accounts or real people.

    4Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders rarely see the moment of theft. They see the edges of it: a failed-login burst, a prompt storm, a new device, a strange rule. This section shows where to look and how to work an alert from first sight to closure.

    How defenders notice

    • A spike in failed sign-ins for one user or one address.
    • Many MFA approval requests in a short window.
    • A sign-in from a new country or device (impossible travel).
    • A new MFA method or security info registered on an account.
    • A new inbox rule, an OAuth grant (a third-party app given access to the account), or an app consent nobody expected.
    • Unusual send volume, or a mailbox replying to strangers.
    • A password the user reports they did not change.

    Artifacts the activity leaves behind

    Common artifacts and where they show up.
    ArtifactWhere it shows upWhat it usually means
    Failed sign-in seriesIdP logs, VPN (virtual private network) logsCredential testing in progress
    Repeated MFA promptsIdP logs, MFA provider logsMFA fatigue attempt
    New MFA deviceIdP audit logsPersistence, often after a takeover
    New inbox ruleOffice 365 unified audit logHiding future attack mail from the user
    New app consentCloud audit logsAn extra door that survives password changes
    Password-reset noticesMail flow logs, mailbox itemsReset-chain expansion into other services

    Where to look (data sources)

    ATT&CK lists no native data sources for T1586, so build coverage from the telemetry below:

    • Identity provider logs: Okta System Log (OktaIM2:log), Microsoft Entra ID sign-in (ms:aad:signin) and audit (ms:aad:audit) logs.
    • Email telemetry: Office 365 unified audit log (o365:management:activity), mail flow and delivery logs.
    • Cloud platform logs: AWS (Amazon Web Services) CloudTrail (aws:cloudtrail) and similar audit logs for other clouds.
    • Network and access: VPN and proxy logs for sign-in geography and device posture (whether the connecting device is known and healthy).
    • Outside feeds: stealer-log monitoring that tells you which of your passwords are already leaked.

    Why this matters, and where it sits

    A compromised account is the cheapest way past the network border: no exploit needed, no malware to catch. It feeds Initial Access (T1078), internal phishing (T1534), and impersonation (T1656). If your detections only watch endpoints, an attacker with a valid account passes them by without a trace. Identity telemetry gives the earliest warning for this whole technique.

    Triage flow: from alert to closure

    • Step 1Alert arrives. An identity alert fires: failed-login burst, MFA prompt storm, impossible travel, or a new MFA device. Note the user, time, IP address, and device from the alert.
    • Step 2Check the context. Pull the last 24 hours of sign-ins for the user. Look for new device, new country, new browser, or a session that overlaps a known one.
    • Step 3Verify out-of-band. Call the user on a number from the HR system, not from the email thread, and ask whether they signed in and whether they approved a prompt. Never reply in the suspicious thread to verify.
    • Step 4Contain. Revoke all sessions and refresh tokens, disable the account if approval fails, and block the source address. Speed matters more than certainty here.
    • Step 5Sweep for persistence. Review MFA methods, app consents, OAuth grants, inbox rules, and forwarding addresses. Remove anything the user did not create.
    • Step 6Reset and re-enroll. Force a password change, re-enroll MFA with the user on a video call or in person, and check the user's other services for reset-chain damage.
    • Step 7Check for spread. Search mail flow for messages the attacker sent, and alert the recipients. Review who received what, and pull malicious messages where possible.
    • Step 8Close and learn. Keep the evidence, record how the attacker got in, tune the detection that fired (and the ones that should have), and brief the team.
    Good practice

    Identity telemetry gives the earliest and cheapest warning for this technique. If you can only invest in one logging source this quarter, make it your IdP sign-in and audit logs, streamed to your SIEM (security information and event management system — the central place where you search logs) in near real time.

    Info-stealer ecosystem

    Commodity malware such as RedLine Stealer harvests passwords and cookies from infected machines. The output, sold as stealer logs, is the supply chain for most password-based takeovers.     Turning intel into detections

    • Collect — pick two or three sources above and schedule them.
    • Analyze — map reported behavior to your log fields; keep a short note per campaign.
    • Deploy — translate the behavior into Sigma rules (Section 5.5), not raw indicators alone.
    • Review — each quarter, retire what stopped appearing and refresh what vendors now report.

    5.3 Threat Ranking and Categorization

    We split T1586 into six concrete threats that cover the whole technique from first login attempt to quiet long-term foothold. They are ranked by a mix of likelihood, impact, and how much damage they do before anyone notices. Each threat links to its log section in 5.4 and its Sigma plus SPL pair in 5.5.

    Six ranked threats for T1586 Compromise Accounts.
    #ThreatSeverityPlatformsPreconditionsImpactWhy this rank
    1 Credential stuffing into SSO/IdP — rule High Identity Cloud Reused passwords leaked in stealer logs or breach dumps Account takeover entry point Volume is very high across the industry, even though the per-attempt success rate is low. Cheap for attackers, so it never stops.
    2 MFA fatigue approval (push bombing) — rule High Identity Valid password plus push-based MFA Full session on the account High success rate against untrained users once the password leaks; needs only patience, not exploit skills.

    5 Password-reset chain into other services — rule Medium Identity SaaS Mailbox or recovery access Widens takeover beyond one account Depends on how many services the user linked to the mailbox, but turns one incident into a personal-identity breach.
    6 Dormant and third-party account takeover unnoticed — rule Medium SaaS No owner, weak MFA, old SaaS or ex-employee accounts Silent foothold used for resource development Low visibility makes it attractive: nobody watches accounts that "never log in". Often discovered months later.

    The ranking is a starting point, not a law. If your organization runs many shared mailboxes or has a large SaaS estate with no inventory, threat 6 can outrank threat 2 for you. Re-score the table against your own environment once a quarter.

    Threat 1 — Credential stuffing into SSO/IdP High Identity Cloud

    Log sources: Okta System Log, Entra ID sign-in logs, VPN logs, stealer-log monitoring feeds. Key fields: eventType, outcome.result, outcome.reason, client.ipAddress, client.userAgent, actor.alternateId, properties.riskLevelDuringSignIn.

    Normal versus suspicious for credential stuffing.
    Normal behaviorSuspicious behavior
    A few failures per user, then a success, from a known device and office or VPN addressTen or more failures for one user in minutes, then a success, from an unknown address or an unfamiliar browser
    Failures share the user's usual browser and operating systemFailures rotate browsers or operating systems while the username stays the same
    Detection logic

    Alert when one account collects ten or more failed sign-ins within a short window and then succeeds, especially when the success comes from a new device or a new location.

    • eventType — what happened; user.session.start is a sign-in attempt.
    • outcome.result and outcome.reason — whether it worked and why it failed; INVALID_CREDENTIALS means the password was wrong.
    • client.ipAddress and geolocation — where the attempt came from; compare with the user's baseline.
    • client.userAgent — the browser and system used; stuffing tools often differ from the user's normal setup.
    • zone — the Okta network zone; OffNetwork means the attempt did not come from a trusted network.

    The same pattern in Entra ID shows a location mismatch plus the failing MFA state:

    • properties.location — the reported city and country; compare with the user's last successful session for the mismatch.
    • properties.status.errorCode — a numeric reason; 50126 means the password was wrong.
    • properties.deviceDetail.isCompliant and isManaged — false values mean the device is unknown to the company.
    • properties.riskLevelDuringSignIn — the identity provider's own risk score for this attempt.

    Threat 2 — MFA fatigue approval (push bombing) High Identity

    Log sources: Okta System Log (MFA factor events), Entra ID sign-in logs (error 500121 repeats). Key fields: eventType (user.authentication.auth_via_mfa), outcome.result, actor.alternateId, properties.status.errorCode, properties.mfaDetail.authMethod.

    Normal versus suspicious for MFA fatigue.
    Normal behaviorSuspicious behavior
    One or two MFA prompts per sign-in, approved on the usual deviceFive or more prompts in minutes, most declined or unanswered, then one approval
    Approvals happen while the user is active on the trusted deviceThe approval comes from a session that is on a different device or in a new country
    Detection logic

    Alert when a user receives five or more MFA challenges within ten minutes and an approval follows, because that sequence is the signature of a push-bombing attempt.

    • properties.status.errorCode — 500121 marks an MFA challenge that was denied or left unanswered; a series of them plus a later success is the storm signature.
    • properties.mfaDetail.authMethod — the second factor in use; push notification is the method most exposed to fatigue.
    • properties.ipAddress — the attacker's address during the storm; it usually differs from the address of the final approval.

    Threat 3 — Rogue MFA device / OAuth app enrollment Critical Identity Cloud

    Log sources: Okta System Log (user.mfa.factor.activate), Entra ID audit logs (User registered security info), AWS CloudTrail (EnableMFADevice on IAM users), O365 unified audit (app consents). Key fields: eventType, actor, client.ipAddress, activityDisplayName, targetResources.

    Normal versus suspicious for rogue device enrollment.
    Normal behaviorSuspicious behavior
    Enrollment happens during onboarding hours, guided by the help desk, from a managed deviceEnrollment at an odd hour, from an unmanaged device, right after a risky sign-in
    The user can explain the change when askedThe user never requested it; the new device never appears in the user's normal pattern
    Detection logic

    Alert on every new MFA method or security-info registration, and treat it as critical when the registering session is new, unmanaged, or geographically odd, because this is how attackers make access survive a password reset.

    • eventType — user.mfa.factor.activate means a new MFA method became valid on the account.
    • actor — shows the stolen identity of the user, not the attacker; that is why the client fields matter more here.
    • client.ipAddress and zone — the attacker's network; OffNetwork plus an odd hour raises the score.
    • target — which factor was added; a software token is easier for an attacker to keep than a hardware key.

    Threat 4 — Trusted-account internal spearphishing High Email Identity

    Log sources: O365 unified audit, mail flow logs, Entra ID sign-in logs. Key fields: Operation (New-InboxRule), UserId, Parameters, ClientIP, message send counts. Note: attackers often create an inbox rule first, to hide the replies to their phishing mail from the real owner.

    Normal versus suspicious for trusted-account phishing.
    Normal behaviorSuspicious behavior
    Send volume matches the user's weekly pattern; recipients are known colleagues and partnersSend volume jumps several times above baseline, with many new external or unusual internal recipients
    Inbox rules sort newsletters into folders and were created months agoA new rule deletes or moves messages that contain words like "payment" or a specific sender
    Detection logic

    Alert when a new inbox rule is created within 24 hours of a risky sign-in, or when it deletes or moves incoming mail, because attackers use such rules to hide the conversation they are abusing.

    • Operation — New-InboxRule fires whenever a mailbox rule is created; pair it with the signer-in IP.
    • UserId — the mailbox that was changed; compare ClientIP with the user's normal sign-in addresses.
    • Parameters — what the rule does; From plus MarkAsRead or DeleteMessage is the classic hiding pattern.
    • CreationTime — correlate this timestamp with risky sign-ins within the previous day.

    Threat 5 — Password-reset chain into other services Medium Identity SaaS

    Log sources: Entra ID audit logs (Reset user password), Okta System Log (user.account.reset_password), O365 unified audit and mail flow logs (reset notices sent to the mailbox). Key fields: activityDisplayName, properties.initiatedBy, targetResources, eventType, recipient.

    Normal versus suspicious for reset chains.
    Normal behaviorSuspicious behavior
    A user resets their own password from a known device during work hoursA password reset follows a risky sign-in, or happens from the same odd address as earlier suspicious events
    Reset emails arrive for services the user actually uses, and the user acts on them from their own deviceSeveral reset notices for unrelated services land in one mailbox within hours, then the mailbox goes quiet
    Detection logic

    Alert when a password reset event occurs within 24 hours of a high-risk sign-in, and watch the mailbox for reset notices addressed to outside services, because both are signs of a reset chain in progress.

    • operationName — Reset user password is the audit event for a self-service or forced reset.
    • properties.initiatedBy — who started it and from which address; the address should match the user's baseline, not the attacker's.
    • targetResources — whose password changed; compare with the mailbox owner.
    • properties.correlationId — join key to the sign-in events that happened just before the reset.

    Threat 6 — Dormant and third-party account takeover Medium SaaS

    Log sources: O365 unified audit (UserLoggedIn), Okta System Log for old app accounts, SaaS admin logs, your SaaS inventory. Key fields: Operation, UserId, ClientIP, UserAgent, ResultStatus, last-seen timestamps.

    Normal versus suspicious for dormant account takeover.
    Normal behaviorSuspicious behavior
    An unused marketing or ex-employee account has no sign-ins for monthsThe account suddenly logs in after 60 or more days of silence, often with an API client (a script or program talking to the service) or a new device
    Service accounts log in on a stable schedule from known addressesThe new session comes from a new address, reads data broadly, or changes its own recovery settings
    Detection logic

    Alert when an account that has been silent for 60 days or more shows a successful sign-in, because dormant accounts are the quietest foothold in the estate and often have no owner watching them.

    • Operation and ResultStatus — a successful UserLoggedIn on an account with months of silence is the key event.
    • UserId — this one is a shared service-style mailbox, exactly the type that loses its owner.
    • UserAgent — a script client on a marketing mailbox is a strong oddity; humans use browsers and mail apps.
    • ClientIP — check the address against threat feeds and against every other event on this account.
    Good practice

    Each JSON above is a teaching example with example.com addresses and documentation IPs. Real event schemas carry more fields; keep the ones listed here as your core, and extend as your IdP adds them.

    5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

    Each of the six threats from 5.3 gets one Sigma rule and one matching Splunk SPL query below (rules 1 to 6). Two cross-cutting rules follow (impossible travel and password spray), because they catch the takeover moment and the precursor spraying that several threats share. Sigma is an open rule format for detections that you can translate into almost any SIEM; SPL is Splunk's search language. Copy, adapt the field names to your tenant, then test in a lab index before enabling in production.

    Sourcetype mapping used by every query in this subsection.

    product: azure, service: signinlogsindex=azure sourcetype=ms:aad:signinSplunk Add-on for Microsoft Cloud Services
    product: azure, service: auditlogsindex=azure sourcetype=ms:aad:auditSame add-on as sign-in logs
    product: microsoft, service: exchange_online / m365index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activityOffice 365 Management Activity API
    Cloud account eventsindex=aws sourcetype=aws:cloudtrailFor example the EnableMFADevice IAM event
    Proxy and VPNEnvironment-specificConfirm your own index and sourcetype before reuse

    Rule 1 (Threat 1) — Okta credential stuffing burst then success High

    Mapping: product: okta maps to index=identity sourcetype=OktaIM2:log. False positives and tuning: password rotations and users returning from long absences create honest failure bursts. Raise the threshold before lowering it, and pair the alert with the stealer-log feed so a known-leaked password raises the priority.

    Rule 2 (Threat 2) — MFA push storm then approval High

    Mapping: product: okta maps to index=identity sourcetype=OktaIM2:log; the Entra ID variant uses ms:aad:signin with error 500121. False positives and tuning: genuine travelers re-authenticating, or a help desk testing a factor. Tune on the denial-to-approval ratio, and treat any approval from a new device as automatic high priority.

    Rule 3 (Threat 3) — New MFA factor or security info enrolled Critical

    Mapping: product: okta maps to index=identity sourcetype=OktaIM2:log; the Entra ID audit variant uses ms:aad:audit with the User registered security info activity. In Splunk, the Okta target array appears as target{}.displayName. False positives and tuning: onboarding and new-phone setups. Keep the rule on, but route it to the identity queue and require a user confirmation for closure. This is the highest-value alert on the page.

    Rule 4 (Threat 4) — New inbox rule after risky sign-in High

    Mapping: product: microsoft, service: exchange_online maps to index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity. False positives and tuning: people do create rules that file newsletters. Score up rules that delete, mark as read, or move messages from senders with payment words, and score up creation right after a risky sign-in.

    Rule 5 (Threat 5) — Password reset following a risky sign-in Medium

    Mapping: product: azure, service: auditlogs maps to index=azure sourcetype=ms:aad:audit; the query joins ms:aad:signin for the risk context. False positives and tuning: users who genuinely forgot passwords after travel. The join to a high-risk sign-in is what makes this actionable; without it, watch reset notices for outside services in mail flow instead.

    Rule 6 (Threat 6) — Sign-in to a dormant account Medium

    Mapping: product: microsoft, service: m365 maps to index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity. False positives and tuning: seasonal accounts, auditors returning yearly, and scripts with stable schedules. Sigma alone cannot see silence, so the SPL adds the 60-day state check; Sigma matches the success event and the SPL supplies the memory.

    Cross-cutting rule A — Impossible travel sign-in High

    Why here: the takeover moment of threats 1 and 2 usually shows up as a sign-in from a new location next to the user's normal pattern. Mapping: product: azure, service: signinlogs maps to index=azure sourcetype=ms:aad:signin. False positives and tuning: real travel, new corporate VPN exits, and phone-hotspot geolocation drift. Suppress known travel and known VPN ranges instead of disabling the rule wholesale.

    Cross-cutting rule B — Password spray across many users Medium

    Why here: password spraying is the precursor of threat 1: the attacker tries one common password against many usernames, hunting for any valid pair. Mapping: product: okta maps to index=identity sourcetype=OktaIM2:log. False positives and tuning: misconfigured mail clients and new-app rollouts that all authenticate badly at once. Whitelist documented service addresses.

    Warning

    Every rule above is a starting point. Run each one in search-only mode for two weeks, record the false positives you see, add allowlists, and only then enable alerting. A detection that fires fifty times a day trains analysts to ignore it.

    6Real-World Case Study

    Uber, September 2022. Based on public reporting (source links below); details are simplified for teaching, and approximate dates are marked as such. The incident is a near-perfect worked example of T1586: a bought password, an MFA fatigue approval, and a fast expansion through identity and cloud systems.

    Context

    Uber runs a large identity-first estate: a VPN guarded by multi-factor authentication, cloud platforms for engineering, and collaboration tools for staff. An attacker, later linked in vendor reporting to the LAPSUS$-style tradecraft (LAPSUS$ on ATT&CK), did not break any encryption or exploit a software hole. The whole entry path was one contractor account taken over with a bought password and an accidental approval. Microsoft documented the same MFA-fatigue tradecraft in its write-up of the group it tracked as DEV-0537 (DEV-0537 criminal attack techniques highlight the vulnerability of MFA).

    Primary sources: Uber Newsroom, Security Update (26 September 2022) and the Microsoft DEV-0537 report linked above. Where reporting does not give an exact time, the timeline below says "approx.".

    Timeline

    • 2022-09-12 (approx.)Credentials bought. The attacker later stated they purchased a contractor's corporate password from a market that resells data harvested by password-stealing malware. The contractor's device had been infected earlier, and the password landed in a stealer log.
    • 2022-09-13 to 14 (approx.)MFA fatigue campaign. Using the valid password, the attacker triggered repeated MFA approval requests on the contractor's phone. After many prompts, the contractor accepted one, reportedly believing it was part of the noise or a legitimate request.
    • 2022-09-14 (approx.)VPN access. With password plus approval, the attacker signed in to Uber's VPN as the contractor. This is the takeover moment: a valid session on a corporate network, from a new device.
    • 2022-09-14 to 15 (approx.)Secrets found in scripts. Inside the network, the attacker found PowerShell script history on a network share. The scripts contained administrator credentials written in plain text — a classic hygiene failure that no alert had flagged.
    • 2022-09-15 (approx.)PAM unlocked. The admin credentials opened Uber's privileged access management system (referred to publicly as "P0"), where large numbers of secrets were stored: cloud platform keys, workspace administration passwords, and virtualization access.
    • 2022-09-15 (approx.)Wide reach. With the vault contents, the attacker reached AWS, Google Workspace, and vSphere hypervisors, and read internal dashboards and code repositories.
    • 2022-09-15Public message in Slack. The attacker posted a company-wide message in Uber's Slack announcing that Uber had been breached. Many staff learned of the intrusion from the attacker, not from the SOC.
    • 2022-09-15 to 16Detected and contained. Uber's internal security teams identified the unauthorized access and began containment: affected user accounts were disabled, sessions revoked, and major internal systems (Slack, Google Workspace, engineering platforms, AWS) taken offline while access was re-secured.
    • 2022-09-17 to 21 (approx.)Recovery in stages. Access to internal systems was restored progressively as credentials and secrets were rotated and reviewed, with security teams watching for renewed activity.
    • 2022-09-26Disclosure. Uber published its Security Update, stating that the attacker had accessed internal systems and that there was no evidence of unauthorized access to sensitive customer data. Regulators and third-party vendors continued to review the incident.

    Attack chain in technique terms

    The Uber 2022 chain mapped to the threats and techniques on this page.
    StepWhat happenedRelated technique or threat
    1Password bought from a stealer-log marketThreat 1 precondition (T1586 credential acquisition)
    2MFA fatigue prompts, then an approvalThreat 2 (MFA fatigue approval)

    Detection point

    Per Uber's disclosure, internal teams identified the unauthorized access; the company did not publish which alert fired first. For teaching purposes: the chain above was visible in identity telemetry at every step — a burst of MFA prompts, a VPN sign-in on a new device, and later mass secret access. Rules 2 and cross-cutting rule A in Section 5.5 are built for exactly these moments.

    Containment

    Affected accounts were disabled and sessions revoked. Uber took major internal systems offline — Slack, Google Workspace, AWS, and engineering platforms — accepting large business cost to stop the attacker moving while access was re-secured.

    Eradication

    Credentials and secrets exposed through the vault were rotated. The attacker's created access was removed, script histories and shares were reviewed, and every path the stolen contractor identity could reach was audited.

    Recovery

    Systems were restored in stages over the following days, with monitoring tuned to the incident. Collaboration and engineering work returned to normal once credential rotation and review were complete.

    Impact and consequences

    Internal systems, code, and dashboards were exposed to the attacker. Uber stated no customer data was breached, but the incident caused days of disruption, regulatory attention, and lasting reputational cost — all from one contractor account.

    Team response and communication

    The compromise of the company chat tool cut off the usual channel, so responders had to coordinate out-of-band, a step worth rehearsing in advance. Leadership published a clear external statement within days. For internal staff, a single trusted channel carried status updates, so rumor did not replace facts. The Slack message from the attacker showed why incident communication plans must assume that attacker-controlled channels may look normal.

    Lessons learned

    • Phishing-resistant MFA first. Security keys or passkeys stop MFA fatigue; push approval with number matching reduces it. Both beat plain push prompts.
    • Monitor stealer logs for your domains. The contractor's password was on sale before the incident; a monitoring feed could have forced a reset days earlier.
    • Never leave secrets in scripts. Credentials in PowerShell history and network shares turned one account into a master-key event. Use vaults and short-lived tokens.
    • Guard the vault. Privileged access management is a high-value target; require strong, separate authentication and alert on unusual vault access.
    • Identity-first detection pays. The takeover moment was visible in identity logs before any data left. Rules like 5.5's MFA storm and impossible travel exist for this.
    • Rehearse losing your chat tool. Response coordination needs a pre-agreed out-of-band channel if the main one is compromised.

    7Mitigation and Defense

    ATT&CK's mitigation entry for this technique is M1056 Pre-compromise. Its official note says this technique cannot be easily mitigated with preventive controls, because the behaviors happen outside the scope of enterprise defenses. Defense in depth is still the answer: make theft harder, make abuse visible, and make recovery fast. The controls below are grouped by where they live.

    Policy

    Write an account lifecycle policy: who approves new accounts, who can add MFA methods, how fast access is removed when someone leaves, and how long accounts may stay unused before they are disabled. Review the policy against the dormant-account threat twice a year.

    Identity

    Roll out phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 security keys or passkeys — login methods that attackers cannot replay) for admins first, then all staff. Enable number matching and limit MFA requests per hour. Use conditional access so risky sign-ins need a compliant device. Block legacy authentication protocols that skip modern controls.

    Endpoint

    Stealer malware is where stolen passwords begin. Keep endpoint detection and full-disk protection on, patch browsers, and watch for credential-dumping behavior. Restrict where PowerShell histories and scripts can be written, and scan shares for stored secrets.

    Network

    Put VPN and remote access behind the same phishing-resistant MFA. Log every VPN session with device posture. Route sign-ins through proxies that record geolocation, so impossible travel can be verified rather than guessed.

    Cloud and SaaS

    Keep a live inventory of every SaaS account with an owner. Disable dormant accounts after 45 to 60 days. Alert on new OAuth grants and admin role changes. Store secrets in vaults with separate authentication — never in scripts, tickets, or chat.

    Logging and monitoring

    Stream IdP sign-in and audit logs to your SIEM in near real time. Enable mailbox auditing everywhere. Subscribe to a stealer-log monitoring feed and force a reset for every leaked credential found. Deploy the Sigma rules in Section 5.5 and give each one an owner.

    Response

    Maintain a session-revocation runbook and test it quarterly: can you revoke all tokens for a user in under five minutes? Pre-agree an out-of-band communication channel for incidents. Include MFA-fatigue scenarios in tabletop exercises (practice drills played out on paper).

    People

    Train users to decline prompt storms and to report them with one click. Teach help-desk staff strict identity-verification scripts, because attackers call the help desk too. Make managers model the behavior: nobody should ever approve a prompt they did not trigger.

    Note

    Home and small office: turn on MFA everywhere, prefer an authenticator app or security key over SMS, never reuse a password between sites, check Have I Been Pwned for leaked accounts, and remove old app access you no longer use. A personal password manager blocks most password reuse attacks on its own.

    8Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    These are the mistakes blue teams make with account-takeover defense, drawn from the threats and rules on this page. Each card gives the symptom you will notice, the root cause, the fix, and the prevention habit that keeps it from coming back.

    Mistake 1: No alert for a new MFA device.

    Symptom: attackers enroll their own authenticator and keep access for weeks.

    Root cause: nobody mapped the IdP audit log to an alert; enrollment events flow to storage only.

    Fix: deploy Rule 3 from 5.5 today and route it to the identity queue.

    Prevention: treat every identity configuration change as alert-worthy by default.

    Mistake 2: Treating MFA prompts as user noise.

    Symptom: analysts close prompt-storm tickets as "user complaints".

    Root cause: the team has not linked prompt storms to push-bombing tradecraft.

    Fix: deploy Rule 2, and add prompt storms to the triage playbook with a user verification call.

    Prevention: weekly review of MFA denial counts per user, with a low bar for follow-up.

    Mistake 3: Impossible travel disabled wholesale.

    Symptom: the rule exists but is muted "because of false positives".

    Root cause: one bad tuning round; instead of allowlisting VPN and travel, the team switched it off.

    Fix: re-enable with travel calendars and VPN ranges allowlisted, and require device-change context.

    Prevention: never disable a high-value rule wholesale; tune it in search-only mode and document why.

    Mistake 4: No stealer-log monitoring.

    Symptom: the company learns of leaked passwords from journalists or researchers.

    Root cause: monitoring is seen as an extra, not as early warning for this technique.

    Fix: subscribe to a feed or build one, and force credential resets for every hit.

    Prevention: add "your domain in a stealer log" to the incident classification list with a fixed SLA (a promised response time).

    Mistake 5: No SaaS inventory.

    Symptom: a taken-over marketing tool had an admin account nobody knew existed.

    Root cause: teams buy SaaS directly; identity stops at the IdP boundary.

    Fix: inventory all accounts with owners, then connect the biggest to the IdP and the SIEM.

    Prevention: quarterly sweep of spend and logs to catch shadow SaaS (apps bought without IT approval) before attackers do.

    Mistake 6: Static thresholds everywhere.

    Symptom: alerts fire on every Monday-morning login backlog, or never at all.

    Root cause: thresholds copied from a template, never tuned to real traffic.

    Fix: measure two weeks of baseline per rule, then set thresholds from the data (see 5.5 notes).

    Prevention: re-baseline each rule after major changes: new apps, mergers, holiday seasons.

    Mistake 7: Identity alerts without an owner.

    Symptom: identity alerts sit unread in a queue that "belongs to everyone".

    Root cause: no named team or on-call path for identity detections.

    Fix: assign each Sigma rule an owning team and an escalation path in the runbook.

    Prevention: include rule ownership in the detection-as-code review checklist.

    Mistake 8: No session-revocation ability.

    Symptom: during an incident, responders discover revoking tokens takes hours or permissions they lack.

    Root cause: the runbook exists on paper but was never tested against production tooling.

    Fix: run a quarterly revocation drill; script it and grant the minimum rights needed.

    Prevention: make "revoke all sessions for user X" a measured, on-call capability with an SLA.

    Mistake 9: Unmonitored shared mailboxes.

    Symptom: an attacker reads a shared inbox for months without a single alert.

    Root cause: shared and service mailboxes are excluded from auditing "for noise".

    Fix: enable mailbox auditing and Rule 6 for shared mailboxes, and alert on any sign-in to them.

    Prevention: inventory shared mailboxes yearly and delete the ones that lost their purpose.

    Mistake 10: Sigma tested only on samples.

    Symptom: rules pass on curated test events, then drown in production noise or miss real events.

    Root cause: field names differ per tenant and ingestion pipeline; samples never show that.

    Fix: run every rule in search-only mode against two weeks of real logs before enabling.

    Prevention: keep a staging index that mirrors production field names and test there first.

    Mistake 11: Offboarding gaps for leavers.

    Symptom: an ex-employee's old SaaS account signs in and nobody notices for a quarter.

    Root cause: offboarding covers the main IdP but not the long tail of SaaS and third-party logins.

    Fix: sweep for accounts with no owner, disable them, and track leaver access centrally.

    Prevention: make "every account has an owner and a review date" a policy that is enforced with reports and consequences.

    Mistake 12: Reset emails treated as routine.

    Symptom: a mailbox receives reset notices for five outside services; nobody looks.

    Root cause: mail flow logs are not watched for reset-notice patterns (threat 5).

    Fix: alert on multiple password-reset notices to one mailbox within hours, and verify with the user.

    Prevention: add reset-notice volume to the daily identity review list.

    Habits that keep the defenses honest

    Daily

    • Review new MFA device and security-info registrations.
    • Check MFA denial counts per user for prompt storms.
    • Scan identity alerts for new-country or new-device sign-ins.

    Weekly

    • Review inbox rules created in the last seven days.
    • Check stealer-log feed results and reset matched credentials.
    • Look at send-volume outliers for trusted mailboxes.

    Monthly

    • Test each Sigma rule against recent real logs and record tuning.
    • Review dormant accounts and disable the silent ones.
    • Run the session-revocation drill and time it.
