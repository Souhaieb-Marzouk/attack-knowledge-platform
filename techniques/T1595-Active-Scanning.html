MITRE ATT&CK · RECONNAISSANCE · TECHNIQUE

T1595 Active Scanning

Active scanning is when an attacker sends traffic to systems that belong to the target to learn what is there. Unlike passive reconnaissance, the attacker touches the network on purpose, so defenders can often see it. This page explains how the technique works, what it looks like in logs, and how to build detections for it.

  • Tactic: Reconnaissance (TA0043)
  • Type: Technique — 3 sub-techniques
  • Platforms: PRE (attacker infrastructure, pre-compromise)
  • Data source: Network Traffic
  • Detection support: Moderate
  • Guide version: v2.0

What it is

Probing live systems to map hosts, open ports, services, and software versions.

Where it happens

Before an intrusion, usually from outside the network, against internet-facing assets.

Main evidence

Bursts of connections from one or a few sources to many ports or many hosts.

Best defense

Shrink the exposed surface, keep logging complete, and alert on scan patterns.

Overview

What this technique is, why it matters, and what you will learn on this page.

What active scanning means

MITRE ATT&CK is a free public catalog of attacker behavior, and T1595 is its entry for active scanning. The attacker sends a packet (a small unit of data sent over a network) or a request to systems that belong to the target. Every answer gives information: a live host, an open port (a numbered door into a system), a service name, or a software version. In passive reconnaissance the attacker only reads public data and never touches your network. Active scanning touches it on purpose, so it usually leaves records in your logs.

Why it matters

  • Reconnaissance is step one of almost every intrusion. The scan chooses the door the attacker walks through.
  • Every new CVE (a published identifier for a known software flaw) triggers scanning waves within days.
  • You cannot defend assets you do not know exist. Scans show attackers what you forgot about.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Active scanning sits in the Reconnaissance tactic (TA0043), before Initial Access (TA0001). It happens outside your network, before any login or malware.

Related entry: T1046 (Network Service Discovery) covers the same probing after a compromise, from inside. Same traffic, different tactic — keep both IDs on your detections.

ATT&CK definition — technique T1595, version 1.0

“Adversaries may execute active reconnaissance scans to gather information that can be used during targeting. Active scans are those where the adversary probes victim infrastructure via network traffic, as opposed to other forms of reconnaissance that do not involve direct interaction.” ATT&CK lists the platforms as PRE (pre-compromise, outside the target network) and gives the technique a detection strategy built on watching for uncommon network flows.

What you will learn on this page

  • How a scan looks on the wire and which records it leaves.
  • How attackers plan and hide their scanning.
  • Which artifacts scans leave in firewall, flow, and web logs.
  • How to write detections for scanning with Sigma and Splunk.
  • How a real incident wave (Log4Shell) started with scanning.

Watch a full active-scan story against a small company network. Each step shows what the attacker does and what the defender sees at the same time.

Words used in this story: a port scan tries connections to many network doors (ports) to see which ones are open. A SYN packet is the first packet of a TCP connection (TCP is the standard protocol behind web and mail traffic). Attackers send it alone as a probe. A banner is the greeting line a service sends when someone connects. A User-Agent is the identification string a tool sends with its requests. An ICMP echo is a small ping probe that asks “are you online?”. The SOC (Security Operations Center) is the team that watches alerts and responds to them.

Caption

Defender view

  1. 1. Choose a target — The attacker picks a range owned by the company. Public registration records show the company uses 203.0.113.0/24, so the attacker writes down all 256 addresses. Defender view: Nothing is attacking yet, so there is nothing to alert on. Make sure you keep an accurate list of the addresses your company owns and what runs on them.
  2. 2. Ping sweep — The attacker sends small probe packets (ICMP echo) to every address in the range to learn which hosts are online. Defender view: The firewall sees quick, small probes to many addresses, including ones with no service. A burst of echoes to unused addresses is an early signal.
  3. 3. Port scan — The attacker sends connection attempts to many ports on the web server. Most are closed, but ports 80 and 443 answer, so they are marked as open. Defender view: Many SYN packets to different ports from one source is the classic port-scan pattern. The firewall log gives you source, target, ports, and the result.
  4. 4. Fingerprint services — The attacker reads the banners that open services return. The web server says it runs nginx 1.18, and the mail server answers as Postfix. Old versions help the attacker pick working exploits. Defender view: Web access logs show the scanner User-Agent. Keep vendor advisories on your watchlist so you know which versions in your estate are outdated.
  5. 5. Probe other hosts — The attacker checks the mail server and one workstation address. Mail accepts connections on port 25. The workstation is blocked at the firewall, which means the segmentation is working. Defender view: A blocked scan is still a report. Confirm that the block was intended, and check why a workstation address is reachable enough to be probed at the edge.
  6. 6. Review findings — The attacker stops sending traffic and reviews the results: two live public servers, their open ports, and their software versions. The recon data is ready for the next step, usually vulnerability scanning. Defender view: The burst has ended, but the evidence stays in your logs. Hunt for other sources that scanned you the same way this month — one source is never alone.
  7. 7. SOC investigates — An SOC analyst pulls the logs for the scanning IP, matches it against threat intelligence, and finds it is a known mass scanner. They confirm no other ports answered and no system was exploited. Defender view: Correlate the source with a scanner reputation feed. Then verify: did anything answer that should not have? Write down the source for future blocklists and reports.
  8. 8. Block and harden — The team blocks the scanning range at the firewall and reviews the exposed services. Blocking one IP will not stop the next scanner, so reducing the exposed surface matters more in the long run. Defender view: Treat this as a drill: it tested your logging, your alerting, and your asset inventory. Close what does not need to be public, and keep watching for new scanners.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

Why attackers scan, how they do it, and what a professional scan looks like in a controlled lab.

When and why attackers scan

Scanning happens before phishing emails or exploitation attempts. It shows the attacker the easiest door, so they do not waste time on patched systems. Some attackers scan for themselves. Others are initial access brokers — attackers who break in first and then sell the way in to ransomware crews. Large botnet operators (Mirai and its many copies) scan the whole internet on a schedule to recruit devices. Most scanning is also quietly blended into the background noise of the internet, where thousands of scanners run at any moment.

A typical scanning workflow

1. List target ranges

Public records give the map. Sources include WHOIS (public registration data for internet addresses). DNS records (DNS is the internet's name lookup system) add host names. Certificate transparency logs (public lists of website certificates) add subdomains.

2. Ping sweep and port scan

Tools such as Nmap (an open-source scanner), masscan, zmap, and RustScan find live hosts and open ports. A ping sweep asks “who is online”; a port scan asks “which doors are open”.

3. Fingerprint services

Open services often announce themselves. Banners (greeting lines a service sends) reveal software names. TLS certificate details (TLS is the encryption protocol behind HTTPS) often show hostnames. HTTP headers (the web protocol) add version hints.

4. Feed results forward

The list of services and versions drives vulnerability scanning (T1595.002) or wordlist scanning of web paths (T1595.003), and then the exploitation attempt.

Where they scan from, and what they need

Launch points

  • Rented cloud VPS (Virtual Private Server — a cheap rented machine) in many countries.
  • Bulletproof hosting — providers that ignore abuse complaints.
  • Residential proxies — home connections rented as relays, so scans look like home users.
  • Short-lived containers, with source rotation (switching addresses) to dodge blocklists.

Preconditions

  • The target must be reachable from the internet.
  • The attacker accepts being logged — they assume some noise is unavoidable.
  • No credentials are needed. Nothing in the target must be trusted or installed.

Lab-only examples (safe targets)

Nmap and masscan against practice targets
# Service scan of Nmap's sanctioned practice host
nmap -sV --top-ports 100 scanme.nmap.org

# Host discovery of a documentation range you own in the lab
nmap -sn 203.0.113.0/24

# Fast sweep of two ports across a lab range (never aim at real networks)
masscan 203.0.113.0/24 -p80,443 --rate 1000

What the flags mean: -sV asks each open service for its version banner. --top-ports 100 checks the 100 most common ports. -sn runs a ping sweep only and skips port scanning. -p80,443 limits the sweep to two ports. --rate 1000 caps masscan at 1000 packets per second. 203.0.113.0/24 is a documentation range (TEST-NET-3), reserved for examples.

Lab only — legal and ethical use

Run scanning commands only against systems you own, systems you have written permission to test, or designated practice targets such as scanme.nmap.org or the documentation range 203.0.113.0/24. Scanning systems without permission is illegal in many countries and violates responsible security practice.

Common variations

  • TCP SYN (half-open) scan: send the first packet of a connection (SYN) and never finish the handshake. Many systems do not log it. A full connect scan completes the handshake.
  • UDP scans: probe UDP services (a connectionless protocol used by DNS, NTP, and SNMP) that TCP scans cannot see.
  • Low-and-spread scanning: run slowly and from many sources, so no single source crosses alert thresholds.
  • Cloud-range scanning: scan from big cloud provider ranges, because allowlists rarely block their own customers.
Where scanners go wrong

Scanning too fast triggers intrusion prevention systems (IPS — devices that block hostile traffic automatically). Scanning without a target list wastes time and money. And banners can lie: hardened services hide or fake their versions, so fingerprint data always needs a second check before the attacker trusts it.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

What a scan looks like from the inside, which artifacts it leaves, and how to triage one.

How defenders see it

  • Firewall or NGFW (next-generation firewall — one that inspects applications, not just ports) deny spikes.
  • Flow records (short summaries of each connection) showing one source reaching many destinations.
  • IDS (Intrusion Detection System) scan signatures — Suricata and Snort rules with names like ET SCAN.
  • Web logs carrying scanner User-Agents (the identification string a tool sends).
  • Honeypot hits (a honeypot is a decoy system with no real users).

Artifacts it leaves

  • SYN packets without handshakes — Zeek (a network security monitor) records these with flow state S0, which means the probe got no answer.
  • Uniform TTL (packet hop counter) and packet size across a whole burst.
  • Probes to addresses that have no service at all.
  • Banner reads right after ports open up.
  • Sequential or random port patterns that no real client produces.

Where to look

  • Edge firewall logs and NetFlow/Zeek (NetFlow is a common flow-record format; Zeek adds protocol detail).
  • IDS alerts from Suricata or Snort.
  • Cloud VPC flow logs (Virtual Private Cloud connection records).
  • Web access logs and mail/proxy logs.
  • Threat-intel reputation of source IPs — GreyNoise tags known scanners.

Why it matters

A scan is reconnaissance with a purpose: it almost always precedes exploitation. The same records also tell you what attackers can see about your exposure. If the scan found something, so did the next scanner — every unanswered burst is free information for you.

Triage flow for a scan alert

  1. Confirm the pattern. Count destinations times ports inside the time window. Breadth, not volume alone, makes a scan.
  2. Classify the source. Check scanner reputation feeds: known mass scanner, security researcher, search engine crawler, or unknown?
  3. Check what answered. Any success response or accepted connection from unexpected hosts or ports? Answers matter more than probes.
  4. Decide. Block, rate-limit, or watch — and open a ticket either way, so the evidence is not lost.
  5. Close the loop. Verify that every exposed service should exist, tune the detection that fired, and record the source for future hunting.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

MITRE ATT&CK

Free knowledge base of attacker behavior. Use it to name what you see, map detections, and plan coverage. Confidence in behavior mapping: high.

Vendor reporting

Blogs and reports from security vendors (CrowdStrike, Mandiant, Microsoft, Palo Alto Unit 42, Cisco Talos, Recorded Future). Usually high or medium confidence; check their stated evidence.

Government advisories

CISA advisories and the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog. High confidence. Use KEV to prioritize patching of what attackers actually scan for and exploit.

OSINT scanner context

GreyNoise classifies internet scanners so you can tell mass scanners from targeted ones. Shodan and Censys show what attackers can see about your exposure. AbuseIPDB collects abuse reports. Confidence: medium to high, verify before blocking.

ISACs

Sector Information Sharing and Analysis Centers (for example MS-ISAC, FS-ISAC, H-ISAC) share member sightings with high trust. Useful for early warning inside your sector.

Internal telemetry

Your own firewall, flow, IDS, and honeypot history. Highest confidence for your environment. Keep 90+ days searchable so you can hunt for patterns you only notice later.

ATT&CK procedure examples for T1595

ATT&CK records two named users of this technique. Salt Typhoon (group G1045) has scanned victim environments for open ports and services. The Triton Safety Instrumented System Attack (campaign C0030) began with network reconnaissance against targets of interest.

Confidence: high — both entries are quoted from ATT&CK's own procedure examples for T1595 (technique page version 1.0, last modified 24 October 2025). ATT&CK's detection strategy (DET0830) says to monitor network data for uncommon data flows — the same idea as the rules below.

How to apply

Tag every scan detection with its ATT&CK ID so reports and coverage charts add up. Keep an allowlist of known benign scanners (search engines, your own approved scan vendor) to cut false positives. Feed confirmed malicious scanners to edge blocklists with expiry dates — a blocklist entry without an expiry slowly turns into an outage.

5.4 Logs and Patterns

Each threat above leaves a different shape in your logs. Six compact blocks tell you where to look, which fields matter, what normal looks like, and the one-sentence logic to detect it.

1. Post-CVE mass scan wave

Log sources: edge firewall, web logs, IDS alerts.

Fields: src_ip, dest_port, action, User-Agent.

Normal: a handful of scanners probes public web ports each day.

Suspicious: hundreds of sources hitting one newly disclosed path, or one source sweeping thousands.

Detection logic: alert when unique sources touching a newly disclosed service exceed baseline by a wide margin.

2. Broker targeted recon

Log sources: firewall, flow records, web logs.

Fields: src_ip, dest_ip, dest_port, duration.

Normal: partners and monitors hit known addresses on known ports.

Suspicious: slow, patient sweeps from one provider network across your whole address range.

Detection logic: alert on low-rate breadth from a single source spread over hours.

3. Botnet / IoT recruitment

Log sources: edge firewall, honeypot, flow records.

Fields: src_ip, dest_port, action.

Normal: some background probing is unavoidable.

Suspicious: waves against telnet-era ports (23, 2323) and default-credential login pages across many addresses.

Detection logic: count denied connections to device ports per source and alert above a threshold.

4. Cloud exposure discovery

Log sources: VPC flow logs, load balancer logs, cloud audit logs.

Fields: srcaddr, dstaddr, dstport, action.

Normal: known scanners and health checks.

Suspicious: unknown external sources probing storage buckets, consoles, and metadata APIs.

Detection logic: alert when an external source touches console or API endpoints with no prior history.

5. Identity perimeter probing

Log sources: reverse proxy logs, firewall, identity provider logs.

Fields: client_ip, url, status, user-agent.

Normal: employees reach mail and VPN portals from known ranges.

Suspicious: many distinct external sources reading login pages, autodiscover endpoints, and version files.

Detection logic: alert on bursts of reads of login or discovery endpoints from sources that never log in.

6. Internal discovery spillover (T1046)

Log sources: internal firewall logs, EDR network events (EDR is endpoint detection software), internal flow.

Fields: src_ip, dest_ip, dest_port, action.

Normal: admins and monitoring tools scan from known servers on purpose.

Suspicious: a workstation or server sweeping internal file-sharing and remote-desktop ports across many subnets.

Detection logic: alert when one endpoint contacts more than N internal hosts on admin ports in ten minutes.

5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

Sigma is an open rule format for detections. Splunk SPL is the search language of the Splunk SIEM (Security Information and Event Management — the system that stores and searches your logs). Six rules cover the main scan shapes. Each rule ships with matching SPL, a mapping note, false positives, and tuning advice. Copy, adapt the index names, and test.

Rule 1 — Single Source Scanning Many Destinations

Sigma YAML — single source scanning many destinations
title: Single Source Scanning Many Destinations
id: 7f4a2c1e-9b3d-4e8f-a6c5-1d2e3f4a5b6c
status: stable
description: >
  One source IP contacts an unusually high number of unique destination addresses in a short
  window. This matches ping sweeps and IP block scans described in ATT&CK T1595 and
  T1595.001. Deny-only traffic keeps the rule cheap, but remove the filter if allowed traffic
  is also logged.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1595/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1595/001/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-20
logsource:
  category: firewall
  product: paloalto
detection:
  selection:
    action: deny
  timeframe: 5m
  condition: selection | count(dest_ip) by src_ip > 100
fields:
  - src_ip
  - dest_ip
  - dest_port
  - action
falsepositives:
  - Approved vulnerability scanners run by the security team
  - Search engine crawlers indexing a public address range
  - Backup or replication jobs that touch many hosts
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1595
  - attack.t1595.001
Splunk SPL — matching search for Rule 1
index=firewall sourcetype=pan:traffic action=deny
| bin _time span=5m
| stats dc(dest_ip) AS unique_dests count AS total_hits by _time, src_ip
| where unique_dests > 100
| sort - unique_dests

Field assumptions: Palo Alto traffic events in index=firewall sourcetype=pan:traffic. The fields dest_ip, src_ip, and action come from the Splunk Add-on for Palo Alto Networks. Adjust index and sourcetype names to your environment (for Zeek use index=network sourcetype=zeek:conn and id.resp_h/id.orig_h).

Mapping note: the Sigma selection (action: deny) becomes the SPL filter. The aggregation count(dest_ip) by src_ip > 100 becomes stats dc(dest_ip) plus where. Sigma timeframe: 5m maps to bin _time span=5m, so the count resets per window exactly like Sigma does.

False positives: approved scanners, search engine crawlers, and backup jobs — keep a documented allowlist of their addresses.

Tuning

Start at 100 unique destinations in 5 minutes, then set the final threshold from your own weekly baseline. Add an exclusion lookup for your scan vendor's addresses the day the rule ships.

Splunk SPL — matching search for Rule 2
index=web sourcetype=access_combined
| regex useragent="(?i)(masscan|nmap|gobuster|dirbuster|wfuzz|ffuf|nikto|sqlmap)"
| stats count dc(url) AS urls by client_ip, useragent
| sort - count

Field assumptions: Apache combined logs in index=web sourcetype=access_combined with client_ip, url, useragent, status fields. For IIS use sourcetype=iis and cs_user_agent/cs_uri_stem.

Mapping note: Sigma's UserAgent|contains list becomes one regex with case-insensitive matching ((?i)). The stats line adds the breadth view (unique URLs per client) that Sigma's fields section exposes.

False positives: contracted pentesters and internal QA tools. Record their windows and addresses, and expect empty strings from tools that strip the User-Agent.

Tuning

Many scanners send empty or random User-Agents. Add a second rule for empty useragent with high request rates, so the detection survives string changes.

Rule 3 — IDS Scan Signature Alerts

title: IDS Scan Signature Alerts
id: 5b8e1f4a-2c93-4d71-9e35-6a7c8d9b0c21
status: stable
description: >

fields:

  - dest_ip
  - dest_port
  - signature
  - severity

  - Approved vulnerability scanners run by the security team

Splunk SPL — matching search for Rule 3
index=ids sourcetype=suricata:alert signature="*SCAN*"
| stats count dc(dest_ip) AS unique_dests by src_ip, signature
| sort - count

Field assumptions: Suricata EVE JSON alerts in index=ids sourcetype=suricata:alert (EVE is Suricata's JSON log format). The fields src_ip, dest_ip, dest_port, and signature come from the Splunk Add-on for Suricata. Adjust the index if you archive EVE under a custom name.

False positives: approved scanners and health checks. GreyNoise-style context on the source sorts researcher noise from real threats.

Tuning

Keep an allowlist lookup of your own scanner addresses and exclude it in the search. Send the remaining researcher noise to a weekly dashboard instead of an alert queue.

Rule 4 — Sweep of a Single Port Across Many Hosts

Sigma YAML — single-port sweep on TCP 3389
title: Sweep of a Single Port Across Many Hosts
id: 9d4c7b2e-6f18-4a55-83ab-1e9f0d2c3b47
status: stable
description: >
  One source sends denied connection attempts to the same port (TCP 3389, Remote Desktop
  Protocol, used for Windows remote login) on more than 50 unique hosts within ten minutes.
  This single-port sweep matches IP block scanning in ATT&CK T1595.001 and often precedes
  credential attacks against RDP.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1595/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1595/001/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-20
logsource:
  category: firewall
  product: paloalto
detection:
  selection:
    action: deny
    dest_port: 3389
  timeframe: 10m
  condition: selection | count(dest_ip) by src_ip > 50
fields:
  - src_ip
  - dest_ip
  - dest_port
  - action
falsepositives:
  - Remote-work connection storms when many users reconnect at once
  - Published RDP gateways or jump hosts that legitimately reach many desktops
  - Session brokers that probe endpoints before routing
level: high
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1595
  - attack.t1595.001
Splunk SPL — matching search for Rule 4
index=firewall sourcetype=pan:traffic action=deny dest_port=3389
| bin _time span=10m
| stats dc(dest_ip) AS unique_hosts count AS attempts by _time, src_ip
| where unique_hosts > 50
| sort - unique_hosts

Field assumptions: Palo Alto traffic events in index=firewall sourcetype=pan:traffic. The fields dest_port, dest_ip, src_ip, and action come from the Splunk Add-on for Palo Alto Networks. For Zeek use id.resp_p and id.orig_h.

Mapping note: the two-field Sigma selection becomes two SPL filters. timeframe: 10m becomes bin _time span=10m, and the condition becomes dc(dest_ip) grouped per window and source.

False positives: remote-work connection storms and legitimate RDP gateways that serve many desktops.

Tuning

Exclude your own VPN gateway from this rule — it is the single noisiest false positive. If your users work from big cloud VPN exits, also allowlist those provider ranges.

Rule 5 — Cloud Flow Log Scan Pattern

Sigma YAML — AWS VPC flow log rejects
title: Cloud Flow Log Scan Pattern
id: 2f6a9e31-7c45-48b2-bd84-90c1e5f6a7d3
status: stable
description: >
  AWS VPC flow logs (per-connection records from Amazon Web Services virtual networks) show one
  source address rejected against more than 100 unique destinations within five minutes. This
  matches broad scanning in ATT&CK T1595 and T1595.001 against cloud assets, where edge
  firewall logs are often missing.

  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1595/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1595/001/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-20
logsource:
  category: firewall
  product: aws
detection:
  selection:
    action: REJECT
  timeframe: 5m
  condition: selection | count(dstaddr) by srcaddr > 100
fields:
  - srcaddr
  - dstaddr
  - dstport
  - action
falsepositives:
  - Load balancers and health checkers that contact many instances
  - NAT gateways (shared translation points) aggregating many clients behind one address
  - Deployment tools that fan out to every instance in a subnet
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1595
  - attack.t1595.001
Splunk SPL — matching search for Rule 5
index=cloud sourcetype=aws:flowlogs action=REJECT
| bin _time span=5m
| stats dc(dstaddr) AS unique_targets count AS rejects by _time, srcaddr
| where unique_targets > 100
| sort - unique_targets

Field assumptions: VPC flow logs ingested by the Splunk Add-on for AWS into index=cloud sourcetype=aws:flowlogs. The fields srcaddr, dstaddr, dstport, and action are native flow-log field names.

Mapping note: the single selection field action: REJECT becomes an SPL filter. timeframe: 5m becomes bin _time span=5m. The aggregation condition becomes dc(dstaddr) with where.

False positives: load balancers, health checks, and NAT aggregation behind one shared address.

Tuning

Allowlist your load balancer and NAT addresses first. Consider a companion rule on ACCEPT records — security groups that silently allow traffic still reveal breadth in accepted flows.

Real-World Case Study

A documented example of how one disclosed vulnerability turned the whole internet into a scanning range within hours.

Background: Log4Shell is the nickname for CVE-2021-44228. The flaw sat in Apache Log4j 2, a Java logging library (a component thousands of applications use to record events). It was privately reported in November 2021, then disclosed and published in early December 2021. Within days, attackers had scanned the whole internet for vulnerable Java services. All numbers below are as reported by the vendors named.

Timeline of the scanning wave

  • 24 Nov 2021 An Alibaba Cloud researcher reports the flaw to Apache in a private message.
  • 09 Dec 2021 CVE-2021-44228 (“Log4Shell”) is published; a proof-of-concept (working attack code) spreads within hours.
  • 09–12 Dec 2021 Mass scanning and exploitation attempts worldwide. Cloudflare and other vendors report hundreds of exploit attempts per second at peak. Honeypots record probes from thousands of unique source addresses.
  • 10–16 Dec 2021 CISA (the US cyber defense agency) publishes guidance AA21-356A and urges patching. Attackers deploy cryptominers and botnet payloads; ransomware crews, including Conti, begin targeted intrusions through the same probe-and-exploit pattern.
  • 23 Dec 2021 Apache releases additional fixes for a follow-up bypass, CVE-2021-45046.
  • Jan 2022 Most large organizations complete patching; cleanup of webshells (attacker-planted web pages that run commands) continues for weeks.

Attack chain

The chain ran step by step. Scan for Java services (T1595 / T1595.002). Then send a crafted ${jndi:ldap://…} probe. The vulnerable server contacts the attacker's LDAP or DNS servers (directory and name-lookup services). Payload download follows — ATT&CK T1190, Exploitation of Public-Facing Application — and lands a miner or webshell. Ransomware crews used the same door for targeted cases.

Detection point

The most reliable signal was egress (outbound traffic from your servers): vulnerable servers resolved attacker domains and connected to LDAP endpoints. WAF (Web Application Firewall) and IDS rules caught ${jndi} strings in requests, and honeypots flagged the scanners early.

Containment

Teams applied WAF virtual-patch rules (filter rules that block the attack without changing the code). They blocked known callback domains and addresses. They also restricted server egress so vulnerable systems could not call out.

Eradication

Teams patched Log4j to fixed versions. They removed webshells and rotated exposed credentials.

Recovery

It meant re-scans and log hunts to prove no callbacks remained. Teams then kept monitoring for weeks before closing the incident.

Impact

Most intrusions were opportunistic — cryptominers rather than ransomware. The response cost was still massive: organizations without asset inventories spent weeks finding every Java service they owned.

Team response and communication

Teams ran daily patch war-rooms. They shared IOC lists (indicators of compromise — known-bad addresses and files). Executive updates framed the work as “known, bounded, in progress”. Teams with inventories communicated patch coverage in days, not guesses.

Lessons learned

  • Asset inventory decides your patch speed — you cannot patch what you cannot find.
  • Egress filtering turns exploitation into failed exploitation.
  • Honeypots are cheap early warning; they saw the scanning wave first.
  • Scanning waves are the alarm clock for patch SLAs (service level agreements — promised fix deadlines).

Mitigation and Defense

ATT&CK lists one mitigation for this technique — M1056 Pre-compromise — because the attacker acts outside your controls. You cannot prevent a scan; you can shrink what it finds, log what it touches, and answer it fast.

Policy

  • Run a quarterly external exposure review with named owners.
  • Govern scanner allowlists: approved tools, ranges, and time windows.

Identity

  • Publish only the portals people need (mail, VPN, single sign-on).
  • Hide admin endpoints behind VPN or IP allowlists.

Endpoint

  • Tie patch SLAs (promised fix deadlines) to the CISA KEV catalog.
  • Minimize banners — do not advertise exact software versions.

Network

  • Rate-limit and apply threat-intel blocklists at the edge.
  • Tarpit repeat scanners (slow them so they waste time).
  • Keep egress filtering strict — it blocks scan-callback validation.

Cloud

  • Enable VPC flow logs everywhere, not just in production.
  • Remove public IPs that do not need them.
  • Reconcile cloud-native inventory daily.

Logging

  • Retain flow logs for 90+ days.
  • Make sure deny logs survive volume — do not drop them at the collector.
  • Ship IDS EVE/alert JSON to the SIEM.

Monitoring

  • Alert on breadth: unique destinations and ports per source.
  • Place honeypots on a few unused addresses.

Backup

  • Keep backups immutable and test restores.
  • Remember that scanning waves often precede ransomware.

Response

  • Run a scan-burst runbook: classify, check answered services, block, document.
  • Record every handled source for future hunting.

Home and small office

Router

Update the firmware and disable WAN-side management (admin pages reachable from the internet) and UPnP (automatic port opening).

Devices

Change every default password. Put smart devices on a guest network, away from work laptops.

Watch

Check your router's inbound firewall log once a month. Repeated probes to one port mean a service is exposed.
