DETECTION GUIDE · T1090.002

T1090.002 — External Proxy

An external proxy is a system on the internet that relays traffic between a victim and a command and control (C2) server. The victim talks to the proxy. The proxy talks to the attacker’s server. MITRE lists port redirectors such as HTRAN, ZXProxy, and ZXPortMap, and rented servers or compromised hosts as common proxies. This guide shows you how to catch that relay in your logs.

Guide v2.0 · audited against ATT&CK v20.0

Sub-technique of T1090 Tactic: Command and Control Platforms: ESXi · Linux · macOS · Network Devices · Windows ATT&CK v1.3 · modified 2026-09-08

Key facts

What it is

A rented VPS, a cloud host, or a compromised external system forwards C2 traffic, so the victim never connects to the real C2 server.

Common forms

Port redirectors (HTRAN-style), SSH or netsh forwards, commercial tunnel services, and abused routers or firewalls.

Why it matters

Your logs show only the proxy’s address. Blocking the proxy looks like a win, while the attacker moves to a new one.

How to catch it

Flag flows to hosting ASNs with no business reason, watch for port-forward tools on endpoints, and hunt tunnel-provider domains.

The idea in plain words

MITRE’s description (in simple words): adversaries use an external proxy as a middleman for traffic to a C2 server. This avoids direct connections to the attacker’s own systems. The victim system talks to the proxy on the internet. The proxy then forwards the traffic to the C2 server.

MITRE names three port redirector tools: HTRAN (S0040), ZXProxy, and ZXPortMap. A port redirector is a small program. It listens on one port and forwards every packet to another address.

MITRE also notes the two kinds of proxies attackers pick. Both sit outside the victim network:

  • Purchased infrastructure — a rented virtual private server (VPS) or a cloud host.
  • Compromised systems outside the victim environment — for example a small-office router or a home device that the attacker controls.

MITRE adds a useful detail: attackers choose proxies with a low chance that a connection to them gets investigated. The proxy also masks the true destination of C2 traffic.

Key words in this guide

C2
Command and Control. The channel an attacker uses to send commands to malware and receive stolen data.
Proxy
A middleman system. It receives traffic and passes it on, so the two ends never talk directly.
Port redirector
A tiny server that forwards traffic from one address and port to another. HTRAN is a well-known example.
Beacon
A short, regular check-in message that malware sends to its C2 server.
Egress
Traffic that leaves your network towards the internet.
ASN
Autonomous System Number. The ID of the organization that owns an IP range, for example a hosting company.
VPS
Virtual Private Server. A cheap rented server in a data center.
LOLBin
A normal, signed system tool (for example netsh or ssh) that attackers misuse, so it looks harmless.

How it fits into a larger attack chain

An external proxy almost never stands alone. It serves the C2 stage of a longer operation. Here is the usual order:

StageWhat happensHow the external proxy helps the attacker
Before: Initial AccessPhishing, a stolen password, or an exposed service gives the attacker a foothold.Phishing and credential attacks can also ride proxy exits, so the source looks harmless.
Before: ExecutionThe victim runs the implant, often from a script or a document.Tooling and updates can be pulled through the proxy.
This technique: C2The implant beacons to the external proxy; the proxy forwards to C2.Hides the C2 address, adds resiliency, and lowers the chance of investigation.
After: Lateral MovementThe attacker reaches more systems with the session the C2 channel provides.New victims can join the same proxy, so one relay serves many hosts.
After: ObjectiveData theft, ransomware, or sabotage.Stolen data can leave through the same innocent-looking proxy.

Why detection is hard, and why it matters

Hard part one: your logs stop at the proxy. You see an encrypted connection to a rented server. The real C2 server, its owner, and its location stay invisible.

Hard part two: the proxy looks ordinary. A hosting ASN (network ID of a data center company) or a consumer broadband IP can carry business traffic too. Many teams allow all outbound port 443, so nobody looks at these flows.

It matters because C2 is the attacker’s lifeline. Cut it and most intrusions stall. Miss it and the attacker keeps access for months. The good news: the relay must enter and leave your network, and that moment always leaves records. This guide teaches you to find them.

Written story: the same eight steps in words

This list is the text alternative to the animation. Each item matches one chip in the player.

  1. Step 1 — Renting the VPS. The attacker rents a small VPS from a hosting company. On it, they install a port redirector in the style of HTRAN. The redirector listens on one port and forwards all traffic to the attacker’s real C2 server. Defender view: nothing yet — the rental is private, but hosting ASNs are a known risk group.
  2. Step 2 — The first beacon. The implant on the victim workstation wakes up and beacons. The packet leaves through the company firewall on port 443 and lands on the VPS. The firewall logs a new outbound TLS flow. Defender view: a new flow to a hosting-ASN IP with no business reason is the first clue.
  3. Step 3 — Forwarded to C2. The redirector on the VPS forwards the beacon to the real C2 server. This leg never touches your network. No log inside your company can record it. Defender view: your logs stop at the proxy IP; everything after it is invisible.
  4. Step 4 — The reply comes back. The C2 server answers. The reply travels from C2 to the VPS, then back through your firewall to the implant. Commands now flow over the tunnel. Defender view: return traffic looks like ordinary TLS from a hosting IP.
  5. Step 5 — The blind spot in one picture. The SOC sees a steady flow from one workstation to one rare hosting IP. It cannot see what happens behind the VPS. The operator, the C2 address, and the protocols stay hidden. Defender view: treat any steady flow to a rare hosting IP as a question, not as noise.
  6. Step 6 — The bypass attempt. The implant tries to reach the C2 server directly. The egress policy denies the connection. The implant then falls back to the proxy path. Defender view: one denied direct flow to the C2 IP is a loud and useful clue.
  7. Step 7 — Following the log trail. The analyst enriches the proxy IP with its ASN. It belongs to a hosting provider, and your business has no service there. The analyst checks which other hosts talk to the same ASN. Defender view: the trail shows only the proxy, but the ASN is a pivot point.
  8. Step 8 — Block and hunt. The team blocks the proxy IP and the whole hosting ASN at the firewall. They hunt the same beacon pattern across all hosts to find other victims. Defender view: block the relay, then hunt — rotation means more proxies may already exist.

Red Team Perspective

This section explains why attackers use external proxies and how they set them up. It is written for defenders who want to recognize the mechanics. Everything below stays at the level of standard administration commands.

When do attackers use this technique?

They use it when they need a C2 channel that survives IP blocks. A direct connection to the attacker’s own server exposes that server. One block and the implant is orphaned. A relay absorbs the damage: blocking the relay costs the attacker only a small rented server. Attackers also use it when they must keep a session alive across connection loss, because they can point the implant at a stable proxy address.

How do they set it up?

The classic setup has two parts. First, the attacker gets a proxy: they rent a VPS, buy cloud capacity, or take over an exposed external host. Second, they install a port redirector on it, such as HTRAN, ZXProxy, or ZXPortMap. The implant is then configured with one address: the proxy. The redirector forwards every connection to the real C2 server.

Where does it appear in the environment?

From your side, the technique shows up at exactly two places. Inside: the endpoint process that opens the tunnel, for example ssh or netsh. Outside: the egress flow to the proxy’s address and port. Everything between the proxy and C2 happens on infrastructure you do not own and cannot log.

Why does it work so well?

Three reasons. One: most egress rules allow outbound port 443 to anywhere. Two: a hosting ASN serves thousands of honest customers, so a single flow looks normal. Three: MITRE notes that attackers pick proxies that defenders are unlikely to investigate, which keeps the relay cheap and quiet for months.

Ethics and scope

This section exists to help defenders practice. Use these ideas only in a lab you own. The examples below connect two lab virtual machines on one isolated network. They show the relay mechanics so you can recognize the log patterns. Never point these commands at systems you do not own.

Lab-only example 1 — a port redirector with netsh

Windows ships netsh, which can act as a port redirector. In the lab, VM A (192.168.56.10) forwards its port 8443 to VM B’s port 443. Any client that connects to VM A on 8443 now lands on VM B — the same mechanics attackers use on a rented VPS.

Lab VM A (Windows) — port redirector
# Lab only — run inside your own isolated test network
# Forward port 8443 on this VM to port 443 on the lab "C2" VM
netsh interface portproxy add v4tov4 listenport=8443 listenaddress=192.168.56.10 connectport=443 connectaddress=192.168.56.20

# Show the active forward (this is also your detection query on the endpoint)
netsh interface portproxy show all

# Remove it after the exercise
netsh interface portproxy delete v4tov4 listenport=8443 listenaddress=192.168.56.10

Lab-only example 2 — a reverse forward with SSH

On Linux and macOS, ssh -R opens a listening port on a remote server and pulls traffic back through the tunnel. In the lab, VM A opens port 8443 on VM B and sends anything that arrives back to VM A’s own web service. Attackers use the same flag to expose internal services, so this pattern belongs in your detection tests.

Lab VM A (Linux) — reverse SSH forward
# Lab only — run inside your own isolated test network
# Open port 8443 on the lab relay VM and forward it back to local port 443
ssh -N -R 8443:127.0.0.1:443 labuser@192.168.56.20

# In another terminal, confirm the listener appeared on VM B
ssh labuser@192.168.56.20 "ss -ltn | grep 8443"

Lab-only example 3 — walking a request through a SOCKS relay

Tor and many other relays expose a local SOCKS5 port. A tool such as curl can send one request through it. The destination then logs the relay’s exit address, not your address. This is the same log signature a proxy relay leaves behind.

Lab VM A (Linux) — SOCKS5 through a local relay
# Lab only — assumes a local SOCKS5 relay is listening on port 9050
# Send one harmless request to the lab web server through the relay
curl --socks5-hostname 127.0.0.1:9050 http://192.168.56.20:8080/ -v

# On VM B, the access log now shows the relay exit, not VM A.
# That difference between "who connected" and "who logged in" is the whole point.

Attacker mistakes that create detections

  • They reuse the same rented proxy across incidents, so one indicator unlocks old cases.
  • They test the relay from the office or the home network before pointing malware at it.
  • They leave the LOLBin trail behind: ssh, plink, or netsh portproxy commands with rare flags.
  • They choose hosting providers whose ASN your business has never used, which makes the flow rare.
  • They rotate proxies too fast, so one host talks to many relay IPs on one port within an hour.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Here is what an external proxy relay leaves in your telemetry, where to look first, and a triage flow your team can run today.

Where to look first

The official ATT&CK data sources for this technique are all network sources. Map them to the products you already run:

ATT&CK data sourceTypical product / logWhat it gives you
Network Traffic: Network Connection CreationSysmon EID 3, Windows Security 5156, EDR network eventsWhich process opened the connection, and to which address and port.
Network Traffic: Network Traffic ContentZeek conn/http/ssl/dns logs, egress web proxy logs, TLS inspectionDomains, user agents, SNI values, and TLS details of the session.
Network Traffic: Network Traffic FlowNetFlow/IPFIX, firewall session logsWho talked to whom, how often, how much data, and on which port.

What “normal” looks like

  • Workstations talk to a stable set of business SaaS domains and CDNs.
  • Servers mostly answer requests; they start few outbound connections.
  • Network devices rarely start outbound sessions, except NTP, DNS, and log shipping.
  • Each host has a known, repeatable weekly rhythm of destinations.

What “suspicious” looks like

  • A workstation keeps a steady daily flow to one rare hosting-ASN IP.
  • ssh.exe, plink.exe, or netsh.exe runs with remote-forward or portproxy flags.
  • DNS queries for tunnel-provider domains such as *.ngrok-free.app.
  • A network device opens outbound sessions to addresses that are not NTP or DNS.
  • One host reaches many external IPs on the same port within one hour.

Seven-step triage checklist

  1. Confirm the flow. Pull the firewall record: src_ip, dest_ip, dest_port, bytes, first and last seen. Ask: is the destination rare for this host?
  2. Enrich the destination. Add ASN, ASN type (hosting, consumer, business), and reputation. A hosting ASN with no business link is a strong hint.
  3. Find the process. On Windows, join the flow to Sysmon EID 3 and EID 1. Which process, which user, which command line?
  4. Check the shape. Beacon relays repeat: similar bytes, similar interval, every day. Human browsing does not.
  5. Check for fallback evidence. Look for a denied direct flow to the same destination that appears seconds or minutes earlier.
  6. Scope the blast radius. Search the last 30 to 90 days for the same destination IP, ASN, or port across all hosts.
  7. Decide the verdict. Ask three questions: Is the destination approved? Does the business own it? Does the process have a reason? If any answer is no, escalate to incident response.

When to escalate to incident response

  • The relay process is unknown, unsigned, or runs from a user-writable folder.
  • The same hosting ASN appears from more than one host.
  • The flow survived a firewall block — the implant switched to another relay.
  • Tunnel-provider domains resolve from servers or from many hosts at once.
  • You find a portproxy or SSH forward on a host nobody can explain.

Quick wins this week

  • Turn on Sysmon EID 3 (network connection) logging on all Windows servers.
  • Ship firewall session logs to the SIEM with 90 days of retention.
  • Add an ASN-type enrichment field to egress logs at ingest.
  • Run one hunt: top 20 hosting-ASN destinations per workstation. Investigate everything that is not a cloud backup or an update service.

A six-step intel workflow

  1. Collect. Pull indicators and reports from the sources above on a fixed schedule.
  2. Process. Normalize them into one format: IP, ASN, domain, port, first seen, expiry.
  3. Analyze. Rank items by how well they match this technique: port redirectors, hosting ASNs, tunnel domains.
  4. Apply. Load the ranked list into lookups, Sigma rules, and firewall blocks.
  5. Review. Each month, measure which items produced true hits and which expired.
  6. Feed back. Send misses and new findings back to your sources and to your ISAC.

5.4 Logs and Patterns

For each threat from 5.3: the logs to collect, what the pattern looks like, one realistic example event, and tuning notes. All examples are synthetic.

Threat 1 — Beacon to a rented VPS/cloud proxy

Log sources: firewall session logs, NetFlow/IPFIX, Zeek conn and ssl logs, with ASN enrichment. Pattern: a host keeps a steady, near-daily flow to one external IP on one port. The ASN is a hosting provider, and your business has no service there. Bytes per session stay in a narrow band.

Firewall session log — Example (synthetic)
# Example - synthetic. Assumed index=firewall sourcetype=pan:traffic,
# enriched with dest_asn and dest_asn_type at ingest.
<140>1 2026-02-03T03:12:04+00:00 fw-edge-01 panos 1 - traffic \
  action=allow src=10.20.4.31 dst=185.199.72.44 dest_port=443 proto=tcp \
  bytes=4821 sessions=1 app=ssl rule=allow-outbound-443

# Same src/dst pair repeats at 04:12, 05:12, 06:12 with 4.6-4.9 KB sessions.
# Suspicious parts: hosting ASN, no business owner, metronome rhythm.

Tuning: build a per-host baseline first; alert only on destinations that are rare for that host. Suppress your approved SaaS, backup, and update ranges.

Threat 2 — LOLBin external forward

Log sources: Sysmon EID 1 process creation, Windows Security 4688, EDR process events, and the matching Sysmon EID 3 network events. Pattern: ssh.exe, plink.exe, or netsh.exe runs with a remote-forward or portproxy flag, and soon after, a new outbound connection appears.

Threat 3 — Commercial tunnel service egress

Log sources: DNS query logs (Zeek dns, resolver logs), egress web proxy logs, Sysmon EID 3 joined to EID 1. Pattern: a process resolves a tunnel-provider subdomain and then connects to it. The subdomain is usually a random name the attacker picked.

Log sources: identity provider sign-in logs, VPN logs, mail gateway click records, with ASN-type enrichment. Pattern: a successful sign-in arrives from a hosting ASN with a new device, shortly after the user clicked a phishing link. No new executable appears on the endpoint.

Threat 5 — Compromised external host as proxy (Quad7-style)

Log sources: firewall session logs, NetFlow, network-device telemetry, asset-inventory enrichment. Pattern: a network device starts outbound sessions to addresses that are not NTP, DNS, or log shipping. The destination port is often a management port such as 8443 or 8291.

Threat 6 — Proxy rotation across several external endpoints

Log sources: firewall session logs, NetFlow/IPFIX. Pattern: one host connects to many external IPs on the same destination port within a short window. Single flows look harmless; the fan-out is the signal.

5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

One Sigma rule and one Splunk SPL query for each threat from 5.3, in the same order. Every rule is stable, and every query states its index and sourcetype assumptions in comments.

Note — field assumptions

Sigma is an open YAML format for detection rules. SPL is Splunk’s Search Processing Language. Field names differ between vendors, so each SPL block starts with # comments that state the assumed index, sourcetype, and fields. The examples use: index=firewall sourcetype=pan:traffic for firewall sessions, index=edr sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational for Sysmon, index=dns sourcetype=zeek:dns for DNS, and index=identity sourcetype=azure:aad:signin for sign-ins. ASN enrichment fields (dest_asn_type) come from your lookup at ingest or in search.

Rule 1 · Outbound C2 beacon to a rare hosting-ASN IP (Threat 1)

Mapping: T1090.002; data sources: Network Traffic Flow + Network Connection Creation. Sigma→SPL field map: action → action; dest_asn_type → dest_asn_type; the count() by condition → the stats ... | where connections >= 10 step. Expected false-positive volume: low after the approved-range suppression; a handful of hits per week in a mid-size network.

Sigma — beacon_to_rare_hosting_asn.yml
title: Outbound C2 Beacon to a Rare Hosting-ASN IP
id: 7c1e5a9f-2d34-4e6b-9a18-3f52c7d0e4b1
status: stable
description: >
  Detects allowed outbound connections from an internal host to an external IP
  whose enriched ASN type is hosting. Adversaries relay C2 traffic through
  port redirectors on rented VPS or cloud hosts (T1090.002), so a steady flow
  to a hosting ASN with no business owner is the core signal. The matching
  Splunk search adds a volume threshold per source and destination pair.
references:

Splunk SPL — matching search
# Assumptions: firewall session logs in index=firewall with
# sourcetype=pan:traffic. Fields: src_ip, dest_ip, dest_port, action.
# ASN enrichment at ingest adds dest_asn and dest_asn_type. If you enrich
# in search instead, add your ASN lookup before the stats step.

Mitigation and Defense

What stops an external proxy relay: the official MITRE mitigation first, then layered prevention, detective controls, and a plan for home and remote workers.

Official MITRE mitigation

MITRE lists one official mitigation for T1090.002: Network Intrusion Prevention (M1031). An IPS can recognize and block relay traffic patterns on the wire. Everything below this note is practitioner advice that goes beyond the official list.

Prevention by layer

LayerHow it helps against external proxies
Network segmentationA relayed implant that lands on one segment reaches less, so the relay carries fewer commands.
Egress filtering and allow-listsDestinations must be approved. A rented VPS is not on any list, so its flows get denied or flagged.
DNS filteringBlocks tunnel-provider domains and freshly registered relay names before a connection opens.
Web proxy with TLS inspectionShows domains and paths inside port-443 flows, so a relay looks less like ordinary browsing.
Network Intrusion Prevention (M1031)The official mitigation. Signatures and protocol checks spot redirector behavior on the wire.
Block Tor and tunnel providersRemoves the cheap relay options, which pushes attackers to infrastructure that is easier to flag.
Harden network devicesStrong admin access, no exposed management, and current firmware stop Quad7-style takeover.
Asset inventoryFeeds the src_role and owner fields that make Rules 1, 5, and 6 possible.
Patch edge devicesCloses the bugs that turn your own routers and firewalls into attacker infrastructure.

Detective controls

  • Log pipeline: firewall sessions, NetFlow, DNS, and Sysmon EID 1/3 all land in the SIEM with 90 days of retention.
  • Sigma deployment: the six rules from Section 5.5 run daily, with lookups under change control.
  • Alert triage: the Section 4 checklist turns a hit into a verdict in under 30 minutes.

Enterprise actions

This quarter

  • Deploy an IPS policy for relay signatures (M1031) and measure its blocks.
  • Move outbound access from allow-all-443 to an approved-destination model, one business unit at a time.
  • Add ASN-type enrichment to egress logs at ingest.

Standing practice

  • Run the six Sigma rules and review hits weekly.
  • Keep the tunnel-provider blocklist and relay-IP lookups updated.
  • Patch edge devices on a fixed schedule and track it to 100 percent.

Home and remote workers

Remote workers sit on the same networks attackers abuse, and their routers can become someone else’s relay. Share these steps with every remote worker:

  • Update the router firmware, or replace routers that no longer get updates.
  • Turn off port forwarding and remote administration on the home router.
  • Use the company VPN and reputable antivirus on every work device.
  • Use a DNS filter that blocks tunnel-provider and malware domains.
  • Keep work on the work profile or the work account, separate from family devices.
  • Report anything strange: a device that is slow while idle, new admin accounts on the router, or lights that flash at night with no activity.

Top detections (see Section 5.5)

  • Rule 1 — outbound beacon to a rare hosting-ASN IP (the core C2 signal).
  • Rule 2 — LOLBin reverse forward or portproxy to a public IP.
  • Rule 3 — DNS query for a commercial tunnel provider.
  • Rule 4 — sign-in from a hosting ASN on a brand-new device (phishing-proxy echo).
  • Rule 5 — network device opens an unexpected outbound session.
  • Rule 6 — one host reaches many external IPs on one port within an hour (rotation).

Response checklist

  • Pull the full flow record for the relay: src_ip, dest_ip, port, bytes, first and last seen
  • Find the process that opened the connection (Sysmon EID 3 → EID 1) and capture the command line
  • Enrich the relay IP with ASN type and reputation; check VirusTotal history
  • Isolate the host and block the relay IP and its /24 at the firewall
  • Hunt the same ASN, port, and rhythm across all hosts for the last 90 days
  • Look for a denied direct-to-C2 flow near the first beacon (fallback evidence)
  • Remove persistence, reset credentials, and rebuild if the host cannot be trusted
  • Share the relay indicators with your ISAC and record the lessons learned

If you only remember one thing

Your logs stop at the proxy, but the proxy must come to you. A steady flow from one host to a rare hosting-ASN IP, or a normal system tool forwarding a port to the internet, is how this technique betrays itself. Find it, block the whole relay range, and then hunt every other host that talks the same way.
