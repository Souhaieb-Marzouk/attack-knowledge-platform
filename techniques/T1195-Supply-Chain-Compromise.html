T1195 Supply Chain Compromise

Attackers do not always break down the front door. Sometimes they change the lock before the door is even delivered. This technique means an attacker tampers with a product, or with the way a product is built and shipped, before you receive it. When you install the product, you install the attacker's code with it, and you trust it because it comes from a vendor you believe in.

  • Tactic: Initial Access
  • Platforms: Linux · SaaS · Windows · macOS
  • Sub-techniques: 3 (T1195.001 / .002 / .003)
  • ATT&CK since: 18 April 2018 · Version 1.7
  • Detection difficulty: Hard
  • Guide v2.0 · checked against MITRE, October 2026
What it is
Malicious code or hardware is hidden inside a product before it reaches the buyer.
Why it is serious
The payload arrives through a trusted, often signed, vendor channel, so normal trust checks pass.
Known cases
SolarWinds (2020), CCleaner (2017), NotPetya via M.E.Doc (2017), 3CX (2023), XZ Utils (2024), ASUS Live Update (2019).
Main defense
SBOMs, vendor vetting, egress monitoring, signature allowlists, and fast patch intelligence.

    1

    Overview

    This section explains the technique in plain words, shows where it sits in an attack, and lists the facts you will use again and again.

    What the technique is

    Supply Chain Compromise means an attacker changes a product, or the path a product travels, before it reaches you. The "supply chain" is the long road from raw code to your machine: developers write code, tools and libraries are added, the code is built, signed, packaged, and then delivered through update servers, app stores, resellers, or hardware factories.

    An attacker can touch any point on that road. They can poison a code library, break into a build server, hijack an update, or even insert a tiny chip into hardware at a factory. The victim receives a product that looks and behaves normally, but the attacker controls a hidden part of it.

    Why it matters

    Most security tools trust vendor software. If your firewall only allows signed installers from known vendors, a backdoored signed installer passes every check. That is why supply chain attacks scale so well: one poisoned build can reach thousands of organizations at once, and each victim invites the attacker in by themselves.

    The 2020 SolarWinds case showed this clearly. About 18,000 customers downloaded a trojanized update, and the attackers then picked only the most interesting hundred or so for hands-on spying. One weak point at the vendor became a door into banks, government agencies, and security companies.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    In MITRE ATT&CK, this technique lives in the Initial Access tactic. It replaces the usual phishing or exploit attempt with something the victim installs on purpose. The malicious code usually then runs (Execution), reaches out to attacker servers (Command and Control), and spreads toward valuable data. Because the first step happens outside your network, at the vendor, your first useful signal often appears only after installation.

    Written version of the simulation

    If you cannot use the animation, or you prefer reading, this is the same story in words. Each step matches one step of the diagram.

    1. Study the vendor. The attacker team spends weeks learning everything about a software vendor called SecureSoft: which product you use, how updates are built, and who works there. Defender view: nothing is visible. This happens entirely outside your network.
    2. Break into the build system. Using stolen passwords from a phishing email, the attacker logs into the vendor's build server. Your network still sees nothing. Defender view: nothing, unless the vendor later discloses the breach.
    3. Plant the implant. The attacker adds a small piece of malicious code to the product's source code in the build. The change is quiet and only activates under special conditions. Defender view: still nothing. The poison is now inside the vendor's pipeline.
    4. Ship a signed update. The vendor's normal build process runs, tests pass, and the company signs the new version with its own trusted certificate. The update server offers it to all customers, including you. Defender view: a routine update appears. Signature checks pass, because the signature is real.
    5. Your fleet installs it. Your deployment tool pushes the update to hundreds of workstations overnight, exactly as designed. The implant sleeps quietly. Defender view: hundreds of "update successful" events. Nothing looks wrong.
    6. The implant calls home. On a timer, the implant on each machine resolves a strange domain, for example a made-up subdomain under avsvmcloud.com, and contacts attacker infrastructure. Now there is traffic. Defender view: partial signal. Egress logs show a signed, legitimate-looking process talking to a domain nobody has seen before.
    7. The SOC connects the dots. An analyst notices that a rare domain is contacted by hundreds of hosts at the same time, all through one vendor process. Threat reports confirm the domain belongs to a known campaign. An incident is opened. Defender view: alert raised and confirmed.
    8. Contain and notify. The SOC blocks the domains, isolates affected hosts, removes the trojanized update, and contacts the vendor, which ships a clean version. Procurement starts asking vendors hard questions. Defender view: contained, with lessons captured for next time.
    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    To detect something well, you must understand why attackers like it. This section explains the attacker's goals, the general path they follow, and what they need to succeed. Everything stays at the level of ideas and safe lab work.

    Why attackers use this technique

    A supply chain attack trades effort for trust. Instead of fighting your defenses, the attacker makes your own administrator install their tool for them. The benefits from the attacker's view are simple:

    • Trust bypass. Signed vendor software passes application allowlists and AV scans.
    • Scale. One poisoned build reaches every customer of that product at once.
    • Stealth. Traffic from a legitimate vendor process blends into normal noise.
    • Persistence. Updates keep coming, so the attacker can re-enter with each new version until it is found.

    The cost is also simple: it takes months of patient work, and it burns the vendor when discovered. That is why this technique is mostly used by well-resourced groups, and why attacks of this kind are rare but severe.

    How an attacker thinks about it

    1. Pick the road, not the door. Map the victim's suppliers: which software do they all run? Which hardware? Which update tool?
    2. Find the weakest point of the road. Options include a developer's laptop, a code repository, a build server, a signing machine, an update server, or the shipping path for hardware.
    3. Get in quietly. Credential theft, a job-offer lure to a vendor engineer, or exploiting an internet-facing vendor system. The goal is access, not destruction.
    4. Poison at the best spot. A dependency (a part), the product build (the whole), or the device firmware (the machine itself).
    5. Stay patient. Sleep, blend with normal vendor traffic, and activate only for chosen targets. Loud implants burn the whole operation.

    Where it applies and what it needs

    This technique applies to any environment that installs software or buys hardware, which means every environment. It works best when three conditions are true:

    • The vendor has a working build-and-delivery pipeline the attacker can reach.
    • Customers trust and auto-install vendor updates.
    • Nobody at the vendor is watching the pipeline for unusual changes.

    Common variations are listed in the parent technique's three sub-techniques: poisoning a dependency (T1195.001), poisoning the software product or its distribution (T1195.002), and poisoning hardware or firmware (T1195.003).

    Safe lab exercise: build your own mini pipeline

    You can learn the mechanics without touching anyone else's systems. In a private, offline lab on machines you own:

    1. Set up a tiny local package registry, for example a private npm or PyPI mirror running in a container.
    2. Publish two versions of a harmless "hello world" package: version 1.0 clean, version 1.1 with an extra function that only writes a marker file.
    3. Build a small client that installs the package, and log every download and process it creates.
    4. Point your logging at a SIEM or even a simple file, then study which events would have exposed version 1.1: the new file, the hash change, the install-time process.
    5. Repeat with a signed installer: sign it with your own test certificate and watch which of your logs record the signer and the hash.

    The lesson transfers directly: every poisoning step leaves a fingerprint somewhere. Your job as a defender is to make sure that fingerprint lands in a log you actually read.

    Common attacker mistakes

    • One C2 domain for everyone. Thousands of hosts resolving the same new domain is a giant flag.
    • Too-broad activation. Running the implant everywhere instead of filtering for chosen victims multiplies detection chance.
    • Touching the build too early. Changes committed by a brand-new account are easy to spot in version control history.
    • Reusing known tools. Public malware frameworks get flagged quickly once the first sample is analyzed.

    What makes an operation succeed

    • Operational security. Separate identities for research, access, and implant work.
    • Deep product knowledge. Knowing where the product's own traffic hides the implant's traffic.
    • Target filtering. Activating only on chosen networks, often by checking a public IP or a domain name.
    • Exit plan. Ability to pull the implant or stop the channel when pressure rises.

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    You cannot watch the vendor's build room, but you can watch everything that crosses your own boundary. This section shows what a supply chain implant looks like from inside the victim network, which artifacts it leaves, and how to triage fast.

    How defenders can see it

    Before installation, you depend on outside help: vendor disclosures, CERT advisories, and threat intelligence feeds. After installation, the implant must eventually behave like any other malware: it runs, it connects out, it touches disk or memory. Those are your windows. The strongest defenders combine three views:

    • Egress view. Every connection leaving the network, with the process and user that caused it.
    • Endpoint view. Process trees, image loads, file writes, and signature details from tools like Sysmon or an EDR agent.
    • Inventory view. What versions of what software run where, ideally with a software bill of materials (SBOM) per application.

    Artifacts it leaves behind

    Process
    A vendor product process spawning a shell or scripting host, which the product never does normally.
    Network
    Newly registered or rare domains contacted by the vendor process, often with odd subdomain patterns.
    Files
    Unexpected DLLs next to a signed executable, or dropped loaders in temp folders.
    Hashes
    Installer hashes that differ from the vendor's published values.
    Identity
    Cloud tokens or service accounts used from hosts that never used them before.

    Where to look first

    • DNS and proxy logs for domains registered in the last 30 days, or domains with long random subdomains (a classic beacon-encoding trick).
    • Sysmon or EDR process trees for signed vendor binaries with children they should never have, such as cmd.exe or powershell.exe.
    • Software inventories to answer "who has version X installed?" within minutes, not days. This is the single biggest time-saver in a supply chain incident.
    • Vendor advisories and CISA alerts subscribed per product, so a disclosure reaches a human the same day.
    • Cloud audit logs for abnormal token use right after big software rollouts.

    Why it matters

    A supply chain implant arrives with admin-level trust. If you only collect AV alerts, you may see nothing at all until the attacker takes a second action. But if you collect egress and process data, the implant's first call home is often a clean, high-confidence signal, because legitimate vendor products rarely talk to brand-new domains. That is why egress monitoring is the highest-value control for this technique.

    Short triage flow

    1. Scope the install. From inventory, list every host with the flagged product and version. This list is your blast radius, meaning the set of machines the attack could reach.
    2. Pull egress history. For those hosts, search DNS, proxy, and firewall logs for the reported indicators and for lookalike domains.
    3. Check process history. Look at the parent-child chains around the vendor process. Note any shell, script host, or unknown child.
    4. Decide the state. Installed but silent, beacons seen, or hands-on activity? The response differs for each.
    5. Contain in order. Block indicators at the boundary, isolate active hosts, then remove the product and rebuild rather than "clean".
    6. Assume credential theft. Treat all secrets on affected hosts as compromised and rotate them.

    Watch out

    Do not judge by the signature alone. In every major supply chain case, the malicious code was validly signed by the vendor. Signatures answer "who built this?", not "is this safe?". Pair signature checks with reputation, behavior, and allowlists.

    Known actors and campaigns

    • Nobelium / APT29 (SolarWinds, 2020). State-linked espionage via a trojanized Orion update; high confidence from multiple vendors and government advisories. MITRE also tracks SUNSPOT, the build-system implant used to insert SUNBURST.
    • UNC2452 activity (same cluster). Overlap in naming between vendors; treat names as labels, not facts.
    • CCleaner intrusion set (BARIUM, 2017). Build compromise with targeted stage-two delivery at technology firms.
    • Labyrinth Chollima / Lazarus (3CX, 2023). DPRK-linked; Mandiant tracks it as UNC4736. The 3CX attack began with an earlier compromise of an old trading program inside the 3CX environment, which MITRE calls a second-order supply chain compromise.
    • "Jia Tan" (XZ Utils, 2024). A long-term cover identity, attributed with medium confidence to a state-linked actor.
    • Ember Bear (G1003) and OilRig (G0049). Listed by MITRE for T1195: they compromised IT providers and software developers to reach final victims through their suppliers.
    • Sandworm Team (G0034). Staged compromised versions of legitimate software installers on forums; also delivered NotPetya through the M.E.Doc update mechanism in 2017.
    • Lumma Stealer and Raccoon Stealer. Distributed through cracked software downloads — the low-cost version of this technique.

    Confidence levels, in practice

    High
    Multiple independent sources or government confirmation; you can alert on it.
    Medium
    One credible source, no second sighting; hunt with it, do not block on it alone.
    Low
    Single unverified post or a vague report; keep as context, document why.

    Write confidence into every intel record. It decides whether an indicator drives an alert, a hunt, or only a note.

    Applying the intelligence

    1. Match to what you run. Only intel about products in your inventory is actionable; keep that inventory fresh.
    2. Turn reports into hypotheses. Example: "if our vendor agent were trojanized, it would resolve a new domain; check egress baselines".
    3. Watch indicators age. Domain IoCs die fast; TTP-level intel (signed process talking to new domain) stays useful for years.
    4. Feed the loop back. Record which sources produced real detections, and drop the noisy ones.

    5.4 Logs and patterns

    For each threat: where the evidence lives, which fields to read, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and a realistic event example. Field names follow common formats; adjust them to your own schema in the mapping notes of section 5.5.

    TH-1 — Signed vendor agent beaconing

    Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 3 (network connection) and Event ID 1 (process creation) via Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational; DNS logs; proxy logs.

    Fields that matter: Image, ParentImage, DestinationHostname, DestinationIp, Initiated, Hashes, User.

    Normal behavior: the vendor agent talks to a small, stable set of vendor domains on a fixed update schedule. Parent processes are the service host or the product's own launcher.

    Suspicious behavior: the agent resolves long, random subdomains; contacts a domain first seen in your environment; or appears as a parent of a shell.

    Detection logic: alert when a known vendor agent process opens an outbound connection to a domain that (a) is not on the vendor allowlist and (b) is either newly observed fleet-wide or has a high-entropy subdomain. Correlate with process creation for shell children.

    Sysmon Event ID 1 — vendor agent spawning a shell (JSON)
    {"EventTime":"2026-01-09 03:41:22","EventID":1,"Computer":"APP-WEB-04",
 "User":"NT AUTHORITY\\SYSTEM",
 "Image":"C:\\Windows\\System32\\cmd.exe",
 "CommandLine":"cmd.exe /c whoami /all",
 "ParentImage":"C:\\Program Files (x86)\\VendorSuite\\VendorAgent.exe",
 "Company":"VendorSuite Inc.","Signed":true,
 "Hashes":"SHA256=9D2F44A6C01B88E3F5A7D1C2290B4E6D8A3F70C5B2E19D4477AA03BB18C5D601"}

    TH-2 — Upstream repository or pipeline tampering

    Log sources: GitHub audit log stream (github.audit), branch protection events, webhook events; vendor status and security pages (via your intel process, not logs).

    Fields that matter: action, actor, repo, created_at, user, ip.

    Normal behavior: branch protection rules change rarely, through known admin accounts, during working hours, with a linked change ticket. Webhooks are created by automation accounts to approved targets.

    Suspicious behavior: branch protection removed or weakened by an account with no history of admin work; a webhook pointing to a non-corporate domain; admin grants followed by workflow changes within minutes.

    Detection logic: alert on protection-rule destruction and on webhook creation where the target host is not in an approved list. For your own vendors, monitor their public repos the same way: a tampered upstream repo is your problem one build later.

    GitHub audit log — branch protection deleted (JSON)
    {"created_at":"2026-01-07T02:14:09Z","action":"protected_branch.destroy",
 "actor":"svc-ci-admin","user":"svc-ci-admin","repo":"vendor-corp/payment-core",
 "branch":"release/2026.1","ip":"203.0.113.88","user_agent":"ghcli/2.40",
 "org":"vendor-corp"}

    TH-3 — Build runner pulling from a non-standard registry

    Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 1 on build runners; proxy logs for pypi.org, registry.npmjs.org, registry-1.docker.io; CI platform logs (Jenkins, GitHub Actions).

    Fields that matter: Image, CommandLine, ParentImage (CI agent), proxy url_host, url_path, CI job_name, runner.

    Normal behavior: runners fetch packages only from your internal mirror (Artifactory, Nexus). The mirror itself syncs from public registries through a fixed service account.

    Suspicious behavior: a runner process passes --index-url, --registry, or --extra-index-url pointing outside the mirror; or proxy logs show a runner host talking directly to a public registry.

    Detection logic: two angles: (a) process creation on runners where the package tool receives an index or registry argument, or where curl/wget fetches a package archive directly; (b) proxy rules that block and log runner subnets reaching public registries, then alert on hits.

    Sysmon Event ID 1 — runner using an external index (JSON)
    {"EventTime":"2026-01-08 11:02:51","EventID":1,"Computer":"BLD-RUN-02",
 "User":"NT AUTHORITY\\SYSTEM",
 "Image":"C:\\Python312\\python.exe",
 "CommandLine":"python -m pip install -r req.txt --index-url https://pypi.example-cdn.net/simple",
 "ParentImage":"C:\\Tools\\jenkins\\agent.exe",
 "JobName":"payment-core-nightly"}

    TH-4 — Network appliance egress through the proxy

    Log sources: proxy access logs; DNS logs; firewall session logs for the appliance or IoT VLANs.

    Fields that matter: client_ip (device), url_host, user_agent, action, DNS query, firewall rule.

    Normal behavior: devices on camera, badge, printer, and network-gear VLANs generate almost no web traffic, and never use the corporate proxy. Their DNS is limited to vendor NTP and update hosts.

    Suspicious behavior: any proxy authentication from a device subnet; DNS queries to newly registered domains; periodic small TLS flows to the same external IP at fixed intervals.

    Detection logic: alert when a source IP inside a device VLAN appears in proxy logs at all, or when device VLAN DNS returns answers outside an allowlist. Treat "device used the proxy" as a high-confidence anomaly.

    Proxy access log — printer VLAN using proxy (JSON)
    {"ts":"2026-01-09T05:12:03Z","client_ip":"10.44.7.21","vlan":"printers",
 "method":"CONNECT","url_host":"cdn-metrics-eu.example-updates.net",
 "url_port":443,"user_agent":"-","action":"blocked",
 "status":403,"bytes_out":1187,"bytes_in":0,"policy":"device-vlan-deny"}

    TH-5 — Hardware swap caught by device identity

    Log sources: Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-PnP/Configuration Event ID 400/410; Sysmon Event ID 13 (registry) for USBSTOR; DHCP lease logs; NAC/802.1X logs; asset inventory diffs.

    Fields that matter: DeviceInstanceId, DriverName, DHCP mac, hostname, NAC auth_result, inventory serial.

    Normal behavior: a workstation's device set is stable for months. Serial numbers in inventory match the physical asset tags. MAC addresses map one-to-one to recorded hardware.

    Suspicious behavior: the same internal slot reports a new device instance ID or serial; a "new" device appears with a known model but unknown serial; NAC sees a known MAC on a different switch port in a different building.

    Detection logic: diff device-instance and serial data against the asset register weekly; alert on any change for server and workstation-internal components. This also catches mixed-part swaps made during repairs.

    Kernel-PnP Event ID 410 — disk serial changed on same controller (JSON)
    {"EventTime":"2026-01-10 08:22:41","EventID":410,"Computer":"SQL-PRD-01",
 "DeviceInstanceId":"SCSI\\DISK&VEN_SAMSUNG&PROD_MZQL21T9\\4&2A7B1C0&0&000100",
 "DriverName":"disk.sys",
 "PreviousInstanceId":"SCSI\\DISK&VEN_SAMSUNG&PROD_MZQL21T9\\4&2A7B1C0&0&000101",
 "InventorySerialMatch":false,"AssetTag":"SRV-004221"}

    TH-6 — Installer from outside the approved channel

    Log sources: Windows Application log, MsiInstaller Event ID 1033/1034; Sysmon Event ID 1 for installer processes; EDR file-signature data.

    Fields that matter: EventCode, ProductName, PackageCode, User, installer Image, CommandLine, signature Issuer.

    Normal behavior: installs come from your software distribution tool (Intune, SCCM) or an approved admin path, with the publisher certificate you expect for that product.

    Suspicious behavior: an MSI for a well-known product installed by a regular user from Downloads, signed by a certificate that is valid but different from the vendor's usual one, or completely unsigned.

    Detection logic: alert when MsiInstaller events show a product from your approved-software list, but the installing user is not the deployment service account, or the certificate issuer differs from your allowlist entry for that product.

    5.5 Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    Real-World Case Study: SolarWinds SUNBURST (2020)

    The clearest public example of T1195 in action is the SolarWinds compromise. One build system, one implant, and about 18,000 organizations installed attacker code signed by a vendor they trusted. The story below follows the attack and the response end to end.

    Context

    SolarWinds Orion is an IT monitoring platform. It runs with very high privileges because its whole job is watching networks, and it updates itself automatically. That combination made it a perfect supply chain target: compromise the build once, and the attacker reaches the most privileged monitoring tool inside thousands of networks at the same time.

    The intrusion was later attributed by multiple governments and security vendors to a group tracked as Nobelium, APT29, and other names, assessed with high confidence to be Russia's SVR foreign intelligence service. The operation targeted espionage goals, not money.

    The implant in one paragraph

    SUNBURST was inserted into Orion builds from February 2020 onward in a way that survived code signing. (The attacker had reached SolarWinds' build systems in September 2019 and first ran a small test injection.) The implant slept for about two weeks, then checked a long list of security tools, and only then resolved a hostname like xxx.appsyncsvc.us-east-1.amazonaws.com under the attacker domain avsvmcloud.com. The subdomain itself encoded victim information, so the attacker's DNS server could decide which victims got a second-stage payload. Most victims got nothing; roughly a hundred high-value ones were selected for further access.

    Timeline

    1. September 2019First access. The attacker breaks into SolarWinds' build environment and runs a small test injection of code into Orion builds. Source control and the build pipeline both show later signs of tampering.
    2. February – June 2020Trojanized updates ship. From February 2020 the attacker inserts SUNBURST into Orion builds; updates carrying the implant (versions 2019.4 HF5 through 2020.2.1 HF1) are released between March and June 2020, validly signed by SolarWinds, and reach about 18,000 customers.
    3. Mid-2020Hands-on phase. On selected networks, a second stage (TEARDROP, RAINDROP) loads Cobalt Strike beacons. Attackers move into cloud and identity systems, reading email at some targets.
    4. December 2020Discovery. FireEye detects its own breach in November 2020 and publishes on 13 December. On the same day, CISA issues Emergency Directive 21-01, ordering federal agencies to disconnect affected Orion systems.
    5. December 2020 – 2021Global response. SolarWinds ships clean builds, organizations rebuild affected systems, rotate credentials, and review cloud estates. The incident reshapes SBOM and software provenance policy in the US (Executive Order 14028, May 2021).

    Attack chain

    • Vendor access
    • →
    • Build poisoning (T1195.002)
    • →
    • Signed update installed
    • →
    • DNS-encoded beacon
    • →
    • Second stage on chosen targets
    • →
    • Cloud and mail espionage

    Detection point

    The first public detection did not come from an alert rule; it came from a security company noticing its own intrusion during an investigation (announced on 13 December 2020). After disclosure, defenders found the most reliable technical signals were:

    • Orion processes resolving avsvmcloud.com subdomains (DNS and proxy logs).
    • SolarWinds.BusinessLayerHost.exe as a parent of shells and script hosts (process telemetry).
    • TLS flows from Orion servers to unusual AWS-hosted endpoints with no business reason.

    Takeaway

    None of these signals needed a 0-day. They needed egress logs and process trees, collected before the day they were needed.

    Containment and eradication

    Containment: block avsvmcloud.com and related sinkhole domains at DNS and proxy layer; disable Orion internet access; isolate hosts where the implant had called out; kill and disable the Orion improvement program service.

    Eradication: treat every Orion host as compromised: uninstall the product, rebuild from trusted media, and hunt for second-stage implants (TEARDROP, RAINDROP, Cobalt Strike) and any hands-on activity that followed. Every credential that lived on those hosts, including service accounts and cloud tokens, was rotated.

    Recovery

    Clean Orion versions were verified and redeployed with restricted network paths. Monitoring returned in stages: first read-only from a jump host, then normal operation after hardening. Cloud estates were reviewed for rogue app registrations and OAuth grants before business units regained trust in them.

    Impact

    About 18,000 customers received the trojanized update; fewer than a hundred are known to have been actively targeted. The U.S. government, several federal agencies, security vendors, and major companies were affected. The financial and reputational damage to SolarWinds was severe, and the incident drove new federal software security requirements.

    Team response and communication

    Effective responses ran a dedicated incident bridge, published daily internal status with a simple "who is affected" answer, and coordinated disclosure with CISA and the vendor. Teams that struggled had no software inventory, so they spent their first days just answering "do we run Orion?"

    Lessons learned

    • Know what software you run and at which version, everywhere, within minutes — an SBOM is the cheapest insurance in this scenario.
    • Egress monitoring from servers is not optional; the beacon was visible for months in DNS logs.
    • Signatures prove origin, not safety; pair them with behavior monitoring.
    • High-privilege monitoring products deserve the same scrutiny as domain controllers.
    • Practice the vendor-breach scenario before it happens: who calls whom, who blocks what, who rebuilds.

    Mitigation and Defense

    No single control stops a supply chain attack. Defense works in layers: reduce what can be poisoned, slow down what slips through, and detect fast when it lands. This section covers enterprise controls by domain, advice for home users, and a prioritized action list.

    ATT&CK mitigation mapping

    MITRE lists six official mitigations for T1195. They map to the controls below like this:

    • M1013 Application Developer Guidance — choose third-party libraries carefully and lock dependency versions.
    • M1033 Limit Software Installation — pull from internal, verified repositories instead of external ones.
    • M1046 Boot Integrity — secure boot and TPM, mainly against hardware and firmware tampering (T1195.003).
    • M1051 Update Software — patch process covering unused or abandoned dependencies and components.
    • M1016 Vulnerability Scanning — continuous monitoring plus automated and manual code review.
    • M1018 User Account Management — least privilege for software execution, no needless admin rights.

    Enterprise: policy and people

    • Vendor risk program. Rank suppliers by criticality; require SBOMs and breach-notification clauses for critical software.
    • Advisory duty. Someone owns watching CISA, vendor bulletins, and ISAC feeds for the products you run, every working day.
    • Approved channel rule. Software installs only via the deployment tool; exceptions are documented and time-boxed.

    Enterprise: identity and secrets

    • Phishing-resistant MFA for engineers, admins, and especially vendor-side build accounts.
    • Least privilege for CI. Build and deploy credentials live in a secrets manager, are short-lived, and are scoped per pipeline.
    • Credential rotation drills. Assume certs, tokens, and keys on a compromised host are burned; practice rotating them fast.

    Enterprise: endpoint and software

    • Signed-installer allowlist that checks signer and channel, not just a valid signature.
    • SBOM per critical product so "are we exposed to component X?" takes minutes.
    • Fast inventory. Answer "who runs product P, version V?" in under 30 minutes.

    Enterprise: network and logging

    • Egress visibility everywhere, including servers and device VLANs, with new-domain alerting.
    • Baseline per vendor agent: expected domains, ports, and schedule; alert on deviation.
    • Collect the logs in section 5.4; uncollected evidence equals undetected attacks.

    Enterprise: cloud, backup, response

    • Review OAuth grants and app registrations after any big software event; attackers pivot to cloud quickly.
    • Immutable backups with restore tests, so rebuild-from-known-good is realistic.
    • Vendor-breach playbook: scope, block, isolate, rotate, communicate, with owners named in advance.

    Home and small office

    • Update from the vendor's own app or the OS store only; never from a search result link.
    • Skip "free" versions of paid software; cracked tools are the classic mini supply-chain attack.
    • Turn on automatic updates for OS, browser, router firmware, and IoT devices.
    • Use a separate network (guest or IoT SSID) for smart devices.
    • If a vendor announces a breach of their installer, wait for their clean version and change the related passwords.

    Key facts

    • Confirm the vendor disclosure or your own detection; capture version and hash facts.

    • Rotate every credential that lived on affected hosts, including cloud tokens.
    • Remove and rebuild affected hosts from known-good media.
    • Notify stakeholders; coordinate with the vendor on a clean build before reinstalling.
    • Write the timeline into the incident record; update this guide's rules with what you learned.
