T1071.004 DNS

Every network must answer name lookups, so DNS is the one door nobody wants to close. Attackers walk through it by hiding orders and stolen files inside queries that look like ordinary lookups. This guide shows what that looks like in your logs and how to close the door without breaking the internet.

1

Overview

What DNS tunneling is, why attackers love it, and what you will learn on this page.

This page is version 2.0 of this guide, re-checked against MITRE ATT&CK in October 2026 (sub-technique page version 1.4, last modified 3 September 2026).

DNS (the Domain Name System — the internet's phone book) turns names like example.com into IP addresses. Every laptop, server, and printer asks DNS questions hundreds of times a day. Because of that, almost every firewall lets DNS out, even on networks that block nearly everything else.

MITRE ATT&CK T1071.004 describes attackers who abuse that trust. Malware encodes small pieces of data into the names it looks up. A query for mfuhq3ttq2f7w2ndogq7c3u62bvdvibqbfgs5iynha3deq.sync-log.net looks like an ordinary lookup, but its 46-character first label is a small encoded message. The answer — often a TXT record (a DNS record type that can hold free-form text) — carries orders back to the malware. The same trick moves stolen files out, a few characters per query. Security teams call this a DNS tunnel.

In the attack chain this sits in Command and Control (C2) — the channel an attacker uses to send orders to malware. The same channel also carries exfiltration (stolen data leaving the network), which ATT&CK tracks separately as T1048. Attackers pick DNS because it survives strict firewalls, needs no direct internet connection from the victim, and looks boring in logs.

The numbers explain why it matters. A 100 KB document can leave a network as about 4,000 small queries spread over 40 minutes, without opening one suspicious port. Each 46-character label carries roughly 29 bytes of data. The OilRig group kept tunnels like this alive for months (see the case study in section 6). Two cheap habits — logging all DNS and learning the query shapes — turn this blind spot into one of your strongest signals.

DNS in one minute

Resolver
The server your devices ask first. On this page it is 10.10.0.53, the internal corporate resolver.
Authoritative server
The server that owns a zone (a domain family) and gives the final answers. Attackers run their own.
Record types
A maps a name to an address. TXT holds text. NULL and CNAME are older or indirect types that tunnels also abuse.
DoH and DoT
Short for DNS over HTTPS and DNS over TLS. Both wrap DNS in web-style encryption, so the queries hide from network logs.
Label
One part of a name, between dots. The 46-character string in the example above is one label. Labels may hold up to 63 characters.
TTL
Time to live — how long an answer may be cached. Tunnel answers use short TTLs so they can change fast.
Entropy
A measure of randomness. Encoded labels score high; normal words score low.

Animated Simulation

Eight steps of a DNS tunnel, from a quiet implant to containment. The panel on the right shows what the SOC (the security operations center — the team that watches alerts) sees at each step. The written story below the canvas tells the same eight steps in words, so nothing is lost without JavaScript.

The story in words

  1. Step 1 — A dormant implant wakes up. A beacon on FIN-SRV-02 (10.20.8.22) wakes after 13 idle days. It needs a channel that always works, and every network allows DNS. The server makes about two normal lookups per minute, so a quiet channel hides well. Defender view: build a per-host baseline of lookup volume before you can spot a change.
  2. Step 2 — Encode and ask. The implant encodes host information with base32 (a way to write any data using 32 letters and digits) into a subdomain: mfuhq3ttq2f7w2ndogq7c3u62bvdvibqbfgs5iynha3deq.sync-log[.]net. It asks the internal resolver for a TXT record. Defender view: a 46-character random-looking label is not a person typing. Alert on label length.
  3. Step 3 — The answer carries orders. The attacker's authoritative server (ns1.sync-log[.]net, 203.0.113.77) answers with a TXT record that holds a base32 task. The resolver caches the answer and returns it, and the implant decodes the orders. Defender view: servers rarely need TXT answers. TXT from a host that never used TXT is a strong signal.
  4. Step 4 — The 30-second loop. The implant checks in every 30 seconds, always TXT, always the same zone. Over one hour that is 120 small queries, each under 150 bytes. Defender view: per-host periodicity beats per-domain detection. Bin lookups per host per minute.
  5. Step 5 — Files out through names. To steal a 100 KB document, the implant splits it into base32 pieces and sends about 4,000 queries over 40 minutes. No port opens, no large upload happens. The file leaves in name-sized pieces. Defender view: watch volume, label length, and entropy per host. 4,000 TXT in 40 minutes is theft, not lookups.
  6. Step 6 — The resolver sees the spike. DNS analytics compare FIN-SRV-02 with its own 30-day baseline: TXT volume is 210 times normal and label entropy is far above normal. The DNS-VOL-01 rule fires. Defender view: alert on deviation from each host's own baseline, not on one global average.
  7. Step 7 — Confirm the tunnel. The analyst replays the queries: a fixed 30-second cadence, one single zone, encoded answers. A short capture from the host shows the same labels. A sinkhole test proves C2: the resolver answers bad names with a fake address, and the implant keeps retrying it anyway. Defender view: confirm with two independent views, resolver log plus host capture, before you isolate.
  8. Step 8 — Sinkhole and close. The team sinkholes sync-log[.]net on the internal resolver to 127.0.0.1, isolates FIN-SRV-02, and pins all egress DNS to internal resolvers only. DoH and DoT to the internet are blocked so the implant cannot rebuild the channel. Defender view: sinkholing buys time. Fix the egress policy so the next implant finds a closed door.

The simulation uses fictional hosts and documentation IP ranges (10.20.8.22, 10.10.0.53, 203.0.113.77). It is a teaching model, not a packet capture.

3

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

Why attackers pick DNS, how the channel works at concept level, and what you can safely repeat in a lab to build your own detections.

WARNING

This section exists for defense education. It explains attacker thinking so you can build better detections. Run the lab commands only against systems you own, in an isolated lab. Using these techniques against systems you do not own is illegal in most countries and causes real harm.

When and why attackers use DNS

  • It always works. Web traffic can be blocked; DNS rarely is. Even a host that can reach nothing else can still ask the internal resolver.
  • It blends in. Thousands of normal lookups per minute hide a few hundred tunnel queries.
  • It needs no direct internet. Only the resolver needs egress, and it already has it.
  • It is patient. Low-and-slow pacing keeps volume under thresholds for months, which is exactly how OilRig kept tunnels alive.
  • It gives free encoding. Base32 or hex inside names looks random but needs no encryption that a proxy could flag.

Where it applies

Any host that can reach the internal resolver can run this channel. ATT&CK lists the official platforms for T1071.004 as ESXi, Linux, Network Devices, Windows, and macOS. In practice the quiet cases are servers and appliances that nobody watches, because workstations already get more attention.

How it is performed (concept level)

  1. Register a zone that looks harmless, for example sync-log.net, and point it at an authoritative server the attacker controls.
  2. The implant encodes data — base32 or base16 — into the first label of a query name, one small chunk per query.
  3. It asks for A, TXT, NULL, or CNAME records. TXT is popular because each answer string can carry up to 255 bytes.
  4. The authoritative server decodes the label, queues a task, and returns the next chunk inside an answer.
  5. The implant paces the exchange (for example one query every 30 seconds) and repeats, chunk by chunk, for commands and files.

Prerequisites

  • Code execution on one internal host, from phishing or any other entry point.
  • An attacker-controlled authoritative server for the chosen zone.
  • An internal resolver that forwards external queries — the default almost everywhere.
  • Patience: the channel is slow, roughly kilobytes per minute, which is why it avoids volume alerts.

Lab-only examples

Try both on your lab machine, then feed the entropy function real resolver logs. Seeing the difference between an encoded label and a normal word is the fastest way to trust the detection in section 5.5.

Variations you will meet

  • Record-type swaps: NULL, CNAME, or MX instead of TXT to dodge naive rules.
  • Encoding swaps: base16 (hex), raw bytes hidden in EDNS0 (DNS extension) padding, or a custom alphabet.
  • Channel wrap: the same data inside DNS over HTTPS, so even resolver logs see nothing.
  • Fast-flux fronting: short TTLs and many addresses make the zone hard to block cleanly.

Mistakes attackers make (and you should expect)

  • Labels longer than 63 characters, which DNS must reject — the retry storm that follows is loud.
  • A fixed, metronome cadence that stands out once you bin queries per host.
  • One zone reused across victims, so a single detection burns the whole campaign.
  • Bad padding and decode errors, which surface as bursts of NXDOMAIN (the DNS answer that means the name does not exist).

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Defenders do not need new sensors to catch DNS tunneling. The resolver already sees every query — the job is to log it and to read it the right way.

How defenders see it

Every tunnel query must cross your resolver, or it escapes through an unapproved DoH connection. That makes the resolver the best choke point: the logs are cheap, complete, and early. A tunnel shows up as a host that is a little bit weird for a long time. It asks more TXT than others, uses longer labels, keeps a steadier rhythm, and asks names that no other host asks.

Artifacts left behind

  • Long, random-looking first labels, often 30 to 60 characters.
  • TXT (or NULL and CNAME) queries from hosts that never used them before.
  • A fixed request rhythm: the same count per minute, hour after hour.
  • One external zone per host, asked with many different random labels.
  • Short TTLs and answer strings that look like encoded data.
  • NXDOMAIN bursts when decoders misalign or generated names fail.

Where to look

Why it matters and where it fits the chain

DNS tunneling usually appears after the attacker is already inside. The implant already has persistence (it re-arms itself after reboots); the tunnel is its voice and its exit lane. That is why this technique is a high-value alert: it almost always means an active intrusion, not just probing. Catching it early breaks tasking (no more TXT orders arrive) and slows exfiltration at the same time.

Triage flow for a suspected DNS tunnel

  1. Confirm the source: which host sent the queries, and is it a server, a workstation, or an appliance?
  2. Count and shape: TXT or NULL volume over 24 hours, leftmost label lengths, digit-letter mix, and NXDOMAIN count.
  3. Check the zone: registration age, name servers, other domains on the same address, and any threat intel matches.
  4. Rebuild the rhythm: bin queries per minute. A fixed cadence, for example every 30 seconds, is a strong C2 signal.
  5. Sample and decode: capture a few queries from the host, try base32 and base16 on the labels in a sandbox, and read the answers.
  6. Scope: search the last 30 to 90 days for other hosts touching the same zone or name server.
  7. Contain: sinkhole the zone on the resolver, isolate the host, and block direct egress DNS plus DoH and DoT.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence turns "someone does this" into "this zone, this cadence, these actors". Use several source types, because each one covers a different blind spot.

MITRE ATT&CK

T1071.004 (page version 1.4, updated 3 September 2026) groups the behavior; the parent T1071 and the related T1568 entries show adjacent moves. Use it to name detections consistently across teams.

Vendor research

Reports from teams such as Cisco Talos or Unit 42 describe how real tunnels behaved, with label styles, cadence, and tooling details you can turn into analytics.

OSINT feeds

Open-source (OSINT) feeds such as abuse.ch and AlienVault OTX flag tunnel-friendly zones and fast-flux infrastructure. Cheap to test, noisier to trust.

Commercial feeds

Paid feeds add curated C2 domain lists with context. Good for blocking, weaker for behavior analytics on their own.

ISACs and CERTs

ISACs (sector threat-sharing groups) and national CERT teams share what hit neighbors first. Regional CERTs often publish working indicator lists in the middle of a campaign.

Internal telemetry

Your own resolver history and passive DNS are the strongest source: first-seen zones and per-host baselines come from here.

How to work the flow

1. Collect

Pull new zones, C2 domain lists, and actor notes weekly. Store them with dates, so retro hunts stay possible later.

2. Analyze

Join intel with your per-host DNS data. A zone only matters when a real client queried it — count clients, volume, and cadence.

3. Apply

Turn confirmed zones into sinkholes and blocklists, write a Sigma rule for the behavior, and retro hunt 90 days to scope the blast area.

THR-01 — DNS tunneling exfiltration Critical

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns; resolver query logs (Infoblox, Unbound, Windows DNS Analytical log)
Key fields
id.orig_h, query, qtype_name, rcode_name, answers, TTLs
Normal
Short names, mostly A and AAAA, a few TXT for SPF (an anti-forgery record for mail), spread over many domains
Suspicious
Thousands of TXT queries per hour from one host, 40-plus character labels, one single zone
Detection logic
Alert when one client sends more than 100 TXT queries in 15 minutes, or six times its own baseline

The 46-character label is base32 data, and both answer strings use the same alphabet. A file server asking for TXT is already unusual; the label length makes it certain.

THR-02 — TXT-record C2 tasking High

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns; resolver logs that capture answers
Key fields
query, qtype_name, answers, TTLs, id.orig_h
Normal
TXT limited to mail anti-forgery records (SPF, DKIM) and verification strings from mail and cloud services
Suspicious
A server that starts asking TXT and receives long encoded strings back every 30 seconds

The query label is a status report; the answer string is the next order. Only the authoritative server for sync-log.net can produce answers like this, which is exactly why the attacker had to own the zone.

Log sources

Normal

Suspicious

A hit count of 4,128 on one name is not a person. Beaconing shows up as arithmetic: the same count every minute, hour after hour, which a baseline catches even when total volume stays small.

Log sources

THR-06 — Fast-flux C2 domains Medium

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns (answers and TTLs); passive DNS services
Key fields
query, answers, TTLs, id.orig_h
Normal
Stable answers for days or weeks; a CDN (content delivery network) changes addresses only inside documented ranges

5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

The six rules below cover five of the six threats. THR-05 gets two rules: a label-mix rule and a volume rule. THR-06 fast flux leans on the TTL and address-churn logic shown in 5.4, because Sigma cannot express that math. Each card holds a complete Sigma rule (valid YAML with every standard field), a matching Splunk SPL query, the mapping between them, known false positives, and tuning advice.

NOTE

Field names follow Zeek JSON (id.orig_h, qtype_name). If your pipeline renames fields, update both the Sigma rule and the SPL in the same change. The code samples on this page are HTML-escaped in the page source; the copy buttons give you the real characters.

THR-01 — DNS tunneling exfiltration Critical

Mapping note. Sigma counts with an aggregation (count() by id.orig_h over a 15-minute timeframe). The SPL reaches the same result with bin _time span=15m plus stats count, and adds a six-times-baseline branch that also catches low-and-slow tunnels under the hard threshold.

Assumes Zeek DNS logs in index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns — adjust index and sourcetype to your environment.

False positives
Mail security appliances and spam filters doing bulk SPF or DKIM lookups; vulnerability scanners.
Tuning
Exclude resolvers and known appliances from the client set; require the TXT queries to target one external zone; raise the hard threshold if you protect heavy mail infrastructure.

THR-02 — TXT-record C2 tasking High

Mapping note. Both versions look for repeated TXT traffic to the zone from your intel. The Sigma rule keeps the zone inline; in the SPL you can swap the zone per feed. The SPL also counts distinct labels, which helps separate a tunnel from a chatty application.

Assumes Zeek DNS logs in index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns — replace sync-log.net with the zone from your threat intel feed.

False positives
A lab or sandbox zone with the same name style; software that reads config from TXT records at a high rate.
Tuning
Add a first-seen-zone condition so old zones stop alerting; raise the threshold after a week of data; require the client to be a server or an unusual talker.

THR-03 — DNS beaconing High

Mapping note. Tunnel and beacon labels are usually long and machine-made. This rule catches that shape; the volume and cadence rules catch the rest. Sigma matches the pattern with a regular expression; the SPL version measures label length with len(), which is cheaper on large indexes.

Assumes index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns. The eval builds the leftmost label; if your Splunk TA renames fields (for example src instead of id.orig_h), adjust the eval and the stats clause.

False positives
Long DKIM selectors, service-discovery instance names, and CDN cache-buster hostnames.
Tuning
Start at 45 characters and watch your normal maximum for a week; allowlist known encode-heavy vendors; combine with the zone filter from THR-02 for high confidence.

THR-05 — NXDOMAIN bursts and pseudo-DGA probing Medium

Mapping note. The character-mix rule approximates entropy, because Sigma has no math functions. The SPL version counts digits and letters per label, and section 3 shows the exact Shannon entropy formula for offline tuning. The NXDOMAIN rule is a plain count in both languages.

Assumes index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns. The two replace() calls count digits and letters in the label — the SPL way to approximate entropy without external scripts.

False positives
Build systems that name hosts by hash; marketing or telemetry domains with long random parts.
Tuning
Require two digits and a 30-character minimum as a floor. For NXDOMAIN bursts, alert over 50 per client in 5 minutes and weight by distinct domains. Review the top labels monthly and allowlist what you understand.

THR-05 — NXDOMAIN burst (volume view) Medium

Mapping note. This is the volume half of THR-05: both tools count NXDOMAIN answers per client in a 5-minute window. Add a distinct-domain count in SPL when you want to separate DGA-style probing from a retry loop against one dead name.

Assumes index=network sourcetype=zeek:dns. Keep the window small: NXDOMAIN bursts are short, and a wide window dilutes the signal.

False positives
Applications stuck in a retry loop against a removed server; inventory tools that guess names.
Tuning
Weight by distinct names before you page anyone; exclude known scanner subnets; keep a 50-per-5-minutes floor and adjust after two weeks of data.

THR-04 — DoH or DoT to a non-approved resolver High

Mapping note. Both versions match the port branch (TCP 853) and the server-name branch (known DoH providers). The Sigma rule reads a network_connection logsource; in Zeek terms that is a join of conn.log and ssl.log. If you only have conn.log, keep the port branch.

Assumes index=network with zeek:conn and zeek:ssl. The field dest_port may be id.resp_p on raw Zeek JSON — adjust to your Splunk TA before running.

False positives
An approved secure-DNS pilot or management agent; browsers with secure DNS enabled before policy lands.
Tuning
Allowlist your approved resolvers first, then alert on everything else; add new DoH providers to the list quarterly; alert only on egress, not on resolver-to-internet traffic.

Real-World Case Study

OilRig's DNS tunneling operations, 2016 to 2020, as reported by Cisco Talos and regional CERTs. It is the clearest public example of T1071.004 used for months at a time.

NOTE

Public reporting gives date ranges, not exact timestamps, and tooling details changed between campaigns. Treat the timeline below as approximate. This page adds no facts beyond the published reporting.

Context

OilRig is an Iran-linked espionage actor that targeted government and telecom organizations in the Middle East. Its custom implants, HELPEM and ISAgent, speak through DNS almost exclusively. Collected data is encoded into subdomain labels of actor zones, and the authoritative DNS returns tasks inside TXT answers. Pacing is low-and-slow to stay under volume thresholds, and operators could even move files through the tunnel — slow, but reliable and hard to block.

Timeline

PeriodEvent
2016First OilRig campaigns documented by Talos; DNS tunneling observed as the core command channel.
2016–2017A spearphish delivers the implant; the implant installs and persists; the DNS TXT channel is established.
2017–2019Periodic tasking continues for months; collected documents leave through the tunnel in small chunks.
PeriodicallyInfrastructure shifts to new zones whenever old zones are blocked or reported.
2018–2020Talos publishes deep technical reports and indicators; defenders add sinkholes, resolver pinning, and TXT alerting.

Attack chain

  1. A spearphish delivers the implant to a targeted user.
  2. The implant installs and persists on the host.
  3. The DNS channel opens: encoded queries flow to the actor zone.
  4. Tasks arrive inside TXT answers and run for months.
  5. Collected documents are staged, then chunked through the tunnel.
  6. When a zone is burned, infrastructure shifts to a new one.
  7. Researchers publish indicators, and defenders close the zones.
DETECTION POINT

The tunnel was visible in ordinary DNS logs: unusual TXT query volume from a few hosts, long and high-entropy labels, and answers larger than normal. No endpoint telemetry was needed to see the channel — only to find the implant.

Containment

Defenders sinkholed the actor zones on internal resolvers, blocked the name server addresses at egress, and isolated hosts that had spoken to them. Sinkholing is fast and buys time, but the actor answered with new zones, so blocklists needed daily updates.

Eradication

Teams removed the implants and rotated credentials the hosts had touched. They re-ran the new DNS analytics over 90 days of stored logs to find older infections the first pass had missed.

Recovery

Organizations restored steady monitoring, pinned endpoints to internal resolvers, enabled TXT alerting, and watched for re-entry — espionage actors come back to networks they value.

Impact and consequences

The cost was sustained espionage access with long dwell times: months of quiet tasking, and classified or business documents lost. The channel itself was slow, but patience beat speed.

Team response and communication

Regional CERTs and vendor researchers coordinated closely. Indicators moved fast between organizations, and vendor deep-dive reports taught defenders what the traffic looked like before their own logs showed it.

Lessons learned

Log all internal DNS

You cannot detect what you did not record. Resolver and Zeek logs made every later detection possible.

Pin egress DNS

Clients should only reach internal resolvers. Pinning turns a live tunnel into a blocked connection.

TXT from endpoints is rare

Alert on it. Almost no legitimate workload needs TXT answers, which makes the signal strong.

Entropy plus cadence wins

Volume thresholds alone missed low-and-slow tunnels. Label entropy and per-host cadence caught them.

Stopping DNS tunneling is mostly policy plus two cheap rules. The table maps each control area to a concrete action, an owner, the effort, and the expected risk reduction.

M1031 Network Intrusion Prevention means devices that inspect traffic and drop malicious flows. The controls below put both ideas to work.

Home and small office

  • Set the router to hand out one filtering resolver, and remove manual DNS rights from devices where you can.
  • Update the router firmware and turn off remote administration.
  • Home routers rarely log DNS. A small always-on resolver box with logging and blocklists adds visibility cheaply.
  • Watch for smart devices that hard-code public DNS. Most home devices do not need any internet access at all.

Prioritized top five actions

  1. Pin egress DNS to internal resolvers and block direct 53 and 853 — network team, low effort, high risk reduction.
  2. Log all DNS into the SIEM — SOC engineering, medium effort, high risk reduction.
  3. Alert on TXT volume plus label length and entropy per host — SOC detection, medium effort, high risk reduction.
  4. Block DoH and DoT to the internet — network team, low effort, high risk reduction.
  5. Write the sinkhole and isolation runbook — incident response, medium effort, high risk reduction.

Habits that keep the detections honest

Daily

  • Review new TXT and NULL alerts and close each with a written reason.
  • Check first-seen external zones against your intel.
  • Confirm every resolver is still reporting; a silent resolver is a blind spot.

Weekly

  • Look at false-positive counts per rule and tune the noisiest one.
  • Hunt one hour on label entropy for hosts you never hear about.
  • Check alert fatigue: if one analyst ignores a rule, fix the rule instead of muting it.

Monthly

  • Refresh per-host baselines and re-baseline after big changes.
  • Re-run the lab tunnel test and confirm every detection still fires.
  • Review ownership: every rule needs a name and a queue, not "the SOC".

Response checklist

  • Identify the source host and pull its last 24 hours of DNS queries.
  • Measure TXT and NULL volume, label lengths, and NXDOMAIN count against its baseline.
  • Rebuild the cadence per minute; a fixed rhythm is a C2 signature.
  • Check the zone: registration age, name servers, and intel matches.
  • Capture a small sample from the host and try to decode labels in a sandbox.
  • Search 30 to 90 days for other hosts touching the same zone or name server.
  • Sinkhole the zone on the internal resolver and isolate the host.
  • Block direct egress DNS and DoH or DoT so the channel cannot rebuild.
  • Eradicate the implant, rotate credentials, and re-run the new detections over stored logs.
  • Record the case with timestamps and add the zone to your intel process.
