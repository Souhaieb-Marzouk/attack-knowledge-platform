T1071.003 Mail Protocols

Email protocols carry the world's messages, so attackers borrow them twice: first to phish their way in, then to control malware and steal files through mailboxes nobody watches. This page shows what that traffic looks like and how to catch it.

1

Overview

Mail protocols move messages between people and servers. Attackers reuse these same protocols to control malware and to copy files out of a network. This section explains the idea in plain words.

What this technique is

The full MITRE ATT&CK name is Application Layer Protocol: Mail Protocols. In short: malware uses SMTP, IMAP, or POP3 for command and control. C2 (command and control) is the channel an attacker uses to send orders to malware. Sub-technique pages like this one describe one specific way a parent technique can appear. The parent, T1071 Application Layer Protocol, covers many everyday protocols; the .003 child covers mail only.

Three protocols matter here. SMTP (Simple Mail Transfer Protocol) sends mail. Mail servers listen on TCP ports 25, 465, and 587. IMAP (Internet Message Access Protocol) reads and organizes mailboxes on ports 143 and 993. POP3 (Post Office Protocol, version 3) downloads mail on ports 110 and 995. A normal company has a handful of mail servers and one outbound relay (the mail server that forwards company mail to the internet). Almost every other host should never speak these protocols to the internet.

That is why the technique works so well. Mail traffic is normal, and most of it is encrypted. STARTTLS is the command that upgrades a plain mail connection to TLS (Transport Layer Security — standard connection encryption). Mail ports are also often missing from egress rules built for web traffic. Malware can post a stolen file as an "attachment" to an attacker mailbox. It can read its next orders from a "Drafts" folder over IMAP. MITRE's description adds a key point: data can also be concealed within the email messages themselves. To a firewall, all of this looks like someone checking email.

Mail sessions also announce a user-agent — the name of the sending program. Zeek (a free network sensor that produces detailed connection logs) records it before encryption starts. Malware authors often forget to change it, so it becomes a free clue.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Mail protocols show up twice in an intrusion. Early, attackers use real mail to deliver phishing. That is a different technique. Here we look at the second use: after malware is already running inside, mail becomes the C2 channel and the exit path for stolen data. The same sub-technique can therefore map to two tactics: Command and Control, and Exfiltration (attacker-speak for stealing data out of a network).

The protocol angle

Mail is allowed, common, and encrypted. It crosses egress rules that were built for web traffic.

The attacker angle

A mailbox is free C2 infrastructure. No server to rent, no strange port to explain, and replies can carry payloads.

The defender angle

The rule is simple and strong: only known mail servers may open SMTP, IMAP, or POP3 sessions. Anything else is worth an alert.

DEFENDER HINT

The written steps below the canvas are the full text version of this animation.

Written version of all steps

  1. Step 1 — Loader lands. A phished loader — a small program that installs malware — runs on the laptop FIN-WS-04 at 10.20.4.17. It is written in Visual Basic Script (VBS). It skips the corporate relay and signs in to the attacker mail server mail.attacker-mail[.]com (198.51.100.60) on port 587 with hard-coded credentials. Defender view: Zeek shows a workstation speaking SMTP directly to the internet. That single connection is the clearest start signal.
  2. Step 2 — Mail-shaped C2. The malware sends task results as mail bodies and picks up its next orders from replies that carry attachments. A .jpg attachment is really a RAR archive with a new module. Defender view: the client user-agent reads Pterodo/1.0, not Outlook — a free, high-value tell that survives STARTTLS encryption.
  3. Step 3 — Data out as attachments. The malware splits stolen documents into 14 mails of 4 to 8 MB each and sends them to the same attacker mailbox as fake .jpg attachments. Defender view: 78 MB leaves one host in minutes. Volume plus receiver domain makes exfiltration easy to score.
  4. Step 4 — Tasking via IMAP. The operator writes commands into a Drafts folder. Every five minutes the malware logs in over IMAP 993, reads the drafts, and sends nothing back from that folder. Defender view: a workstation polling IMAP on a fixed schedule is rare enough to alert on by itself.
  5. Step 5 — Relay abuse attempt. The malware also tries the corporate relay smtp.fin-example.com, spoofing the sender finance@fin-example.com. The relay demands authentication and rejects the attempt nine times. Defender view: relay auth failures that come from a client IP point to stolen or guessed mail credentials.
  6. Step 6 — Alert fires. The egress rule "workstation to external SMTP or IMAP" fires. The SIEM (the platform that stores logs and fires alerts) joins the two views: network and mail gateway. The host receives legitimate mail but sent 78 MB with no legitimate cause. Defender view: one alert plus one join turns a suspicious flow into a likely incident.
  7. Step 7 — Confirm and scope. The analyst pulls Zeek smtp.log for the host: one receiver domain, one consistent user-agent, subjects that look like finance reports. Neighbor hosts show no similar sessions. Defender view: hunting the user-agent and the domain across 30 days gives a clean scope: one host.
  8. Step 8 — Contain. The SOC blocks attacker-mail[.]com at the egress firewall and the mail gateway, isolates FIN-WS-04, rotates the mailbox credentials, and turns on deny-all outbound SMTP from user ranges. Defender view: the policy fix matters most. The next loader will find a closed door.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains, at concept level, why attackers choose mail protocols and how the pattern works. It stays defensive on purpose: understanding the idea helps you detect it.

WARNING

All content on this page is for defense education. The lab commands below run a private debug mail server on your own computer. Do not point any of these ideas at systems you do not own, and never test against real mail accounts or mail servers you do not control.

When and why attackers use mail protocols

Mail protocols solve three attacker problems at once. First, cost: a mailbox is free, while rented C2 servers expire and cost money. Second, trust: SMTP, IMAP, and POP3 are allowed almost everywhere, and STARTTLS or TLS wraps the session in encryption. Third, reach: mail ports are often forgotten by egress rules (the rules that decide what may leave a network), which focus on web traffic.

Because of this, mail-based C2 appears most often in fast, high-volume espionage operations. These actors care more about output this week than stealth for months. They accept that a blocked mailbox means switching to a new one tomorrow.

How it is performed (concept level)

  1. Channel setup. The malware signs in to an attacker mailbox with SMTP AUTH or IMAP, using credentials hidden inside the loader.
  2. Tasking. The operator places commands in a folder, a draft, or a message body. The malware reads them over IMAP or POP3.
  3. Results and theft. The malware sends command output and stolen files as message bodies or attachments, often renamed to .jpg.
  4. Renewal. When a mailbox or domain gets blocked, the actor rotates to a new one and keeps going.

Where it applies and what it needs

  • An outbound path on 25, 465, or 587 for SMTP, or 143, 993, or 995 for IMAP and POP3.
  • Access to at least one mailbox the attacker controls.
  • A small mail client inside the malware, often just a scripting library.
  • Patience for the slower round-trips that mail adds compared with HTTP C2.

Common variations

  • Relay abuse. Malware signs in to the corporate relay with stolen credentials, so its mail looks internal.
  • Drafts dead-drop. Commands wait in a Drafts folder; no message is ever sent between the two parties.
  • Attachment renaming. Stolen files travel under .jpg or .png names with archive content inside.
  • Scheduled sends. Messages go out at fixed times to look like routine reporting.
  • Odd ports. Mail protocols move to 2525, 3535, or 5870 to dodge simple port rules.

Lab only — run against systems you own

You can watch a complete SMTP conversation without any risk. Run a debug mail server on your own machine, send one test message to it, and read the exchange in the terminal. Nothing leaves your computer.

Reading the output: you will see EHLO, AUTH LOGIN, MAIL FROM, RCPT TO, and DATA in plain text. On a real network, STARTTLS encrypts this conversation after the greeting. That is why metadata matters: ports, sizes, timing, and the user-agent line, which Zeek records before encryption starts.

Mistakes attackers make (and you can detect)

  • They leave a custom user-agent string in the SMTP session.
  • They reuse one receiver mailbox across many victims.
  • They send on a perfect schedule, which turns into a beacon pattern.
  • They keep attachments large, so volume spikes are easy to see.

Each mistake maps to one detection in section 5.5.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Defenders do not need fancy tools to catch this technique. They need one clear policy — mail protocols only from mail servers — and the logs that prove who broke it.

How defenders see it

In a well-configured company, the mail map is small. One relay sends mail out. A few named providers receive mail. User laptops never speak SMTP, IMAP, or POP3 to the internet. Every exception to this map is a signal. Attackers know the traffic looks normal, but the moment a workstation opens a mail session, the map draws itself.

Artifacts left behind

  • A direct SMTP session from a client IP to an external host on port 25, 465, or 587.
  • A custom or missing user-agent string in Zeek smtp.log.
  • Outbound messages with archive-like attachments renamed to .jpg or .png.
  • IMAP or POP3 logins from a workstation on a fixed schedule.
  • Authentication failures on the corporate relay, with client hosts as the source.
  • Firewall flows with high sentbyte counts to a single external mail endpoint.

Where to look

Why it matters

This technique often carries both the attacker's orders and the stolen data. If you miss it, you lose visibility at two stages of the same intrusion. The controls are also cheap: one egress rule and a few log sources protect the most trusted channel in the company.

GOOD PRACTICE

Make "only mail servers may speak mail protocols" a written policy with named owners. A policy that nobody owns becomes a stale allowlist, and stale allowlists are how this technique survives for months.

How it fits the attack chain

After initial access, mail protocols give the attacker a C2 channel (tactic: Command and Control) and an exit path for files (tactic: Exfiltration). Blocking outbound mail from endpoints cuts both at once, which is why this control ranks at the top of section 7.

Triage flow for an outbound-mail alert

  1. Open the alert and note the source host, destination IP, and destination port.
  2. Check whether the source is a known mail server or relay. If yes, close the alert as expected traffic.
  3. Pull Zeek smtp.log for that host: user-agent, from and to addresses, subjects, attachment names.
  4. Sum firewall sentbyte for the last 24 hours to size a possible exfiltration.
  5. Search the same destination and user-agent across all workstations for the last 30 days.
  6. If the traffic is confirmed malicious: isolate the host, block the domain and IP, rotate mail credentials, and open an incident.
  7. Save the evidence, then brief incident response and, where required, compliance and legal teams.

5.4 Logs and Patterns

Each threat below gets one card. It shows where the logs live, the fields that matter, normal versus suspicious behavior, the detection logic, and one realistic event. Field meanings follow each code block.

THR-01 — SMTP exfiltration via direct outbound mail

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:smtp, index=firewall sourcetype=fortigate_traffic
Key fields
id.orig_h (client IP), id.resp_h/dstip (receiver), id.resp_p/dstport, useragent, attach, sentbyte
Normal
Only relay 10.10.0.25 and approved providers speak mail protocols. Laptops appear in web traffic, not in mail logs.
Suspicious
A 10.20.4.0/24 host sends mail straight to 198.51.100.60:587 with user-agent Pterodo/1.0 and .jpg attachments that are really archives.
Detection logic
Alert when a workstation-subnet source opens SMTP to any host except the corporate relay.

Field notes. host is the domain the client announced during EHLO or STARTTLS. from and to are envelope addresses; both point at the attacker here. attach lists attachment names — doc_04.jpg hides a document archive. last_reply is the server's final status, so 250 Ok: queued proves the mail was accepted.

THR-02 — IMAP/POP3 C2 channel

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:conn joined with sourcetype=zeek:ssl (Zeek has no native IMAP analyzer, so you see encrypted sessions)
Key fields
id.orig_h, id.resp_h, id.resp_p, service, server_name, duration
Normal
IMAPS sessions come from mail clients on a handful of hosts, to known providers, at human times of day.
Suspicious
A workstation connects to 993 on an unknown mail domain every five minutes, around the clock.
Detection logic
Alert on IMAP or POP3 ports from the workstation subnet to anything outside the mail subnet, then check periodicity.

Field notes. service: ssl means the payload is encrypted, so ports and timing carry the signal. server_name comes from the TLS handshake (SNI) and names the receiver even when content is hidden. A 5-minute session rhythm across hours is the C2 poll pattern.

THR-03 — Corporate relay abuse with stolen credentials

Log sources
index=mail sourcetype=postfix_syslog (relay logs)
Key fields
client (IP in brackets), sasl_method, sasl_username, queue ID, message-id
Normal
Relay authentication comes from application servers and mail clients with known accounts; failures are rare and from single users.
Suspicious
Repeated SASL failures from a workstation IP, followed by a success with an account that normally never logs in from a client host.
Detection logic
Alert on relay auth failures from the workstation subnet, and on successful client logins with unusual accounts.

Field notes. The bracketed value after client= is the connecting IP. sasl_method and sasl_username come from SASL — the login step mail clients use to authenticate to the relay. Here the account is finance@fin-example.com, a shared account that should never log in from FIN-WS-04. The queue ID (9C41B2F6) lets you follow this message through the rest of the relay log.

THR-04 — Malware user-agent and client anomalies

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:smtp
Key fields
useragent, id.orig_h, id.resp_h, subject
Normal
Known products only: Outlook, Microsoft Office, Thunderbird, Apple Mail, plus printers and monitoring tools on an allowlist.
Suspicious
Any user-agent outside the allowlist, especially short names, script-library banners, or version-only strings like Pterodo/1.0.
Detection logic
Alert on SMTP sessions whose user-agent is present but not in the known-good list.

Field notes. useragent is read from the SMTP banner exchange before STARTTLS encrypts the session, so it is visible in Zeek even when message content is not. Treat the allowlist as the rule: anything unlisted is worth a look, even if it turns out to be a printer.

THR-05 — Bulk scheduled-mail beaconing

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:smtp, index=firewall sourcetype=fortigate_traffic
Key fields
ts (send times), id.orig_h, sentbyte, message count per window
Normal
Human mail has irregular timing and small sizes; bulk sends go through the relay with approved tools.
Suspicious
Small messages at exact intervals — for example 10:00:00, 10:10:00, and 10:20:00, each within one second of the mark.
Detection logic
Alert when one host sends more than 20 messages or more than 50 MB in 10 minutes, or when send times repeat on a fixed interval.

Field notes. The message itself is tiny — the signal is the clock. The same event appeared again at 10:10:00 and 10:20:00, always within one second of the interval, always empty of attachments. That is a check-in beacon wearing a mail costume.

THR-06 — Mail-protocol recon and probing

Log sources
index=network sourcetype=zeek:conn
Key fields
id.orig_h (scanner), id.resp_h/id.resp_p (target port), duration, orig_bytes, conn_state
Normal
External hosts connect to your mail server on one port, complete a session, and leave. Scanners do not appear at all.
Suspicious
One external source touches 25, 465, and 587 on the relay within a minute, with short sessions and small byte counts.
Detection logic
Alert when one source opens more than 30 mail-port connections in 5 minutes, or hits several different mail ports.

Field notes. uid is Zeek's connection ID. duration of 0.412 seconds and orig_bytes of 2148 fit a banner grab, not a real message. conn_state: SF means the session finished normally, which scanners often achieve while collecting server banners. The same source hit ports 465 and 587 within the next 40 seconds.

5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

Six Sigma rules, one per threat, each paired with a matching Splunk SPL query. Copy, adapt the index names, and tune with your own baselines. Every rule includes the attack.t1071.003 tag so the rules stay traceable to this technique.

THR-01 — Direct outbound SMTP from a workstation

SEVERITY: HIGH   The single highest-value rule on this page. It fires on the exact behavior in simulation steps 1 to 3.

Mapping note. Sigma watches Zeek's smtp.log directly. The SPL keeps the same three ideas: source subnet (id.orig_h), mail ports (id.resp_p), and an exclusion for the relay (id.resp_h).

Assumption: index=network sourcetype=zeek:smtp — adjust the index and sourcetype names to your environment.

False positives
Approved mail providers on 587; a lab host running a test client; a migration tool pulling mail from a legacy server.
Tuning
Turn the relay exclusion into an allowlist of named providers and ranges. Add a useragent column to the alert so analysts see the client at once.

THR-02 — IMAP or POP3 from a workstation to an external host

SEVERITY: HIGH   Catches the Drafts-folder tasking pattern from simulation step 4 and any POP3 backdoor traffic.

Mapping note. The Sigma rule uses conn.log because IMAPS traffic is encrypted; only ports and timing are visible. The SPL mirrors the port list and the mail-subnet exclusion.

Assumption: index=network sourcetype=zeek:conn — adjust the index and sourcetype names to your environment. Grouping by 5 minutes makes repeated polls obvious in the results table.

False positives
Approved BYOD mail clients; a one-time migration tool; a mobile-device mail test.
Tuning
Keep a named allowlist of providers. Add a follow-up search that flags sources with sessions in at least four consecutive 5-minute bins, which is the poll rhythm from THR-05.

THR-03 — Relay authentication failures from an internal client

SEVERITY: HIGH   Shows that someone inside is trying stolen or guessed mail credentials against your relay.

Mapping note. Sigma reads postfix syslog lines and pulls the client IP out of the bracketed client= field with a regex (a text pattern that matches part of a line). The Splunk rex does the same extraction, then cidrmatch keeps only workstation sources.

Assumption: index=mail sourcetype=postfix_syslog — mail relay syslog. Point cidrmatch at your own user ranges and adjust the failure threshold to your baseline.

False positives
A password change that leaves one user locked out for a few minutes; a monitoring script with an expired service account.
Tuning
Threshold of 3 failures in 10 minutes suits a 250-host network; raise it if password resets are common. Also alert on successful logins from client IPs with accounts that normally log in only from servers.

THR-04 — Non-standard mail user-agent

SEVERITY: MEDIUM   The easiest metadata rule in the set: malware clients rarely pretend to be Outlook.

Mapping note. Sigma requires a user-agent to be present, then removes known-good products with a contains-list. The SPL inverts this into a NOT search, which reads better in Splunk and returns the same rows.

Assumption: index=network sourcetype=zeek:smtp — adjust the index and sourcetype names to your environment.

False positives
Printers, scanners, copiers, HVAC and UPS monitors, backup-to-mail jobs, and any script that sends status mail.
Tuning
Build the allowlist from 30 days of your own useragent values, then review every new string weekly. Add printer and scanner hostnames as a filter rather than widening the UA list.

THR-05 — High outbound mail volume from a single host

SEVERITY: MEDIUM   The volume rule that caught the 78 MB exfiltration in simulation step 3.

Mapping note. Sigma aggregates with count() by srcip inside a 10-minute timeframe. The SPL shows both thresholds from the brief — 50 MB (52,428,800 bytes) or more than 20 messages — in one stats call.

Assumption: index=firewall sourcetype=fortigate_traffic — FortiGate traffic logs. Adjust the index, the user ranges, and the two thresholds to your environment.

False positives
A one-off newsletter send; a monthly invoice batch; a department moving mail between providers.
Tuning
Baseline sentbyte per subnet for two weeks before fixing 50 MB. Exclude the relay host from the source range so relay traffic never triggers the rule.

THR-06 — Mail port probing and non-standard mail ports

SEVERITY: LOW   Precursor activity. Alone it is noise; in context it warns you before the exfiltration rules ever fire.

Mapping note. Sigma keeps to one count threshold so the rule stays spec-valid. The SPL adds a second branch: at least three different mail ports from one host in five minutes. Splunk aggregation makes that easy, and it catches slow scanners that the count alone would miss.

Assumption: index=network sourcetype=zeek:conn — adjust the index and sourcetype names to your environment.

False positives
Your own vulnerability scanner; a mail migration appliance; a load balancer health check.
Tuning
Filter scanner and mail-gateway hosts by name. Keep the low severity, but route matches to the same triage flow as section 4 — probing often precedes real mail abuse.

Real-World Case Study

Gamaredon is tracked as UAC-0010 by CERT-UA, Ukraine's computer emergency response team. In 2022 the group ran a persistent document-theft campaign against Ukrainian organizations, and mail protocols were its exit door. The case below follows CERT-UA advisories and Unit 42 research (Palo Alto Networks' threat research team).

Context

Gamaredon is a persistent, fast-moving espionage actor. Its tooling leans on Visual Basic Script and SFX archives (self-extracting archives that unpack and run when opened), spreads with a USB worm, and steals documents in bulk. For exfiltration over SMTP, stolen files were sent as attachments to attacker-controlled mailboxes. The infrastructure rotated weekly, which kept static blocklists from catching up.

Timeline

Date or quarterEvent
Early 2022CERT-UA and partners track a wave of espionage intrusions against Ukrainian organizations, later tied to Gamaredon (UAC-0010).
Day 0A spearphish delivers an SFX or VBS loader. When opened, it starts the mail channel to attacker inboxes.
Days 1 to 7A USB-worm component spreads to removable drives and jumps into adjacent networks.

ContinuouslyThe actor rotates mailboxes and domains, so blocklists age within days.

Attack chain

  1. A spearphish delivers an SFX or VBS loader to an employee.
  2. The loader runs, registers scheduled tasks, and opens the first SMTP session to an attacker inbox.
  3. A USB-worm component copies itself to removable drives and spreads to other networks.
  4. Scheduled tasks collect documents from file shares and endpoints.
  5. Pterodo-style tooling sends the archives as SMTP attachments to attacker inboxes.
  6. Infrastructure rotates weekly; tasking continues from new mailboxes.
  7. CERT-UA publishes IOCs (indicators of compromise — clues such as domains and file hashes you can block), which enables blocking and cleanup across many organizations at once.
DETECTION POINT

Public reporting highlights two signals: endpoint-initiated SMTP sessions to rare domains, and custom mail user-agent strings, matched against CERT-UA IOC lists. Both are exactly the detections in rules 1 and 4 of section 5.5 — this campaign is detectable with cheap, well-placed logs.

Containment

Teams isolated affected hosts first. They blocked attacker domains and IPs at the egress firewall and at the mail gateway. Then they disabled the malicious scheduled tasks that kept feeding the channel.

Eradication

Analysts removed VBS loaders and the USB worm, cleaned or reimaged affected USB drives, and rebuilt endpoints where the worm had spread. Cleaning drives mattered: the worm made re-infection easy.

Recovery

Services were restored, and extended monitoring stayed in place because the actor returns quickly. IOC lists were refreshed from CERT-UA on a regular schedule instead of once.

Impact and consequences

The campaign caused document theft from military and government organizations, real intelligence loss, and repeated re-compromise that raised response costs. The mail channel itself was cheap for the attacker and hard to stop with endpoint tools alone, which is why egress policy became the lesson of the case.

Team response and communication

CERT-UA coordinated the response across organizations and published IOCs quickly. Rapid IOC sharing between affected sectors meant that one team's blocklist became every team's blocklist within days.

Lessons learned

Mail egress policy is cheap and high-value

Deny outbound SMTP from endpoints. Most of this campaign's exfiltration would have failed at the first hop.

Fast rotation defeats static blocklists

The actor rotated infrastructure weekly. Only automated intel feeds, refreshed from CERT-UA, kept up.

USB control matters in high-risk sectors

The worm spread through removable drives. Device control in sensitive zones removes that whole path.

Custom user-agents are stable signals

Malware mail clients log their own names. Zeek smtp.log made them visible even under STARTTLS.

NOTE

Public advisories describe the tooling and the flow of the campaign, but exact victim counts, internal timelines, and mailbox names are not fully public. This case study follows CERT-UA advisories and Unit 42 reporting and adds no invented facts.

Mitigation and Defense

This technique is one of the few that a single well-placed control can nearly erase. MITRE lists two official mitigations for it. M1037 Filter Network Traffic limits which servers and systems may start outbound connections. M1031 Network Intrusion Prevention watches traffic and blocks hostile sessions on the wire. The table below lists the full defense stack, then narrows it to five actions worth doing first.

Home and small office

Small networks get most of the same protection with three steps. Block outbound port 25 on the router so only the mail provider's own relay is reachable. Use your provider's submission port 587 with app passwords instead of the account password. If no computer in the house needs POP3 or IMAP from the internet, block those ports too. Then check the router log once in a while for home machines that reach odd mail ports.

Habits that keep the detections healthy

Daily

  • Review every outbound-mail alert and record its true/false verdict.
  • Check that Zeek smtp.log and relay logs arrived on time.
  • Skim new receiver domains that appeared in mail-port sessions.

Weekly

  • Tune the noisiest of the six rules; log what changed and why.
  • Review relay authentication failures and any new sasl_username values.
  • Hunt for user-agent strings that are new to the environment.

Monthly

  • Audit allowlist ownership and remove entries nobody defends.
  • Replay the lab mail exercises from section 3 against the rules.
  • Verify retention and completeness of mail, Zeek, and firewall logs.
NOTE

Alert fatigue is the real failure mode of mail detections. Six quiet, high-quality rules beat forty loud ones. When a rule fires more than it informs, tune it or retire it.

Response checklist

  • Isolate the affected host; keep it powered on if your plan collects memory first.
  • Block the receiver domain and IP at the egress firewall and at the mail gateway.
  • Rotate mail credentials and revoke app passwords for affected users.
  • Hunt the user-agent and the receiver domain across 30 days to scope the intrusion.
  • Check the relay for successful logins from client IPs and unusual accounts.
  • Disable malicious scheduled tasks; remove the loader and any worm copies.
  • Clean or replace USB drives that touched affected hosts.
  • File the incident report and share sanitized IOCs with your ISAC or CERT.
