1

Overview

This section explains the technique in plain words, why your own website is the risk, and what this guide will teach you.

What is T1594?

T1594, called Search Victim-Owned Websites, describes attackers browsing, crawling, and probing websites that the victim owns. They read what is published on purpose: departments, office locations, staff names and roles, business partners, and job ads. They also look for what is published by accident: old files, backup folders, admin panels, sitemap entries that name internal systems, and version-control folders such as .git.

ATT&CK notes that attackers may go beyond browsing and probe for hidden directories and files. They do this with automated wordlist scanning, listed as Wordlist Scanning (T1595.003), and by reading files like sitemap.xml and robots.txt, which are meant for search engines but also hand attackers a map of the site.

Why it matters

Everything an attacker needs for a convincing attack is often on your own domain. A staff page gives names and titles. A sitemap can name a staging server. An exposed .git folder can leak source code and passwords. Unlike most recon, this activity hits your servers, which means your logs can see it and your WAF can stop it.

Where it fits in the attack chain

It lives in Reconnaissance (TA0043). Its findings feed Phishing for Information (T1598), Gather Victim Identity Information (T1589), Gather Victim Org Information (T1591), Phishing (T1566), and Trusted Relationship (T1199). If hidden folders expose vulnerable software, it feeds Exploit Public-Facing Application (T1190).

Written version of the simulation (same 8 steps)

  1. Discovery. The attacker finds the victim with a search engine and reads cached and archived copies of the site. Defender view: nothing yet; this happens on external platforms.
  2. Site map. The attacker fetches robots.txt and sitemap.xml. A sitemap entry names staging.corp-example.com, a forgotten test site. Defender view: one line in the access log; easy to miss.
  3. Crawl. The attacker walks the main pages with a normal browser, then again with a script. Defender view: access logs show sequential page requests from one address.
  4. Staff harvest. The attacker studies the leadership and team pages and collects names, titles, and email patterns. Defender view: focused, repeated access to /about and /team paths from the same source.
  5. Hidden probing. A wordlist scanner fires thousands of path guesses: /admin, /backup, /.git/config. Most answer 404, the "not found" code. Defender view: a 404 storm; the WAF starts throttling.
  6. Hit. The scanner finds /.git/config returning 200, the "found" code. The attacker downloads the repository and reads configuration with hostnames and credentials. Defender view: a few 200s on sensitive paths, the most important lines of the day.
  7. SOC response. The SOC sees the 404 spike, correlates it with the earlier staff-page crawl, and blocks the source range at the WAF. Defender view: recon confirmed and cut short.
  8. Attacker adapts. The attacker switches to slow crawling from a new address and leans on archive sites for old content. The SOC fixes the exposure, removes the .git folder, and adds rate rules. Defender view: risk reduced; monitoring stays on.
3

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains how attackers approach a victim website and why they bother. All commands here are for lab systems you own or are authorized to test.

When and why attackers use it

The victim's own website is the most reliable source about the victim. It is always up to date on structure and people, it never sends a legal warning for polite browsing, and it quietly exposes technical leftovers. Phishing teams use staff pages for target lists; exploit teams use sitemaps and hidden folders for entry points.

Where it applies

Every organization with a web presence. Larger sites give more surface: subdomains, regional pages, document portals, and legacy apps. Reported users of this style include groups tracked as EXOTIC LILY, Silent Librarian, Kimsuky, and Volt Typhoon, each pairing website recon with tailored social engineering.

What an attacker needs (prerequisites)

  • A reachable victim website, ideally with subdomains and document links.
  • Free tools: a browser, a crawler, a wordlist scanner, archive sites.
  • Optional: code search platforms to find leaked employee code.
  • Patience; polite, slow recon is harder to detect than loud scans.

How it is performed (conceptual steps)

  1. Search engines and archives: collect current and historical pages with site: operators and archive snapshots.
  2. Read robots.txt and sitemap.xml; both are meant to guide crawlers and both reveal structure.
  3. Crawl and mirror the site; harvest staff, partners, projects, and technologies.
  4. Extract document metadata: author names, internal paths, software versions.
  5. Probe for hidden paths and files with wordlists; note every non-404 answer.
  6. Pull any exposed development artifacts such as .git, backups, or admin panels.
  7. Feed everything into target lists, pretexts, and exploitation plans.
lab-only-commands.sh — inspect your OWN site the way an attacker would (lab only)
# Run these against a site you own or a lab host only.

# 1. What does your robots.txt advertise?
curl -s https://lab.example/robots.txt

# 2. What does your sitemap reveal about structure and hosts?
curl -s https://lab.example/sitemap.xml | head -40

# 3. Which old pages do archives still serve?
#    (manual step) visit https://web.archive.org and search lab.example

# 4. Gentle hidden-path check on your LAB host, never on someone else's:
ffuf -u https://lab.example/FUZZ -w /usr/share/wordlists/small.txt \\
     -mc 200,301,302,403 -rate 20 -t 2
⚠
Danger — ethics and law

Wordlist scanning, mirroring, and probing against sites you do not own is unauthorized access testing in many jurisdictions. Practice only on lab hosts or assets with written authorization. Downloading an exposed .git folder from a real site is not a benign act.

Common variations

  • Archive mining: old snapshots reveal deleted staff, old subdomains, and past breaches of hygiene.
  • Code search: public code platforms leak employee repos that name internal systems.
  • Document metadata: PDF and Office properties expose authors, paths, and versions.
  • Slow crawling: one page per minute, different source each day; built to stay under rate rules.

Mistakes attackers make

  • Running wordlist scans at full speed and lighting up every WAF rule.
  • Using default scanner user agents that log like a flare.
  • Probing honeypot paths planted by defenders.
  • Downloading exposed source from their main IP, tying recon to later access.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

This is your best-visible recon technique. Web servers see every request. The work is keeping the logs, parsing them well, and reading the patterns.

How defenders can see it

Each recon style has a shape in the logs: a crawl is sequential requests from one address; harvesting is focused access to people pages; probing is a storm of 404s; a find is a rare 200 on a sensitive path. Learn the four shapes and you can name the technique on sight. MITRE's official guidance (detection strategy DET0810) says the same in other words: watch for rapid successions of requests, large quantities from a single source, and metadata such as referrer and user-agent fields.

Why it matters to you

Recon sets the clock for everything after it. A 404 storm this week is a phishing wave or an exploit attempt next week. Also, probing finds real problems: exposed .git folders, backup files, and forgotten admin panels are your bugs being inventoried by strangers.

ArtifactWhere you find itWhat it means

Where to look (log inventory)

  • Web server access logs: the primary evidence; keep at least 90 days searchable.
  • CDN and load balancer logs: see traffic that never reaches origin servers.
  • WAF logs: rule hits, bot scores, rate-limit events, blocked clients.
  • DNS and certificate logs: catch recon of subdomains and new lookalike names.
  • Honeypot paths: fake /admin or fake backup files that only probing would touch.

Short triage flow

  1. Confirm the shape: crawl, harvest, probe, or find? Each has a different urgency.
  2. Check for any 200 answer on a sensitive path. If found, treat as an incident now.
  3. Scope the source: one IP, one ASN, rotating addresses?
  4. Rate-limit or block at the WAF; keep logging, do not blackhole silently.
  5. Verify the public surface: remove exposed files, fix the sitemap, review staff pages.
  6. Correlate with later phishing or exploit attempts and record the link.

5.4

Logs and Patterns

For each threat: sources, key fields, normal versus suspicious, and the detection idea. The core source for all of them is the web access log; WAF and CDN logs add depth.

Threat 1 — Exposed sensitive files

Sources: web logs, WAF logs. Fields: src_ip, uri_path, status, bytes, time.

Normal: no requests to sensitive paths at all. Suspicious: any request to /.git, /.env, /backup, or admin paths, especially with a 200 or 403 answer.

Detection logic: path-based rule; a 200 on a sensitive path is an incident, not an alert.

Threat 2 — Bulk scraping

Sources: web/CDN logs. Fields: src_ip, uri_path, status, user_agent, bytes.

Normal: humans browse a handful of pages per visit. Suspicious: hundreds of sequential pages from one source at machine speed.

Detection logic: request count per source in a time window, with a path-diversity check.

Threat 3 — Employee harvesting

Sources: web logs. Fields: src_ip, uri_path, referrer, time.

Normal: candidates and clients view a few profile pages. Suspicious: one source sweeping every page under /about, /team, /leadership.

Detection logic: path-group count rule per source; compare to a 30-day baseline.

Threat 4 — Search and archive mining

Sources: web logs (referrers), archive services directly.

Fields: referrer, uri_path, user_agent.

Normal: occasional referrers from search engines on public pages. Suspicious: archive or search referrers landing on deep or retired pages.

Detection logic: referrer rules plus a monthly review of what archives expose.

Threat 5 — Sitemap and robots enumeration

Sources: web logs. Fields: src_ip, uri_path, user_agent, time.

Normal: search engine crawlers fetch these files on schedule. Suspicious: unknown agents fetching both files, then following Disallow paths.

Detection logic: fetch of both files by a non-search-engine agent, followed by Disallow-path hits.

Threat 6 — Technology fingerprinting

Sources: web logs, WAF logs. Fields: method, uri_path, user_agent, server header.

Normal: HEAD requests are rare and human-driven. Suspicious: HEAD/OPTIONS bursts across many paths from one source.

Detection logic: method-based counting rule; also remove version banners.

access.log — technology fingerprinting with HEAD bursts (Threat 6)
198.51.100.120 - - [14/Oct/2026:03:41:02 +0000] "HEAD / HTTP/1.1" 200 0 "-" "Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; probe/1.1)" rt=0.01

How to read it: HEAD and OPTIONS requests return no page body, which is why the byte count is 0; they only ask the server to describe itself. Bursts of them fingerprint your stack. On their own they are minor; combined with probing they show a plan.

How to read it: the referrer proves the visitor came from an archived copy of a page you deleted years ago. The old staff list is still shaping someone's target list even though the page is gone from your navigation.

Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

Rule pair 1 — Sensitive path probing and exposure (Threat 1)

sigma/web_2026_01_sensitive_paths.yml
title: Sensitive Path Probing and Exposure on Public Website
id: 71a2b3c4-5d6e-4f70-8a91-2b3c4d5e6f71
status: stable
description: |
  Detects requests to version-control, configuration, backup, and admin
  paths on a public website. A successful (200) answer means the file is
  exposed and must be treated as a security incident.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1594/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1595/003/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-20
logsource:
  category: webserver
  product: nginx
detection:
  selection_paths:
    cs_uri_stem|contains:
      - /.git
      - /.env
      - /.svn
      - /backup
      - /.aws
      - /.ssh
      - /phpinfo
      - /admin.php
  condition: selection_paths
fields:
  - src_ip
  - cs_uri_stem
  - sc_status
  - http_user_agent
falsepositives:
  - Vulnerability scanners run by your own security team
  - A documented admin path that matches a keyword, such as /backup-status
level: critical
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1594
  - attack.t1595.003
splunk — matching SPL
index=web sourcetype=access_combined
    (uri_path IN ("*/.git*","*/.env*","*/.svn*","*backup*","*/.aws*","*/.ssh*","*phpinfo*","*admin.php*"))
| eval exposure=if(status=200 OR status=403, "confirm-exposure", "probe")
| stats count AS probes values(exposure) AS outcomes values(uri_path) AS paths
        max(_time) AS last_seen BY src_ip
| eval exposed=if(isnotnull(mvfind(outcomes, "confirm-exposure")), "yes", "no")
| sort - exposed, - probes

Field assumptions: standard combined access feed with uri_path, status, and src_ip parsed. The exposure field is computed in the query, not in the log.

False positives and tuning: add your scanner CIDRs to an allowlist lookup. Any confirm-exposure row with status 200 should open an incident automatically; probing rows alone can stay at High.

Rule pair 2 — Single-source crawl spike (Threat 2)

sigma/web_2026_02_crawl_spike.yml
title: Single Source Crawl Spike on Public Website
id: 82b3c4d5-6e7f-4a81-9b02-3c4d5e6f7a82
status: stable
description: |
  Detects one external source requesting an unusually high number of
  unique pages in one hour, the shape of automated site mirroring used
  to harvest structure and staff information at scale.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1594/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-20
logsource:
  category: webserver
  product: apache
detection:
  selection:
    sc_status: 200
  filter_assets:
    cs_uri_stem|endswith:
      - .css
      - .js
      - .png
      - .jpg
      - .woff2
  filter_engine:
    http_user_agent|contains:
      - Googlebot
      - bingbot
  timeframe: 1h
  condition: selection and not filter_assets and not filter_engine | count(distinct cs_uri_stem) by src_ip > 300
fields:
  - src_ip
  - http_user_agent
  - cs_uri_stem
falsepositives:
  - Approved partner integrations and monitoring
  - Shared NAT addresses with many real users
level: high
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1594
splunk — matching SPL
index=web sourcetype=access_combined status=200
    NOT (uri_path IN ("*.css","*.js","*.png","*.jpg","*.woff2"))
    NOT (user_agent IN ("*Googlebot*","*bingbot*"))
| bin _time span=1h
| stats dc(uri_path) AS unique_pages count AS requests BY _time src_ip user_agent
| where unique_pages > 300
| sort - unique_pages

Field assumptions: combined access feed; dc() counts unique paths, which separates a real crawler from one user refreshing the same page.

False positives and tuning: large offices and universities share public IPs; if your CDN passes client IP headers, group by that instead. Baseline for two weeks, then set the threshold near the 99.5th percentile.

| sort - probes

Field assumptions: combined access feed with the HTTP method parsed as method.

False positives and tuning: allowlist known monitors and API gateways; if uptime monitors stay noisy, add a dc(uri_path) > 10 check on top. Route this rule to a daily report rather than paging; escalate when it overlaps with rule pair 1 or 2 from the same source.

Rule pair 4 — Staff page harvesting pattern (Threat 3)

sigma/web_2026_04_staff_harvest.yml
title: Staff Page Harvesting Pattern
id: a4d5e6f7-8a9b-4ca3-9d24-5e6f7a8b9ca4
status: stable
description: |

  - cs_uri_stem

Rule pair 6 — Robots and sitemap enumeration (Threat 5)

sigma/web_2026_06_robots_sitemap_enum.yml
title: Robots and Sitemap Enumeration or Private Path Probing
id: c6f7a8b9-0abd-4ec5-9f46-7a8b9cadbec6
status: stable
description: |
  Detects non-search-engine clients fetching robots.txt or sitemap.xml,
  or requesting paths these files usually hide, such as internal, admin,
  private, or staging. When both happen for one source in a short
  window, the files were used as a map; the SPL below adds that
  correlated check.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1594/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-20
logsource:
  category: webserver
  product: apache
detection:
  selection_files:
    cs_uri_stem:
      - /robots.txt
      - /sitemap.xml
  filter_engine:
    http_user_agent|contains:
      - Googlebot
      - bingbot
      - DuckDuckBot
      - YandexBot
  selection_followup:
    cs_uri_stem|contains:
      - /internal
      - /admin
      - /private
      - /staging
  condition: (selection_files or selection_followup) and not filter_engine
fields:
  - src_ip
  - cs_uri_stem
  - sc_status
  - http_user_agent
falsepositives:
  - Marketing analytics tools that legitimately read sitemaps
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1594
splunk — matching SPL (component events)
index=web sourcetype=access_combined
    (uri_path="/robots.txt" OR uri_path="/sitemap.xml" OR uri_path IN ("*internal*","*admin*","*private*","*staging*"))
    NOT (user_agent IN ("*Googlebot*","*bingbot*","*DuckDuckBot*","*YandexBot*"))
| stats values(uri_path) AS paths count AS requests max(_time) AS last_seen BY src_ip
| sort - requests
splunk — optional stronger SPL (correlated session)
index=web sourcetype=access_combined
    (uri_path="/robots.txt" OR uri_path="/sitemap.xml" OR uri_path IN ("*internal*","*admin*","*private*","*staging*"))
    NOT (user_agent IN ("*Googlebot*","*bingbot*","*DuckDuckBot*","*YandexBot*"))
| transaction src_ip maxspan=30m
| where match(_raw, "(robots\\.txt|sitemap\\.xml)") AND match(_raw, "(?i)(internal|admin|private|staging)")
| table _time src_ip duration eventcount uri_path status user_agent

Field assumptions: combined access feed. The first SPL mirrors the Sigma rule on single events. The second, heavier query ties the map files and the follow-up hits into one session per source; replace transaction with stats by src_ip if your volume makes transaction too slow.

False positives and tuning: SEO tools read sitemaps all day; keep the search-engine filter current. The strongest signal is the correlated version: files first, private paths after, same source, short window.

Real-World Case Study

A composite case follows one actor from robots.txt to credential phishing, and shows exactly where defenders could have caught it a week earlier.

✎
About this case study

This is a composite case modeled on documented behavior, including EXOTIC LILY-style website harvesting followed by tailored phishing. Names and numbers are invented; the pattern is real.

Context

Organization: "Halcyon Insurance", a 2,000-staff insurer (fictional) with a rich public website: leadership bios, an annual report, a careers section, and a staging site that a sitemap entry still references. Threat actor: a financially motivated group that harvests victim websites, then runs executive impersonation and credential phishing.

Timeline

  • Week 1, Day 2 — Structure files. The attacker fetches robots.txt and sitemap.xml. The sitemap lists staging.halcyon-example.com, a forgotten test site.
  • Week 1, Day 3–4 — Crawl and harvest. A slow crawl copies 1,300 pages. The leadership and team pages yield 47 names, titles, and the first.last@ email pattern.
  • Week 1, Day 5 — Probing. A wordlist scan sends 6,100 path guesses at the main site and staging host in 38 minutes. Most return 404. The staging host answers 200 on /.git/config.
  • Week 1, Day 5 — Source leak. The attacker downloads the exposed repository. Inside: configuration with the VPN portal hostname, internal naming conventions, and a test service account.
  • Week 2, Day 9 — Pretext built. Using real names and the real VPN portal, the attacker drafts an "IT maintenance" email that asks staff to re-verify credentials on a lookalike portal.
  • Week 2, Day 10 — Phishing wave. The email goes to 47 harvested names. Nine visit the lookalike; four submit credentials; one is an IT administrator.
  • Week 2, Day 11 — Detection. The SOC correlates two earlier signals: the 404 storm on the staging host and an impossible-travel sign-in (a sign-in from places too far apart to be one person) with the admin account. The intrusion is caught before lateral movement.
  • Week 2–3, Day 11–17 — Response. Sessions are revoked, credentials rotated, the repository removed, and the WAF given rate rules. Public staff pages are trimmed and the sitemap is fixed.

Attack chain

  1. Map the victim site with structure files and archives (T1594).
  2. Crawl and harvest staff information for target lists.
  3. Probe for hidden paths; find the exposed .git repository.
  4. Use leaked configuration to select a credible pretext.
  5. Run credential phishing against harvested staff (T1566 pattern) and attempt access.

Detection point

The recon was visible twice in week 1: the sequential crawl of people pages, and the 38-minute 404 storm that ended in a 200 on /.git/config. Neither had a rule at the time. The first rule that mattered was the sign-in anomaly on day 11, which worked but came late. The section 5.5 rules exist so the next case is caught in week 1, not week 2.

Containment

Revoked all sessions for the affected accounts, blocked the recon and phishing infrastructure at the proxy, and took the staging host offline from the public internet.

Eradication

Removed the .git directory and any other exposed files, rotated the test service account and all phished credentials, and purged the lookalike domain via the registrar's abuse process.

Recovery

Deployed the six web rules from section 5.5, added subdomain inventory checks to asset management, trimmed public staff pages, and retrained the web team on sitemap hygiene.

Impact and consequences

No financial loss occurred. Real costs: roughly 140 staff-hours, an external forensic review of the staging host, and a public-relations debate about how much staff detail belongs on the website. The insurer now treats its public web estate as part of its security perimeter.

Team response and communication

The SOC briefed the executive team on day 11 with a one-page summary that showed the recon timeline side by side with the phishing wave. Web, IT, and security held a joint cleanup week. Staff received a short advisory that used the real (anonymized) email as an example, which raised report-button usage by half the following month.

Lessons learned

  • Structure files are maps; audit robots.txt and sitemaps like public documents.
  • Staging hosts eventually get found; never expose them without auth and WAF rules.
  • A 200 on a sensitive path is an incident, not a log line.
  • Staff pages feed phishing; publish fewer names and use role-based contacts.
  • Recon-to-phish correlation turns two small alerts into one clear case.
  • Web log analytics are cheap, high-value detection; fund them early.

Mitigation and Defense

Public-surface hardening, edge protection, and log analytics: controls that shrink what recon finds and make the rest visible.

MITRE lists M1056 Pre-compromise as the mitigation. In practice that means shrinking what your public site teaches and watching who studies it. The controls below do both.

Public surface management

  • Trim staff pages: fewer names, role-based contact forms.
  • Fix robots.txt and sitemaps; never list internal hosts.
  • Inventory subdomains monthly; retire stale ones.

Web platform hardening

  • Block access to .git, .env, backups, and admin paths by default.
  • Remove version banners and verbose error pages.
  • Scrub document metadata before publishing PDFs and Office files.

Edge protection

  • WAF in blocking mode with rate limits per source.
  • Bot scoring for unknown user agents.
  • Honeypot paths that only scanners touch.

Logging and monitoring

  • Keep 90+ days of web, CDN, and WAF logs, searchable.
  • Deploy the six rules in section 5.5.
  • Dashboard for 404 rate, unique-page crawls, and sensitive-path hits.

Data and identity

  • Never publish credentials in any repository or config.
  • Phishing-resistant MFA, so harvested passwords alone fail.
  • Leak-monitoring for your domains on code platforms.

Response

  • Playbook: recon spike, sensitive-path hit, harvest pattern.
  • Correlate recon with later phishing and sign-in anomalies.
  • Practice the "200 on /.git" drill twice a year.

Home and small office

  • Check your own site for /wp-admin, /.git, /backup, and /phpinfo; if any answers 200, remove it today.
  • Publish a short team page; use a form or a generic address like office@example.com instead of personal emails.
  • Search your domain on archive sites once a year and remove anything you would not post today.
  • Keep your CMS and plugins updated; scanners find old versions fast.
