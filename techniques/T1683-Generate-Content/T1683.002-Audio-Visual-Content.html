Overview

What this sub-technique covers, why it changed the risk math for fraud and identity, and the facts to carry into the rest of the guide.

What is this sub-technique?

Adversaries create or manipulate audio, image, and video content to support targeting and malicious operations. That includes synthetic voice recordings, real-time altered audio or video during live interactions, fabricated profile photos and identity documents, and video showing people who never existed or impersonating people who do.

The media can be produced by hand with editing tools, generated with AI-assisted services, or bought from third-party synthetic media providers. Modern AI tooling made all of it cheap, fast, and hard to tell apart from real recordings. MITRE links the output to Phishing, Spearphishing via Service, Phishing for Information, Internal Spearphishing, Social Engineering, Financial Theft, and Establish Accounts.

Why it matters

Most security controls inspect text and code. A voice clone walks past all of them: it attacks a phone call, a video meeting, or a photo review. The losses are real and public. In 2024, a Hong Kong finance employee wired about US$25 million after a video call with a deepfaked "CFO". That September, the US Treasury's FinCEN issued a formal alert about deepfake fraud schemes against financial institutions.

For defenders, the lesson is simple: identity claims made through audio and video now need the same scrutiny as links and attachments.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Synthetic media is a Resource Development product. It is manufactured first, then used where human trust guards a process:

Note · What "PRE" means

PRE marks activity before any compromise of your systems. Cloning a voice needs no access to your network. ATT&CK's official detection strategy for this sub-technique (DET0918, analytic AN2061) says: "Much of this takes place outside the visibility of the target organization, making detection difficult for defenders. Detection efforts may be focused on related stages of the adversary lifecycle, such as during Initial Access." Every threat and detection in section 5 lives at that seam: the moment synthetic media is used against your people and processes. Two words used a lot below: telemetry means the log data your systems record, and a detection is a rule that searches that data.

Defender hint

The same story in words

Step 1

Collect public media

The attacker downloads public speeches, interviews, and webinars featuring your executives. Earnings calls are ideal: high quality, long, and free. Defender hint: executive media exposure is attack surface; catalog it.

Step 2

Train the clone

Voice models learn the executive's voice in hours. Face models learn mannerisms from video. Nothing touches your network. Defender hint: this is invisible to you; prepare the process controls instead.

Step 3

Prime the target

A phishing email tells the finance employee a confidential deal is underway and must not be discussed. Isolation prepares the victim to skip normal checks. Defender hint: "keep this secret" themes are a red flag worth a gateway rule.

Step 4

Send the meeting invite

An invite arrives from a look-alike domain for an urgent video meeting with senior staff. Defender hint: organizer domain checks on calendar invites are cheap and effective.

Step 5

Run the fake meeting

The "CFO" and several familiar colleagues appear on camera. They are all deepfakes. The voice sounds right; the faces nod at the right moments. Defender hint: compare participants against the real calendar; missing calendar entries are a strong tell.

Step 6

The wire request

Instructions arrive: urgent transfers for a secret acquisition, and do not confirm with headquarters. The request breaks dual-approval policy. Defender hint: policy exceptions during pressure are exactly what fraud monitoring should catch.

Step 7

Money moves

Fifteen transfers go to five fresh accounts. Within an hour the funds are spread across mule networks. Defender hint: many-to-few transfer patterns with brand-new beneficiaries are a classic fraud signal.

Step 8

Discovery and response

A callback to the real CFO reveals the fraud. Banks are called for recalls, police and the ISAC are notified, and verification controls are rebuilt. Defender hint: minutes matter; recall success drops fast.

Red Team Perspective

How attackers plan and run synthetic media operations, and where the process leaks signals. Educational and defensive only; the lab example is safe.

When and why attackers use it

Synthetic media is chosen when the target of the attack is a human decision guarded by a live interaction: a phone verification, a video interview, an approval call. Text filters, email security, and code signing do nothing here. A cloned voice is the cheapest known bypass for "I heard them, so it is them".

AI-assisted tooling changed the economics: voice cloning needs minutes of public audio, face models need public video, and real-time alteration now runs on a single laptop. That is why MITRE, the FBI, and Europol all flagged synthetic media fraud as a growing operational threat.

What the media is for

Each product has a job. A cloned voice earns an MFA reset or a payment approval. A live deepfake wins a video meeting. A fabricated photo and documents pass identity checks for a fake employee. A synthetic "colleague" in a meeting adds social proof. The media is never the goal; the money and access that follow it are.

How it is performed (generic flow)

  1. Collect references. Public speeches, videos, social clips, and photos of the people to impersonate.
  2. Train or buy. Clone voices and faces with AI tools, or commission third-party synthetic media services.
  3. Prime the target. Written lures and isolation tactics prepare the victim to skip normal verification.
  4. Deliver live. Run the altered audio or video in a call or meeting, or send pre-recorded audio as "evidence".
  5. Push the ask. Urgent transfer, credential reset, or approval, always with a reason not to verify.
  6. Extract and launder. Move value quickly through mule accounts before discovery.
Ethics notice

This page explains attacker behavior so defenders can detect it. Never create media that impersonates a real person. If you demo deepfake risk, use your own face and voice with consent, in a lab, under your organization's rules.

Where it applies

Executive video fraud

Fake CFOs and boards instructing payments in live meetings (the Arup pattern).

Help desk vishing

Cloned employee voices requesting MFA resets and credential changes.

Fake candidates

AI photos and altered video in interviews, plus fabricated identity documents.

Voice-note scams

Pre-recorded "CEO" voice messages delivered over chat apps to push urgent action.

Account verification fraud

Selfie-video bypasses of identity verification and account recovery flows.

Brand and market abuse

Fake executive statements that move opinions or prices; AI-generated "proof" in scams.

Lab-only example: understand the artifacts

Synthetic audio has measurable properties. These safe exercises use only your own recordings in an isolated VM.

bash · lab only
# LAB ONLY - your own voice, your own machine, no impersonation.

# 1) Record 30 seconds of your own voice as lab_own_voice.wav.

# 2) Compare spectra: natural speech has rich high-frequency texture.
sox lab_own_voice.wav -n spectrogram -o lab_spectrogram.png

# 3) Trim the recording with an editor, save as lab_trimmed.wav,
#    then compare spectrograms. Notice what editing removes.

# 4) Practice the analyst habit: record file hashes and timestamps
#    for any media you preserve as evidence.
sha256sum lab_own_voice.wav lab_trimmed.wav

Common variations and attacker mistakes

Variations you will meet

  • Pre-recorded voice notes: no live interaction, no lags; delivered over chat or voicemail.
  • Real-time face swap: only the face is altered; body and voice stay the attacker's.
  • Full deepfake meetings: several synthetic participants for social proof.
  • Synthetic documents: AI photos and forged ID papers for hiring and banking checks.

Mistakes attackers make

  • Live lags and lip-sync drift under questioning; flat refusal to turn sideways or speak on demand.
  • Meeting invites from look-alike domains, absent from real calendars.
  • Synthetic images with broken hands, teeth, glasses edges, or background text.
  • Metadata and model fingerprints left in generated files.
  • Fresh beneficiary accounts that fail payment-fraud baselines.

Section 5 turns each leak into a detection.

Blue Team Perspective

You will rarely detect the media itself. You can detect the behavior around it: who joined what meeting, who reset which MFA, and which payment broke which rule.

How defenders can see it

Meeting platform audit

Organizer domains, participant lists, device fingerprints, and recordings are all logged if you collect them.

Identity events

MFA method changes and re-registrations, especially right after calls or help desk tickets, are gold-tier signals.

Payment monitoring

Dual-approval exceptions, new beneficiaries, and transfer bursts tell you fraud happened, even when media was perfect.

Help desk telemetry

Ticket timelines, caller numbers, and reset patterns expose voice-clone vishing campaigns.

Endpoint and SaaS

Installation of voice and face tooling on corporate hosts, and traffic to synthetic media services.

People

Interviewers, treasurers, and help desk agents who report "something felt off" are your fastest and best sensor.

Why it matters for the chain

Synthetic media turns Resource Development into a direct attack on trust-based processes. The technique prepares weapons that bypass your technical stack entirely, so your defenses must be behavioral and procedural: verification rituals, telemetry correlation, and fast fraud response. Every deepfake case stopped early saves both money and the reputation of the people whose faces were used.

Consumers of this content: T1656 Impersonation, T1657 Financial Theft, T1566 Phishing, and T1598 Phishing for Information.

Triage flow

  1. Intake. Report of a suspicious call, meeting, or media file; open a case with precise timestamps.
  2. Freeze the transaction. If money or credentials are in motion, stop the process first; analyze later.
  3. Verify out of band. Call the real person on a known number; use a challenge phrase agreed in advance.
  4. Collect meeting evidence. Export participant lists, organizer domains, device details, and recordings.
  5. Check identity events. Look for MFA changes and new devices within hours of the interaction.
  6. Scope. Query for the same organizer domain, caller number, or media fingerprint across all meetings and tickets.
  7. Contain. Block the infrastructure, revoke changes made during the window, and force re-registration through a verified path.
  8. Report and share. Notify finance, the bank, police, and your ISAC; preserve media for forensics.
Warning · Treat it as fraud first

In confirmed deepfake fraud, the first hour belongs to the bank recall and account freeze, not to forensic questions. Evidence keeps; recalled money often does not.

ATT&CK procedure examples for T1683.002

ATT&CK records five procedure examples on this sub-technique's page. APT-C-36 (G0099) used phishing pages that looked like legitimate banking login portals. APT38 (G0082) created fictitious social media profiles with fake photos and personas. Contagious Interview (G1052) used AI to clone video-conferencing applications that distributed their BeaverTail malware, and also generated deepfake videos. NIMBUS MANTICORE (G1059) built fictitious job-themed websites, used decoy documents for legitimacy, and showed fake Microsoft Outlook login prompts to steal credentials. The Gentlemen (G1058) placed a branded graphic on the desktop wallpaper of systems they compromised.

Confidence: High

Confirmed losses, enforcement actions, or multi-source reporting. Build detections and change process.

Confidence: Moderate

Credible single-source reporting. Hunt and tune; confirm internally before blocking.

Confidence: Low

Unverified claims. Track in the register; never automate on it.

Good practice

Keep an "executive media exposure" register: where your leaders' voices and faces appear publicly, with dates and links. It drives both takedown decisions and risk conversations about what an attacker could clone today.

Critical · TH-01 Deepfake video-call fraud

What happens: attackers run a live meeting with deepfaked executives who instruct urgent, confidential transfers, bypassing normal verification.

Preconditions: public executive media, voice and face tooling, a target with payment authority, and weak out-of-band verification.

Impact: multi-million losses in single incidents; the Hong Kong case lost about US$25 million.

Justification: bypasses every text-based control and exploits live trust; confirmed large losses worldwide.

Critical · TH-02 Voice-clone vishing

What happens: a cloned employee or executive voice calls the help desk for an MFA reset or password change, or pressures a user directly.

Preconditions: minutes of public voice audio, a help desk without strong verification, and MFA re-registration telemetry nobody watches.

Impact: silent account takeover; fraud and lateral movement follow.

Justification: cheap, repeatable, and it defeats the "I heard them" heuristic used in most help desks.

High · TH-03 Synthetic candidates

What happens: fake applicants use AI photos, altered interview video, and forged documents to get hired, then use the seat for access or malware.

Preconditions: remote hiring, weak identity proofing, no interview media review.

Impact: insider access, pre-loaded malware, data theft.

Justification: documented across DPRK-linked operations; converts directly into a persistent seat inside the company.

Medium · TH-04 Pre-recorded voice lures

What happens: cloned-voice messages ("CEO" voice note, family emergency calls) push urgent payments or codes without any live interaction.

Preconditions: cloned voice, a delivery channel (voicemail, chat), and targets who trust voice notes.

Impact: payment and credential fraud; smaller per-incident sums but high volume.

Justification: no live artifacts, harder to catch in real time; ranked medium because limits and callbacks usually cap losses.

Medium · TH-05 Synthetic media tooling on corporate hosts

What happens: voice or face tooling is installed on corporate endpoints, or corporate accounts feed synthetic media services, either by insiders preparing fraud or by compromised hosts.

Preconditions: no software allowlist, no proxy/CASB monitoring of AI media services.

Impact: preparation for TH-01/TH-02 from inside; possible data exposure through service uploads.

Justification: preparatory behavior, but fully detectable from your own telemetry, so value is high as an early sensor.

Real-World Case Study

The Hong Kong deepfake video-call fraud reported in February 2024: roughly US$25 million moved after a finance employee met a fully deepfaked "CFO" and colleagues on a video call. Every control lesson in this guide is visible in this case.

Context

A finance employee at the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup received an email claiming to be from the chief financial officer in the United Kingdom, describing a confidential transaction. Days later, the employee joined a video call with what looked and sounded like the CFO and several familiar colleagues. Everyone on the call was a deepfake. Following the meeting, the employee executed a series of transfers. The company confirmed the incident in May 2024; reporting by the South China Morning Post had described the case in February.

Reported details: fifteen transfers to five local bank accounts, totaling about HK$200 million (roughly US$25.6 million). The call was convincing enough that experience, hierarchy, and "everyone saw it" all failed at once.

Timeline

Late January 2024

The priming email

An email arrives from the "UK CFO" about a confidential deal requiring secrecy and fast action.

Days later

The video call

The employee joins a live meeting with deepfaked executives who repeat the instructions and add social proof.

Same period

Fifteen transfers

Trusting the call, the employee wires funds to five local accounts in a series of transfers.

After the transfers

Discovery

A cross-check with headquarters reveals no such transaction exists. The employee contacts Hong Kong police.

4 February 2024

Public reporting

SCMP describes the case; police confirm details. Deepfake fraud becomes front-page news worldwide.

May 2024

Company confirmation

Arup confirms it was the victim and that the deepfake call was part of the scheme.

September 2024

Regulatory response

FinCEN issues a formal alert on deepfake fraud schemes targeting financial institutions, citing patterns like this one.

Attack chain

  1. Collect public voice and video of real executives.
  2. Train voice and face models for the impersonated cast.
  3. Prime the target with a confidential-sounding email.
  4. Deliver the live deepfake meeting from attacker-controlled infrastructure.
  5. Push urgent, secretive transfer instructions.
  6. Move funds through mule accounts before verification catches up.

Detection point

There was no technical detection: the fraud was discovered by a human cross-check with headquarters after the money moved. This is the case's hardest lesson. The available signals were real: meeting organized from unfamiliar infrastructure, a calendar that never contained the meeting, dual-approval policy broken under pressure, and a burst of transfers to fresh accounts. Any of them, monitored, gives the response a chance before settlement.

Containment, eradication, recovery

Containment meant immediate calls to the receiving banks for recalls, freezing what could still be frozen, and reporting to Hong Kong police. Eradication focused on the process gap: verification rituals were rebuilt around out-of-band callbacks and dual approval that no call can override. Recovery of the full amount is rarely possible once funds reach mule networks; the durable recovery was procedural, not financial.

Impact and consequences

Direct impact: roughly US$25.6 million lost across fifteen transfers. Wider impact: the case rewrote enterprise risk assumptions about video identity, triggered global training programs, and pushed regulators to act. For the employee, the human cost was severe; the attackers' design made the employee the last control point, deliberately.

Team response and communication

The response required finance, security, legal, and communications to move together: bank recalls, police reports, and a measured public posture while facts were confirmed. The company's eventual confirmation was honest and limited, which is the right pattern: acknowledge, correct the record, and describe what changed, without inventing certainty about attacker details.

Lessons learned

  • Verify payments out of band, always; a video call is not verification.
  • Monitor meeting infrastructure: organizer domains and calendar mismatches.
  • Watch payment telemetry: dual-approval exceptions and fresh beneficiaries.
  • Train for pressure and secrecy cues, not just for suspicious links.
  • Drill the first hour: who calls which bank, in what order, with what authority.

Mitigation and Defense

MITRE assigns M1056 Pre-compromise: the attacker's render farm is out of reach. Defense means making synthetic media worthless against your processes.

Enterprise controls

Verification rituals (the core control)

Out-of-band verification for every payment, credential reset, and access approval: call back on a known number, use an agreed challenge phrase, and give people explicit permission to hang up and verify. No video call, however convincing, counts as verification. This single control defeats most deepfake fraud.

Payment process hardening

Dual approval with monetary limits no one can waive verbally. Beneficiary cooling-off periods and alerts for new accounts. Fraud monitoring for many-to-few transfer bursts. Treat "policy exception under time pressure" as an alarm, not a favor.

Identity and help desk

Written help desk verification scripts with identity proofing for resets, cooling-off periods, and manager confirmation for high-risk accounts. Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 keys, passkeys) so that even a successful voice-clone reset yields nothing usable. Monitor MFA re-registrations (detection TH-02).

Meeting and UC hygiene

Log meeting platform audit events into the SIEM. Verify organizer domains on sensitive meetings; keep a known-partner list. Brief executives on challenge phrases and on the right to refuse on-camera requests. Keep executive public media exposure low and cataloged.

Endpoint and SaaS controls

Application allowlisting to block synthetic media tooling on non-media hosts (detection TH-05). Proxy and CASB monitoring of AI media services with DLP on uploads. EDR everywhere, including onboarding devices for remote hires (detection TH-03 support).

Logging, monitoring, and fraud response

Correlate meetings, identity events, and payments on one timeline. Build the fraud runbook: who calls which bank in the first hour, in what order. Pre-agree police and ISAC notification paths. Preserve meeting recordings and media as evidence with hashes.

People and training

Run live deepfake-awareness demos using the CEO's own (consented) voice and face; the shock works. Teach the tells: refusal to turn sideways, lags, lighting drift, and pressure plus secrecy combined. Reward the slow-down: an employee who verifies and offends no one is the goal.

Governance and provenance

Adopt media provenance practice (content credentials — signed labels that record where a media file came from) for what you publish, aligned with EU AI Act Article 50 disclosure duties. Add synthetic media scenarios to the risk register, the audit plan, and the annual tabletop with finance and the board.

Home and small-office advice

Cloned-voice scams target families and small businesses hardest because they have no dual-approval policy. Make one: agree that no urgent money request is honored until you call the person back on the number you already have. Set a family code word for emergencies. Teach everyone that voices can be copied from a single social media clip, so keep your audio and video footprint small. Enable bank transfer alerts, add transfer delays where your bank allows, and treat any request for gift cards or crypto as a scam.
