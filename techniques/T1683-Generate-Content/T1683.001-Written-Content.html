T1683.001 Written Content

Attackers write their way in. T1683.001 covers the written half of content generation: phishing lures, fake invoices, fabricated job postings and employment credentials, decoy documents, and persona posts. AI writing tools now make all of it fluent, tailored, and fast. This guide shows defenders where that text touches your organization and how to detect it.

Overview

What this sub-technique covers, why it matters right now, and the facts you need before the deeper sections.

What is this sub-technique?

Adversaries create or tailor written materials to support targeting and malicious operations. The content includes phishing lures, fraudulent financial communications, fabricated job postings, and fabricated employment credentials. It also covers decoy documents, social media persona content, and the supporting narratives that keep fake personas alive over time.

The text may be written by hand, commissioned from third parties, or produced with AI-assisted tools. AI is also used to tailor content: translate it into the target's language, or mimic the communication style of a trusted sender. Written materials can impersonate government correspondence, diplomatic messages, or internal company documents. MITRE is explicit about the boundary: this sub-technique does not cover malicious code or scripts. That is Develop Capabilities (T1587).

Why it matters

For twenty years, the strongest phishing signal was bad writing. Language models ended that. A lure is now fluent, personalized, and free of the tells that training taught people to spot. ATT&CK already records real use: APT-C-36 writes fake official notifications, Contagious Interview builds fake recruiter personas, and the Kali365 tooling generates branded BEC lures and even AI-written replies.

For defenders this means the detection weight must move from "how the text looks" to "how the text arrives": sender history, domain age, thread behavior, and process controls around money and credentials.

Where it fits in the attack chain

Written content is a Resource Development product. It is manufactured first, then consumed by Initial Access techniques:

Note · What "PRE" means

PRE marks activity before the attacker compromises anything you own. Writing a lure costs you nothing to watch. ATT&CK's official detection strategy for this sub-technique (DET0917, analytic AN2060) says: "Much of this takes place outside the visibility of the target organization, making detection difficult for defenders. Detection efforts may be focused on related stages of the adversary lifecycle, such as during Initial Access." Every threat, log, and rule in section 5 sits at that seam: the moment attacker-written text meets your mail flow, portals, or staff. Two words used a lot below: telemetry means the log data your systems record, and a detection is a rule that searches that data.

Animated Simulation

Follow one AI-written spear phishing attempt from research to SOC response. Press Play, jump with the number chips, and hover the nodes to see what each system does.

Step 1 of 8

Defender hint

The same story in words

Step 1

Research the target

The attacker reads the employee's professional profile and the company's org chart. They learn names, projects, and the tone your executives use. Defender hint: oversharing feeds this step; trim public role details.

Step 2

Draft the lure with an AI model

The attacker prompts a writing service for an urgent, executive-style request. The draft comes back flawless, in perfect business language. Defender hint: this happens outside your network; expect no logs yet.

Step 3

Iterate on tone and brand

Several prompt rounds copy your brand voice: correct phrases, the right urgency, a clean signature block. Defender hint: after iteration, content-only filters have nothing left to catch.

Step 4

Set up delivery

The attacker registers a look-alike domain and a fresh mailbox. The TLS certificate appears in public logs within hours. Defender hint: Certificate Transparency is your earliest alarm; watch it.

Step 5

Send the email

The message enters your gateway from a first-time sender with no reputation. Headers show a display name that matches a real executive. Defender hint: sender history plus display-name checks catch this where content checks cannot.

Step 6

The lure lands

The gateway scores the text as clean because it is clean. The mail reaches the inbox. Defender hint: a perfect spam score on first contact is itself suspicious.

Step 7

Employee engages

The employee clicks the link and reaches a credential page from the new domain. Proxy logs record the visit from inside your network. Defender hint: newly registered domain visits are a top ten detection for a reason.

Step 8

SOC triage and response

The SOC hunts the sender across all mailboxes, blocks the domain, resets any exposed credentials, and shares the pattern. Defender hint: speed decides whether one click stays one click.

Red Team Perspective

How attackers plan and produce written lures, and where their process leaks signals. Educational only: everything here is defense-oriented, and the lab example is safe to run.

When and why attackers write

Written content is the cheapest weapon in the set. It needs no exploits, no infrastructure inside your network, and almost no budget. What it needs is believability, and AI writing tools now supply that at zero marginal cost. An operator can draft, translate, and brand-match a lure in minutes, then reuse the template against thousands of targets.

Attackers choose this technique when the entry point is a person: a recruiter, a finance clerk, a help desk agent. The writing is tuned to that person's role, language, and current pressures, which is exactly what AI tailoring is good at.

What the text is for

Each written product has a job. A lure earns a click or a reply. A fake invoice earns a payment. A fabricated resume earns an interview. A persona post earns trust over weeks. Decoy documents explain a suspicious attachment to a curious user. The text is never the goal; access and money are. That is why ATT&CK links this sub-technique to Phishing, Internal Spearphishing, Financial Theft, and Establish Accounts.

How it is performed (generic flow)

  1. Collect raw material. Public profiles, org charts, real email styles, current events, and your brand assets.
  2. Draft with a model. Generate the lure text: executive request, invoice, job offer, or HR notice, in the target's language.
  3. Tailor per target. Adjust names, projects, deadlines, and tone; mimic the real sender's style.
  4. Build the shell. Look-alike domain, mailbox or service delivery, and a landing page or attachment to carry the payload.
  5. Test the pass. Check the draft against common filters and a casual human read; iterate until it passes both.
  6. Send and manage replies. Deliver in small batches; use AI-generated replies to keep conversations alive with victims.
Ethics notice

This page explains attacker behavior so defenders can detect it. Do not write or send lures to real people. If you test, use your own isolated lab tenant, your own staff with consent, and follow your rules of engagement.

Where it applies

Spear phishing & BEC

Tailored executive and finance text; reply chains maintained with generated responses (Kali365, S9044).

Fake recruiting

Fake job postings and fake recruiter personas that carry malicious coding tests (Contagious Interview, G1052).

Employment fraud

Fabricated resumes, references, and credentials that pass screening and background checks.

Official impersonation

Government and corporate notice text that makes the payload look routine (APT-C-36, G0099; APT38 decoys, G0082).

Persona farming

Weeks of AI-written posts that build a believable account before the scam (fake LinkedIn and Telegram accounts).

Brand damage & scams

Fake support threads, branded ransom notes, and scam pages using stolen brand voice (TeamPCP, The Gentlemen).

Prerequisites

AI writing accessPublic model subscriptions, underground services, or stolen API keys. See T1588.007.

Delivery assetsLook-alike domains (T1583.001), mailboxes or accounts (T1585), and landing infrastructure.

Lab-only example: feel the metadata trail

Written content leaves file-level traces. These safe commands let you see them on your own test files in an isolated VM.

bash · lab only
# LAB ONLY - isolated training VM, your own test files, no real targets.

# 1) Make a test document two ways and compare metadata.
pandoc lab_normal.md -o lab_normal.pdf
exiftool lab_normal.pdf        # note Producer, Creator, dates

# 2) Export the same text through an AI or template tool you use
#    lawfully, then compare fields:
exiftool lab_from_template.pdf

# 3) Practice the analyst read: which fields differ, which repeat,
#    and what would you query in SIEM? (Section 5.4 shows the fields.)

# 4) Hash both files for your practice incident record.
sha256sum lab_normal.pdf lab_from_template.pdf

Common variations and attacker mistakes

Variations you will meet

  • Manual craft: hand-written lures for high-value targets, slow but precise.
  • AI-tailored templates: one base lure, personalized per victim by a model.
  • Commissioned text: native-language writers hired for a region or industry.
  • Reply automation: models write the follow-ups, keeping threads alive for days.

Mistakes attackers make

  • Template artifacts: identical phrasing and structure across many lures.
  • File metadata: AI tool or template names in document Producer fields.
  • Domain patterns: same registrar, TLD set, or certificate issuer across campaigns.
  • Persona reuse: the same bio text and photos across fake accounts.
  • Timing bursts: many personalized sends leaving one host within minutes.

Section 5 turns each leak into a detection.

Blue Team Perspective

You cannot watch the text being written. You can watch it arrive, and you can watch what it does next. Here is the full sensor map.

How defenders can see it

Mail gateway telemetry

Sender reputation age, first-contact flags, display-name versus header mismatches, and reply-to changes on existing threads.

Attachment metadata

Document Producer/Creator strings that name AI or template tooling; missing or contradictory timestamps.

Proxy and DNS

Visits to newly registered domains that host credential pages or fake portals.

Identity and mailbox audit

New inbox rules, mass internal forwards, and sign-ins that follow successful lures.

HR and recruiting audit

Uploads with generator metadata, fabricated references, and interview anomalies.

External monitoring

Brand job-scam postings, fake support accounts, and persona farms building on your name.

Why it matters for the chain

Written content is the first brick of most social-engineering chains. When you catch a lure, you are reading the attacker's Resource Development homework: their themes show what they researched, their domains show their infrastructure, and their timing shows their tooling. Feed every blocked lure back into detections and you degrade every campaign that follows.

Consumers of this content: T1566 Phishing, T1566.003 Spearphishing via Service, T1598 Phishing for Information, T1534 Internal Spearphishing, T1585 Establish Accounts, and T1657 Financial Theft.

Triage flow

  1. Intake. User report, gateway alert, or brand monitor; open a case with timestamps.
  2. Preserve. Full headers, raw message, attachments, and metadata before quarantine scrubs anything.
  3. Header analysis. From versus Reply-To, display-name versus authenticated domain, first-contact status.
  4. Infrastructure check. Domain age, certificate age, hosting reputation, prior campaign sightings.
  5. Verify out of band. Call the claimed sender on a known number if the lure claims internal origin.
  6. Scope and purge. Hunt the sender and theme across all mailboxes; purge and block.
  7. Check downstream. If anyone clicked or replied: credential reset, session revocation, and IdP review.
  8. Feed back. Update gateway rules, tune the Sigma rules in 5.5, and share with peers.
Warning · If credentials or money moved

Reset and revoke first, then investigate. For payments, call the bank immediately to attempt recall. Evidence keeps; recalled transfers may not wait.

Critical · TH-01 LLM-polished spear phishing

What happens: a flawless, targeted email arrives at first contact from a fresh domain or service, carrying a link or attachment and an urgent, plausible story.

Preconditions: target research, an AI writing service, delivery infrastructure, and no domain-age or first-contact logic in the gateway.

Impact: credentials, session tokens, or a foothold for ransomware.

Justification: highest-volume path; legacy content tells are gone, so infra signals decide outcomes.

Critical · TH-02 AI-assisted BEC

What happens: generated executive or vendor text requests payment changes; automated replies keep the thread alive until money moves.

Preconditions: payment processes verifiable only in-channel, or a compromised vendor or executive mailbox.

Impact: direct financial loss; BEC remains the costliest fraud class per incident.

Justification: tooling like Kali365 industrializes it; text-only filters fail against fluent threads.

High · TH-03 Fabricated credentials and fake jobs

What happens: fake resumes, references, and portfolios pass screening; or fake recruiter/job content (Contagious Interview style) delivers "work samples" that are malware.

Preconditions: remote hiring, weak identity proofing, references verified only via candidate-supplied contacts.

Impact: insider access, or developer endpoint compromise through malicious coding tests.

Justification: multiple documented DPRK-linked operations; converts directly into access or payloads.

High · TH-04 AI-drafted internal spearphishing

What happens: from a compromised mailbox, the attacker sends generated internal lures that inherit your trust boundary (T1534), often with new inbox rules to hide replies.

Preconditions: an already compromised account; no monitoring of internal mass-sends or rule creation.

Impact: rapid credential theft that spreads from account to account (lateral movement); payroll and MFA fraud.

Justification: internal mail is trusted by users and often under-filtered; AI removes the writing effort.

Medium · TH-05 AI-written persona farming

What happens: weeks of generated posts build believable fake recruiter, partner, or support personas on social platforms, later used for scams or delivery.

Preconditions: platform accounts, stolen or generated profile media, patience.

Impact: indirect; enables TH-01 and TH-03 with pre-warmed trust.

Justification: preparatory and external, but it is where high-quality targeting begins, so early detection still pays.

Real-World Case Study

In July 2024, the security awareness company KnowBe4 publicly documented that it had hired a North Korean operative using a fully fabricated written identity. It is the cleanest public example of T1683.001 doing its job, and of a control stack catching it.

Context

KnowBe4, a US-based security awareness firm, was hiring a remote IT worker. The applicant, using the first name "Kyle", presented a professional US identity, an AI-altered photo, a polished resume and portfolio, and references who answered their phones. All of the written material was fabricated: employment history, credentials, and supporting documents. This matches a wider DPRK "fake IT worker" economy that US authorities have repeatedly warned about, and the same family of activity MITRE records as Contagious Interview (G1052).

Four rounds of video interviews and a background check were passed. The company made the offer and shipped a company laptop to the new hire.

Timeline

Before June 2024

Identity manufacturing

The persona is built: stolen identity elements, an AI-altered photo, fabricated employment history, and a portfolio of work products.

June 2024

Application and interviews

The candidate applies for a remote IT role and passes four video interviews, using AI-assisted answers and a convincing performance.

June 2024

Background check passed

Forged documents and accomplice references satisfy the verification, showing that process-level trust was the weak control.

Week of 15 July 2024

Hire and laptop delivery

The offer is signed and a laptop is shipped. The device arrives already prepared for the operation.

Day 1, first hour

Malware loads

On first boot, a hidden loader runs. Endpoint detection and response software flags it within about 25 minutes.

Same day

Containment

The device is isolated, the account is disabled, and the investigation begins with external forensics support.

25 July 2024

Public disclosure

KnowBe4 publishes the full story so other companies can harden hiring, and the case becomes a reference for fake IT worker tradecraft.

Attack chain

  1. Fabricate identity: stolen identity parts, AI-altered photo, generated resume and portfolio.
  2. Pass remote interviews with AI-assisted answers.
  3. Pass background checks using forged documents and accomplice references.
  4. Receive a company laptop with pre-loaded malware.
  5. Run the loader at boot; establish the foothold and wait for work.

Detection point

The save was endpoint telemetry: EDR flagged the loader within 25 minutes of first boot, before any damage. But the case study teaches a second lesson: earlier signals existed and were not readable. Document metadata, reference-domain clustering, and onboarding telemetry all could have raised the flag weeks earlier. Section 5.5 rules TH-03 exist exactly for that.

Containment, eradication, recovery

Containment: isolate the host, disable the account, stop shipping, and freeze onboarding. Eradication: reimage the device, revoke issued credentials, and review anything the identity touched. Recovery: no data breach occurred, so recovery focused on process. Identity proofing was rebuilt with verified document checks, references now get called on independently found numbers, and onboarding telemetry became a monitored data source.

Impact and consequences

Direct impact was contained to one laptop and a lot of time. The potential impact was severe: an attacker with an employee seat, credentials, and a laptop inside a security company. Industry consequences were positive: the case became standard training material for HR and security teams on fake IT worker schemes.

Team response and communication

The response combined IR, HR, and leadership, with external forensics from Mandiant. The disclosure itself was a controlled communication: what happened, what was caught, what changed. Publishing quickly turned an embarrassing hire into a sector-wide defense improvement, and it followed the pattern this guide recommends: verify, contain, then communicate with limits you can stand behind.

Lessons learned

  • Fabricated written identities pass normal hiring; identity proofing must go beyond resume review.
  • References must be verified through independently obtained channels, not candidate-supplied contacts.
  • Onboarding is a security event: watch first-boot telemetry like a production deployment.
  • EDR on every device, including new hires, is the last line that works.
  • Fast, honest disclosure strengthens the whole sector's defenses.

Mitigation and Defense

MITRE assigns M1056 Pre-compromise: you cannot patch the attacker's word processor. Defense means hardening every place written content lands: mail, hiring, finance, and internal trust.

Enterprise controls

Email authentication and policy

Enforce DMARC at p=reject, require alignment for partner mail, and quarantine first-contact external mail that mimics internal teams (HR, IT, payroll). Banner external mail, and treat sender history and domain age as primary signals, because content no longer betrays AI-written lures.

Hiring and identity proofing

Verify applicant identity with government-issued documents checked against liveness. Call references on numbers found independently. Watch for reference clusters on fresh domains. Treat onboarding as a monitored security event with EDR present from first boot, and review remote hires for location and identity consistency.

Finance process controls

Require dual approval and beneficiary cooling-off periods. Verify any banking-detail change by phone on a known number. Never treat an email thread, however long and fluent, as verification. These controls defeat even perfect AI-written BEC.

Identity and mailbox security

Use phishing-resistant MFA for all users, monitor inbox rule creation and internal mass-sends, and alert when they follow anomalous sign-ins (rule TH-04). Revoke sessions on credential reset so stolen tokens die.

Logging and monitoring

Centralize mail gateway, proxy, IdP, HR SaaS, and finance workflow logs. Run the five detections in 5.5, validate them quarterly, and track alert-to-report ratios. Keep document metadata extraction in the attachment pipeline so PDF Producer fields survive into the SIEM.

Brand and external monitoring

Monitor domains and Certificate Transparency for brand look-alikes, and social platforms for fake recruiter or support personas. Maintain an authorized-accounts list and a fast takedown path with marketing and legal.

People and training

Train for verification habits, not spelling: pause, check the sender path, and confirm unusual requests out of band. Run simulations built from the current lure theme board. Make reporting one click and celebrate good catches publicly.

Response readiness

Keep a written lure-response runbook: preserve, analyze headers, hunt, purge, reset, share. Pre-agree the fraud path with finance, and pre-agree the takedown path with legal. Practice quarterly with a written-lure scenario, not just a payload scenario.

Home and small-office advice

The same written attacks hit households and small businesses: fake invoices, fake delivery notices, fake job offers. Verify every payment-detail change by phone using a number you already have. Never pay an urgent invoice that arrived by email alone. Be careful with job offers that send you equipment or ask you to cash checks; both are known scam patterns. Keep MFA on every important account, and talk openly in the family about "too perfect" messages.
