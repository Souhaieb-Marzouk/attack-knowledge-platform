MITRE ATT&CK · Initial Access · Sub-technique of T1195

T1195.003 Compromise Hardware Supply Chain

The deepest version of the technique: the attacker tampers with a physical device — its firmware, a chip on its board, or the shipment itself — before it reaches you. The implant can survive disk wipes, operating system reinstalls, and even replacement of the device's software entirely.

  • Tactic: Initial Access
  • Platforms: Linux · Windows · macOS · (hardware and firmware in scope)
  • Parent: T1195 Supply Chain Compromise
  • ATT&CK since: 11 March 2020 · Version 1.1
  • Detection difficulty: Very hard
  • Guide v2.0 · checked against MITRE, October 2026
What it is
Malicious capability is added to a device between the factory and your rack: firmware implant, added chip, or in-transit tampering.
Why it is serious
Reinstalling the OS does not remove it; the implant sits below the software you manage.
Documented cases
Juniper ScreenOS unauthorized code (2015); the disputed "Big Hack" report (2018); counterfeit and interdiction cases in government advisories.
Main defense
Buy through verified channels, verify firmware versions and hashes, monitor device networks, and track hardware identity.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    What "hardware supply chain" means in practice, why firmware-level attacks are so hard to remove, and what this guide teaches about catching them.

    What the technique is

    Every device you own is itself built by a chain: chip factories, board assemblers, firmware developers, integrators, shippers, and resellers. An attacker who touches any link can deliver a device that is the attack. The three most practical forms are:

    • Firmware implants. Extra code in a device's firmware (the built-in software that starts the hardware) or in the firmware of a component like a disk controller or BMC.
    • Added chips. A tiny extra component on the circuit board that modifies data between other chips.
    • In-transit tampering. A shipment opened and modified between the vendor and you.

    Why it matters

    Software attacks leave software evidence, and you can usually remove software. A firmware implant sits below the operating system: it survives reimaging, can lie to the OS about what it does, and can persist even when the disk is replaced. That is why this sub-technique is the most feared and least publicly documented member of the T1195 family.

    The best public case is the Juniper ScreenOS backdoor of 2015: unauthorized code inside the firmware source of firewall products gave a master password and weakened encryption. Whether it entered via a build compromise or development, its lesson stands: the device you trust can betray you at a level your tools may not see.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    As an Initial Access technique, the implant waits inside hardware you install. It may offer direct remote access, harvest traffic, or disable protections. Because it lives below the OS, it also makes a unique persistence layer — attackers sometimes come back through it for years.

    Two words, defined

    Firmware: the low-level software stored inside a device that starts and controls its hardware. BMC (baseboard management controller): a small computer inside servers that allows remote management even when the server is powered off — which makes it both precious and dangerous.

    Written version of the simulation

    The same eight steps in words.

    1. Map the route. The attacker identifies the appliance model your organization buys and studies its shipping path from integrator to data center. Defender view: nothing. You have not bought anything unusual.
    2. Intercept the shipment. A crate is diverted for hours at a logistics hub. The tamper is designed to leave no visible damage; seals are restored. Defender view: nothing, unless you compare carrier timestamps with delivery scans.
    3. Install the implant. The device firmware gains a small addition: passive listening with periodic check-ins to attacker infrastructure, sleeping until activated. Defender view: nothing. The device now contains the attack, but has never touched your network.
    4. Delivery and deployment. The crate completes its journey. Your team racks the switch, restores the configuration, and the device passes every functional test. Defender view: a normal delivery and a normal deployment event.
    5. The implant calls home. Weeks later, the appliance begins sending small, periodic flows to an external address on an unusual port. Defender view: first partial signal — device-range traffic that no policy explains.
    6. SOC sees the anomaly. The network baseline flags egress from an appliance subnet to a first-seen external host. Device-range traffic is rare enough that any hit is investigated. Defender view: alert raised.
    7. Physical verification. The SOC pulls the device from service, checks serials against procurement records, hashes the firmware against vendor images, and finds the mismatch. Defender view: confirmed compromise; replacement ordered.
    8. Hardening the route. Procurement switches to verified channels, delivery scans are compared with carrier data, and firmware verification becomes a deployment gate. Defender view: contained, and next time the implant dies at the deployment gate.

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    Hardware attacks trade cost for permanence. This section explains what makes a target attractive, the realistic attack paths, and why most attempts still fail — described at the level of concepts and lab-safe exercises only.

    Why attackers use this technique

    • Permanence. Reimaging does not touch firmware. The implant survives most remediation playbooks.
    • Invisibility. The operating system's tools see what the firmware chooses to show them.
    • Position. A tampered network device sits where all traffic flows; a tampered BMC reaches the server even when it is off.
    • Deniability and time. Weeks or months can pass before any behavior difference appears.

    Realistic attack paths

    1. Interdiction. Intercept and modify devices in transit (the classic nation-state pattern described in government advisories).
    2. Factory or integrator access. Compromise or coerce a link in the manufacturing chain.
    3. Firmware update abuse. Serve modified firmware through a hijacked update path — the software-supply-chain bridge into hardware.
    4. Counterfeit components. Substitute chips or whole devices with altered versions in the spare-parts flow.

    What it takes to succeed, and what usually fails

    This is the most expensive technique in the family. The attacker needs physical or insider access to a shipping route or factory, deep knowledge of the target firmware, and a way to keep the implant silent during the victim's acceptance testing. State-level resources are the realistic minimum; that is why confirmed public cases are so rare.

    What usually fails: the covert channel. No matter how well the implant hides, it must eventually communicate, and communication is the one thing defenders can baseline. Devices that never talk externally must be reached another way, which means physical proximity — a major operational cost.

    Safe lab exercise: fingerprint your own fleet

    You need no implants to learn the defense — only a rack and curiosity:

    1. Pick two device classes (a server model and a switch model) and record their BIOS/BMC firmware versions and hashes from the vendor console.
    2. Dump device identity: serials, MAC addresses, and board identifiers, and store them in your asset register.
    3. Baseline the devices' networks: what do they contact, how often, from which subnets? Devices should produce almost nothing.
    4. Update one device with vendor firmware and re-check hashes; repeat after a reboot. This exercise builds exactly the telemetry that section 5.5 turns into detections.

    If you cannot answer "what firmware version and hash is on that BMC?" for a device in your data center, that is the finding — before any attacker is involved.

    Common attacker mistakes

    • Breaking functionality. A device that fails acceptance testing gets returned, not deployed.
    • Talking too early. Calling home during the soak period burns the operation.
    • Leaving physical traces. Broken seals, torque marks, and serial mismatches are caught by inspection programs.
    • Reusing infrastructure. A covert channel shared with other operations links them all together.

    Variations seen in the wild

    • Firmware source insertion at a vendor (Juniper ScreenOS) — software means, hardware-level effect.
    • Alleged board-level implants (the disputed 2018 server report) — never independently confirmed, but it reshaped procurement policy.
    • Counterfeit network gear found in government supply chains, with backdoor configurations.
    • Keystroke-injecting USB devices swapped in shipments — the low-budget version of the same idea.

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    You cannot audit silicon, but you do not need to. A hardware implant must be shipped, must boot, must manage itself, and eventually must communicate. Every one of those steps can be observed. This section shows where.

    How defenders can see it

    • Procurement and logistics data. Serials, purchase orders, and carrier scans; tampering usually leaves a paperwork gap.
    • Boot and integrity telemetry. Measured boot logs, CodeIntegrity warnings, and vendor firmware hashes that change without a documented update.
    • Device identity telemetry. Kernel-PnP and NAC data: serials, MACs, and device instance IDs that differ from the asset register.
    • Device network behavior. Appliance and BMC subnets should be almost silent; anything they emit is worth reading.

    Artifacts it leaves behind

    Physical
    Seal damage, weight or screw differences, serial or MAC not matching records.
    Firmware
    Version or hash drift without a change ticket; blocked drivers at boot.
    Network
    Management-plane flows to first-seen external hosts; odd ports or periodicity.
    Identity
    A "new" device claiming an existing slot; the same MAC from a different switch port.

    Where to look first

    • Device VLANs and management networks: baseline them to zero external traffic, then alert on any hit.
    • Firmware version and hash inventory: collect it per device class; drift without a ticket is a finding.
    • Kernel-PnP and NAC logs: hardware identity changes on servers are rare and review-worthy.
    • Procurement: buy only through channels that give you chain-of-custody data; it makes the logistics diff possible.

    Why it matters

    Firmware implants defeat most of the response toolbox: rebuilds, reimaging, even disk swaps. The controls that work are simple but powerful — verified channels, identity records, network baselines for devices, and firmware hash gates. They cost little and they cover counterfeits, gray-market gear, and plain old rogue updates as a bonus.

    Short triage flow

    1. Treat the alert as physical. For device-network anomalies, the first question is "which device, where racked?" — answer from NAC and asset data.
    2. Freeze the device's traffic. Contain at the switch port or firewall before touching the hardware.
    3. Verify identity. Serial, MAC, and model against procurement records and the asset register.
    4. Verify firmware. Version and hash against the vendor's signed image; use the vendor's verification tool where one exists.
    5. Replace, do not clean. A confirmed-firmware compromise ends with vendor-supported replacement and out-of-band forensics.
    6. Close the route. Feed findings back into procurement and logistics checks.

    Watch out

    Do not diagnose a suspected firmware implant from the running OS alone. Its tools are the implant's tools. Pull the device from the network, verify from outside (vendor tooling, hashes, physical inspection), and document before reconnecting anything.

    Known actors and cases

    • Unauthorized ScreenOS code (Juniper, 2015). Backdoor password and weakened RNG in firewall firmware; one of the clearest firmware-trust failures.
    • 2018 server implant allegation. Widely reported, firmly denied, never independently confirmed — a case study in how to handle disputed intel.
    • Counterfeiting rings in government supply chains, documented by customs and agency audits.
    • Interdiction tradecraft described in national advisories as a state-level capability.

    Confidence levels, in practice

    High
    Vendor-confirmed unauthorized code, or your own hash mismatch on a device. Act immediately.
    Medium
    Credible research on implant feasibility in your device class. Verify your fleet.
    Low
    Disputed press allegations. Document, review fleet against the described pattern, move on.

    Applying the intelligence

    1. Track your vendors' firmware advisories as seriously as software CVEs; unauthorized-code disclosures are the signal.
    2. Translate cases into checks: each public case adds one fleet check (hash, identity, route).
    3. Handle disputes honestly: label allegations with confidence, act on the cheap checks, avoid panic buys.
    4. Watch logistics data as an intel source: rerouted shipments and reseller anomalies are early warnings.

    5.4 Logs and patterns

    Sources, fields, normal versus suspicious, detection logic, and one event example per threat.

    TH-1 — BMC reached from the wrong subnet

    Log sources: firewall or switch ACL logs for the management VLAN; BMC/Redfish access logs forwarded via syslog; DHCP logs.

    Fields that matter: src_ip, dst_ip, dst_port, action, Redfish user, method, path.

    Normal behavior: only the jump-host subnet talks to BMC addresses, on 443/22/623, with named admin accounts. Nothing else should appear.

    Suspicious behavior: any source outside the management subnets reaching a BMC; new users created in Redfish logs; firmware update commands from unusual accounts.

    Detection logic: firewall events where destination is inside the BMC range and source is not the jump-host range; plus Redfish log events for user creation or firmware operations outside the change window.

    BMC access log — Redfish firmware push from wrong subnet (JSON)
    {"ts":"2026-01-09T22:14:09Z","bmc":"10.60.3.21","product":"BMC-Redfish",
 "remote_ip":"10.31.8.44","method":"POST","path":"/redfish/v1/UpdateService",
 "user":"admin","status":202,
 "detail":"firmware update task created (Oem-Manufacturer-Image)",
 "asset_tag":"SRV-004221"}

    TH-2 — Device beaconing to first-seen infrastructure

    Log sources: Zeek connection and DNS logs; firewall session logs for device VLANs.

    Fields that matter: id_orig_h (device), id_resp_h, id_resp_p, proto, duration, orig_bytes/resp_bytes, DNS query.

    Normal behavior: appliance subnets talk to vendor update and NTP hosts only, on documented schedules, and mostly never leave the site.

    Suspicious behavior: any periodic outbound flow to a first-seen external IP, especially fixed-interval, small, on odd ports; DNS queries to new domains from device ranges.

    Detection logic: connection logs where the origin is in a device range and the responder is external and not on the device allowlist; period detection raises severity.

    Zeek conn log — periodic flow from appliance VLAN (JSON)
    {"ts":"2026-01-10T01:32:11Z","uid":"CzqT4L2VbP9","id_orig_h":"10.50.4.12",
 "vlan":"appliance","id_resp_h":"198.51.100.77","id_resp_p":8443,"proto":"tcp",
 "duration":4.2,"orig_bytes":512,"resp_bytes":2048,
 "periodic_seconds":3600,"first_seen_responder":true,"conn_state":"SF"}

    TH-3 — Hardware identity drift on a known slot

    Log sources: Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-PnP/Configuration; Sysmon Event ID 13 for USBSTOR and network adapter registry keys; NAC/802.1X logs; asset register diffs.

    Fields that matter: DeviceInstanceId, DriverName, registry SerialNumber, DHCP mac, NAC auth_result.

    Normal behavior: a server's NIC and storage identities are stable for years; changes come only with documented maintenance.

    Suspicious behavior: a NIC with a new MAC claims the same switch port and VLAN; a storage device with a different serial appears in the same controller slot; an unrecorded serial shows up in NAC.

    Detection logic: alert on device-instance or MAC changes for servers where no change ticket exists; weekly diff of identity data against the asset register.

    Kernel-PnP Event ID 400 — NIC instance changed on server (JSON)
    {"EventTime":"2026-01-11 07:55:32","EventID":400,"Computer":"DB-PRD-03",
 "DeviceInstanceId":"PCI\\VEN_8086&DEV_1593&SUBSYS_00018086&REV_02\\4&37FE54A&0&00E7",
 "DriverName":"e1d.sys",
 "PreviousInstanceId":"PCI\\VEN_8086&DEV_1593&SUBSYS_00018086&REV_02\\4&37FE54A&0&00E1",
 "MacAddress":"A4-BB-6D-11-22-33","PreviousMac":"A4-BB-6D-9E-0F-41",
 "TicketMatch":false,"AssetTag":"SRV-003988"}

    TH-4 — Boot-integrity warnings after new hardware

    Log sources: Microsoft-Windows-CodeIntegrity/Operational Event ID 3076/3077; Secure Boot and measured-boot event data; deployment logs.

    Fields that matter: EventID, FileName, PolicyName, SIPEolicyVersion, Computer.

    Normal behavior: fleets with enforced code integrity log few or no 3076/3077 events; new hardware images match approved builds.

    Suspicious behavior: blocked unsigned drivers on a machine with no recent software change; a burst of integrity warnings right after delivery or a firmware "update".

    Detection logic: alert on 3077 (enforced block) always, and on clusters of 3076 (audit) for servers, correlated with delivery or firmware-update events.

    CodeIntegrity Event ID 3077 — unsigned driver blocked (JSON)
    {"EventTime":"2026-01-12 05:47:18","EventID":3077,"Computer":"DB-PRD-03",
 "FileName":"\\Device\\HarddiskVolume3\\Windows\\System32\\drivers\\ioctrl.sys",
 "PolicyName":"Microsoft Windows Driver Policy",
 "ProcessName":"","SHA256Hash":"B1F2E4D8A6C3F7902E5B8D1C4A7F0E3B6D9C2A5F8E1B4D7C0A3F6E9B2D5C8A1F",
 "CorrelationFirmwareUpdate":"2026-01-11 23:10"}

    TH-5 — Unapproved driver loaded via vendor channel

    Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 6 (driver loaded); driver inventory jobs; vendor update logs.

    Fields that matter: ImageLoaded, Signed, SignatureStatus, Hashes, Company.

    Normal behavior: servers load only drivers on the approved list, signed by known vendors, with stable hashes.

    Suspicious behavior: a driver that is unsigned, self-signed, or signed by an unknown certificate loads on a server; a known driver's hash changes without a patch cycle.

    Detection logic: driver-load events where the signature is not valid or the signer is not in the allowlist; hash changes for approved driver names.

    Sysmon Event ID 6 — unsigned driver loaded (JSON)
    {"EventTime":"2026-01-13 03:21:44","EventID":6,"Computer":"HV-NODE-05",
 "ImageLoaded":"C:\\Windows\\System32\\drivers\\hwassist.sys",
 "Signed":false,"SignatureStatus":"Unavailable",
 "Hashes":"SHA256=6E8A1C3F5B7D9E0A2C4F6B8D0E2A4C6F8B0D2E4A6C8F0B2D4E6A8C0F2B4D6E8A",
 "Company":"-","LoadedViaService":"HwAssistSvc"}

    TH-6 — HID-capable device appearing where it should not

    Log sources: Microsoft-Windows-Kernel-PnP/Configuration Event ID 400/410; Sysmon Event ID 13 for USB registry entries; endpoint USB control software.

    Fields that matter: DeviceInstanceId, DeviceID (VID/PID), ClassName, SerialNumber.

    Normal behavior: user machines have a small, stable set of HID devices (keyboard, mouse, headset) recorded at provisioning.

    Suspicious behavior: a device reporting a storage vendor ID that also exposes a keyboard interface; a "new" HID on a locked-down host; the same VID/PID/serial appearing on many machines in one day.

    Detection logic: PnP events where a device instance contains both USBSTOR/USB\VID_ of mass-storage vendors and Class HIDClass; plus any new HID on hosts with a USB allowlist policy.

False positives: should approach zero once ranges are right; treat every hit as a finding. Tuning: pair with the Redfish log alert for user creation and firmware pushes outside change windows.

    Real-World Case Study: Juniper ScreenOS (2015) and the Disputed "Big Hack" (2018)

    Hardware implants leave the thinnest public record of the whole T1195 family. Two cases anchor the honest picture: a confirmed unauthorized code insertion in firewall firmware (Juniper ScreenOS), and a disputed report of board-level implants in servers. Together they show both the reality of the technique and how defenders should judge unconfirmed information.

    Case one: Juniper ScreenOS — confirmed

    In December 2015, Juniper announced that unauthorized code had been found in ScreenOS, the operating system of its NetScreen firewalls: a master-password bypass (CVE-2015-7755) and a change that weakened random number generation, allowing passive decryption of VPN traffic (CVE-2015-7756). The affected code had shipped in official firmware for years. Whoever inserted it had deep knowledge of the platform; researchers linked the RNG manipulation to the Dual EC DRBG design years earlier, and attribution speculation has never been confirmed publicly.

    For defenders, the lesson was uncomfortable: a device bought to enforce trust boundaries had been betrayed inside its own firmware, and no customer configuration could have prevented it. Detection meant vendor disclosure, differential firmware analysis, and hunting for backdoor logins.

    Case two: the "Big Hack" — disputed

    In October 2018, a business publication reported that tiny chips had been added to server motherboards at contract factories, allegedly to exfiltrate data for a nation-state customer. Apple, Amazon, Supermicro, and U.S. officials publicly denied the story, and no independent confirmation ever followed. Whether or not the specific events occurred, the report triggered concrete industry change: stricter vendor provenance programs, hardware bills of materials, and government bans on certain suppliers.

    The case is a lesson in intelligence discipline: treat a disputed report as a hypothesis, run the cheap verifications (identity records, firmware hashes, route audits), and avoid panic decisions based on unconfirmed claims.

    Timeline (combined)

    1. 2012 – 2015ScreenOS code inserted. Unauthorized changes ship inside official Juniper firmware for multiple releases before discovery.
    2. December 2015Juniper disclosure. Two backdoor capabilities announced; patches issued; agencies worldwide scramble to assess exposure.
    3. October 2018The implant allegation. The server-chip story is published; denials follow within hours; procurement policies tighten anyway.
    4. 2019 – 2020sStructural response. Defense procurement bans selected vendors; provenance and hardware-BOM practices spread; firmware security becomes a purchase criterion.

    Attack chain (ScreenOS, as understood publicly)

    • Deep platform knowledge
    • →
    • Unauthorized code in firmware source or build
    • →
    • Signed official releases
    • →
    • Backdoor login and weak RNG
    • →
    • Vendor disclosure and patch

    Note the boundary: this is firmware-level compromise with software means, which is why classification between .002 and .003 blurs at the firmware layer. Defensively, the lesson belongs on this page because the response — firmware verification, vendor trust review — is the hardware playbook.

    Detection point and response

    In both cases, victims learned from outside: vendor disclosure and press, not local alerts. After disclosure, the practical response was the same in kind: identify affected devices and firmware versions, verify installed images against vendor-signed builds, hunt for backdoor logins and anomalous management access, and replace what could not be verified. The Juniper response also required treating years of VPN traffic as potentially readable — a rare case where the blast radius extended backward in time.

    Impact and communication

    ScreenOS: firewall fleets worldwide required emergency patching; enterprises re-evaluated single-vendor trust at the perimeter. The 2018 report: no confirmed victims, yet billions in procurement decisions moved. In both, calm, evidenced communication mattered: teams that published verified facts (versions, hashes, actions) built trust faster than teams that said either "nothing to see" or "worst case" without evidence.

    Lessons learned

    • Firmware is a trust boundary: verify versions and hashes, and record them per device.
    • Vendor disclosure is your likely first signal; make sure their advisories reach you same-day.
    • Disputed reports deserve cheap checks, not panic purchases.
    • Backdoor-capable incidents extend backward in time: assume past sessions were observed.
    • Procurement is a security control: verified channels and provenance data pay for themselves.

    Mitigation and Defense

    Hardware defense is mostly process discipline: buy verifiably, record identities, keep devices silent, and gate firmware changes. Enterprise and home guidance below, then priorities.

    ATT&CK mitigation mapping

    MITRE lists one official mitigation for T1195.003:

    • M1046 Boot Integrity — use Trusted Platform Module (TPM) technology and a secure or trusted boot process so system integrity cannot be quietly compromised, and check the existing BIOS or EFI for vulnerability to modification. Secure boot and measured boot are the host-side parts of this control; firmware hash verification is the device-side part.

    Enterprise: procurement and routes

    • Verified channels only: buy from vendors or integrators that provide chain-of-custody data; avoid gray-market resellers for infrastructure.
    • Receiving inspection: compare serials, seals, and weights against the purchase order; photograph and record on arrival.
    • Logistics diffing: compare carrier route data with delivery scans; investigate divergences.

    Enterprise: firmware and identity

    • Firmware inventory: per device class, record BIOS/BMC/appliance versions and hashes; verify against vendor-signed images.
    • Identity records: serials, MACs, and board IDs in the asset register; diff monthly.
    • Update gates: firmware updates only from vendor-signed packages, in change windows, with hash verification and rollback plans.

    Enterprise: network containment

    • Management isolation: BMCs and appliance management planes on dedicated VLANs reachable only from jump hosts.
    • Device baselines to zero: appliance and IoT ranges emit nothing external by policy; alert on any hit.
    • Secure boot and code integrity enforced on hosts, so firmware tampering meets a loud boundary.

    Enterprise: response readiness

    • Hardware-incident playbook: contain at the port, verify identity and firmware from outside the OS, replace rather than clean.
    • Vendor IR contacts for every infrastructure vendor, tested yearly.
    • Forensic preservation: image firmware and log what you can before RMA — your evidence leaves with the device.

    Home and small office

    • Buy routers and cameras from official channels; register them with the vendor.
    • Apply firmware updates when the vendor offers them; check monthly.
    • Put smart devices on a guest or IoT network away from work machines.
    • Replace, do not troubleshoot, accessories that behave strangely (keyboards typing by themselves, hubs running hot).
    • Reset routers to factory defaults and change default passwords at setup.

    Verifying the controls work

    • Attempt a BMC login from a user subnet in a test segment; the firewall must block and log it.
    • Force a firmware hash mismatch in the lab gate; deployment must halt.
    • Re-run the identity diff after a real maintenance window; tickets must match changes.

    Cheat Sheet and Quick Reference

    The compressed hardware playbook for your next architecture review or unexplained device alert.

    Key facts

    Technique
    T1195.003 Compromise Hardware Supply Chain (Initial Access) · ATT&CK version 1.1, created 11 March 2020, last modified 24 October 2025
    Platforms
    Linux · Windows · macOS (MITRE), with hardware and firmware in scope
    Core idea
    Poison the device or its firmware before it reaches you; it survives OS rebuilds
    Attack paths
    Interdiction, factory access, firmware update abuse, counterfeits
    Confirmed public case
    Juniper ScreenOS unauthorized code (2015); the 2018 implant report remains unconfirmed
    Best signals
    Device-range egress, BMC access from wrong subnets, identity drift, integrity blocks, unsigned drivers
    Best prep
    Verified channels, identity records, firmware hash gates, device network baselines

    Top detections

    • BMC or management plane reached from a non-management subnet (TH-1 rule).
    • Appliance VLAN talking to a first-seen external host (TH-2 rule).
    • Device identity change with no maintenance record (TH-3 rule).
    • Code Integrity blocking drivers on servers (TH-4 rule).
    • Unsigned or unknown-signer driver loaded (TH-5 rule).
    • Storage-class USB device exposing a keyboard interface (TH-6 rule).

    Response checklist

    • Identify the device from NAC and asset data; note rack, port, and owner.
    • Contain at the switch port or firewall before touching hardware.
    • Verify identity: serial, MAC, model against procurement records.
    • Verify firmware version and hash against vendor-signed images.
    • Preserve what you can: firmware dumps, logs, photographs.
    • Replace confirmed-compromised devices through the vendor with custody data.
    • Assume past management sessions or VPN traffic may have been observed.
    • Check sibling devices from the same shipment batch.
    • Feed findings into procurement and the receiving checklist.
    • Record the case in the asset register so the next diff starts informed.
