Virtual Network Computing (VNC)

VNC is remote-control software. It shows you the screen of another computer and lets you move its mouse and type on its keyboard. Attackers love old VNC servers. These servers often have short weak passwords, they rarely keep logs, and a VNC session looks exactly like a support engineer doing their job.

Written for beginners, SOC analysts, and detection engineers. All examples are for learning and defense only.

TCP 5900 (+5800 web)
Default port for display 0; 5800 serves the browser client.
Full desktop control
The viewer sees the screen and drives mouse and keyboard.
Weak legacy passwords (max 8 chars)

Red Team Perspective

How VNC works, why attackers pick it, and what a minimal access chain looks like.

VNC (Virtual Network Computing) is software that shares one computer's screen with another computer over the network. The part that shares the screen is the server. The part that watches and controls is the viewer. The viewer sends mouse moves and keystrokes, and the server draws them on the real machine. The protocol underneath is called RFB (Remote Framebuffer). It is old, simple, and runs on almost every operating system.

How the pieces fit together

The server side

The server listens on a TCP port, which is a numbered door on the machine. Display :0 uses port 5900, display :1 uses 5901, and so on up to 5906 and beyond. Many servers also open 5800 to serve a browser-based viewer. Some servers can also dial out to a viewer that waits on TCP 5500. That is called listening mode, or a reverse connection. The server waits for a viewer, asks for a password, and then streams the screen.

The viewer side

The viewer is a small client such as vncviewer. It connects to the server, answers one password prompt, and then shows the remote desktop in a window. There is no extra login, no second factor, and no ticket like Kerberos (the ticket system Windows domains use). One password is the whole front door.

Where you find VNC: VNC is common on point-of-sale (POS) terminals, shop kiosks, industrial control panels, lab machines, and any device a vendor wants to support remotely. Installers often leave it running and reachable long after the support visit ends. This is why ATT&CK lists VNC under Remote Services (T1021.005), and why it shows up in retail intrusions so often.

Why attackers use VNC

  • Weak legacy passwords. The classic VNC check reads at most 8 characters of the password. It uses an old DES-based challenge and response (DES is an outdated encryption method). There is no lockout after wrong guesses. A short shared password like support123 falls to a word list in minutes.
  • Often no logs. Many VNC servers record nothing, or only success events. Failed guesses, session content, and file moves leave no trail on the endpoint.
  • Full GUI control. The attacker can click the antivirus tray icon, open the payment app, or plug a USB drive in software. Everything looks like a human at the keyboard.
  • Looks like support activity. A remote session on a POS terminal is expected at some level. Security teams assume a vendor is behind the mouse.
  • Cross-platform. The same viewer reaches Windows, Linux, and macOS servers, so one tool covers the whole store.

How it is done (lab only)

The three commands below are the whole chain: find the exposed server, guess the password, and open the desktop. They are shown for defensive learning only. Run them only on machines you own or have written permission to test.

bash
# 1. Find internet-exposed VNC servers (lab range only)
masscan -p5900 203.0.113.0/24 --rate 1000

# 2. Guess the VNC password with a word list
hydra -P rockyou.txt vnc://203.0.113.40

# 3. Connect and take the desktop (display :0)
vncviewer 203.0.113.40::5900

# Linux lab variant: start a short-lived test server on port 5901
# (-localhost keeps it on this lab box only)
x11vnc -rfbport 5901 -passwd 'LabTemp-2026' -localhost
Watch out (noise): This chain is loud. A mass scan at 1000 packets per second fills flow logs, and password guessing produces dozens of short sessions to one port. Once the session is open, the traffic pattern flips to one long, steady flow. Those two shapes are exactly what the detections in section 4 look for.
Ethics: Connecting to a VNC server you do not own is unlawful access in most countries, even with an open port and a guessed password. Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Use an isolated lab with reserved IP ranges such as 203.0.113.0/24 or 10.3.8.0/24.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

The server often logs nothing. The network and the endpoint must tell the story instead.

For defenders, VNC has one lesson above all others: assume the VNC server itself gives you no logs. Many servers write nothing at all. Almost none record failed password attempts. Some Windows builds add a few lines to the Application log, and open-source servers keep quiet by default. When the endpoint stays silent, the truth lives in two other places: the network between machines, and the Windows events that the attacker's actions create as side effects.

Watch out: A missing log is not proof that nothing happened. If port 5900 is open anywhere in your estate, treat "no VNC logs" as a blind spot to design around, not as a clean result.

Why this matters: the chain position

VNC can sit at two points of an attack. As initial access, an internet-exposed server with a weak password is the way in — this is the retail pattern in the simulation. As lateral movement, an attacker already inside the network reaches for VNC. They want to control a machine that has no agent, such as a kiosk or a panel on an operational technology (OT) network. In both cases the attacker wants full desktop control without dropping a noisy exploit. In both cases your strongest evidence is on the wire.

Canary idea: A canary is a fake system placed to attract attackers and alert on any touch. Put a canary VNC endpoint on each retail VLAN, with a tempting name like POS-SUPPORT. Nobody should ever connect to it. One session means someone is probing VNC endpoints, and the alert is nearly false-positive free.

First response when you find a VNC session

First hour

  • Pull every flow involving port 5900 on that subnet for the past 30 days.
  • Ask who the support vendor is, and request their session window.
  • Check the endpoint for Sysmon 1, 13, and Events 7045 and 7036 around the session. Read the VNC password values in the registry for changes.
  • Decide fast: is the machine part of payment processing? If yes, escalate to the PCI (payment card) response process.

First day

  • Block the source ASN (the network owner of the source IP) at the edge.
  • Isolate the terminal from the store VLAN but keep memory and logs.
  • Remove rogue persistence: Run keys, scheduled tasks, VNC services.
  • Rotate every vendor-support password and close direct remote paths.

Where to look

  • Retail VLANs (a VLAN is a separated network segment): every store subnet that holds POS terminals should export flow records.
  • OT and kiosk networks: sign displays, check-in kiosks, and industrial panels often run VNC with nobody watching them.
  • Vendor-support tooling: keep an inventory of every machine with VNC installed, who installed it, and why it still exists.
  • Outbound 5900: a workstation connecting out to 5900 on the internet is a pivot or a rogue server, not support.
  • Non-standard ports: attackers move VNC to ports like 15900 to dodge port-based watchlists; fingerprint the RFB banner instead of trusting ports.
  • Registry password keys: a change to HKLM\SOFTWARE\TightVNC\Server\Password or a RealVNC password value means someone reset the access.
  • The payment environment: any VNC inside the cardholder data environment is a PCI DSS finding by itself.

Detection Engineering Deep Dive

Regulations, threat intelligence, ranked threats, log samples, and seven Sigma rules.

This section turns the blind-spot lesson into engineering. You will map the risk to control frameworks, load the actor context, and rank the threats. Then you will read the raw log shapes and deploy seven Sigma rules. Sigma is an open format for writing detection logic once and running it on any SIEM. A SIEM is the system that collects and joins logs from many sources.

Regulation Mapping

VNC sits at the crossing point of remote access, payment data, and monitoring duties. That makes it relevant to most security frameworks your auditors use.

Plain VNC without TLS fails 2.2.7, because screens and keystrokes travel unencrypted. A single shared support password fails 8.4.2, because there is no second factor for vendor access.

Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence (CTI) is the work of turning public reporting into detection ideas. The loop has three steps. Collect: pull reports and indicators from MITRE ATT&CK, CISA advisories, and vendor blogs. Useful blogs include Unit 42, Tenable, Exa, and AhnLab. Add sharing feeds such as MISP and OTX, plus retail sector groups like the retail ISAC. Analyze: decide which behaviors apply to your estate — for VNC, that means exposed ports, weak legacy passwords, and hidden VNC installs. Apply: turn each behavior into a hunt query or a Sigma rule, and track when it last fired.

Hunting hypotheses

Run these as scheduled hunts. Each one is a question your data can answer even when VNC logs nothing.

H1 — Inbound 5900 from non-support subnets

Keep an allow list of support-source networks. Any 5900 flow from outside that list, especially from a hosting provider or an unusual country, deserves a case. The simulation fired on exactly this shape.

H2 — New VNC process outside the gold image

The gold image is your approved software baseline. Any VNC binary that is not in the baseline, especially one under Users\Public, Temp, or AppData, is rogue persistence.

H3 — VNC session plus AV stop within 10 minutes

Join 5900 flows with Event 7036 lines where a security service entered the stopped state. A remote session that ends protection first is an attacker preparing the machine, not a vendor doing support.

Turning hunts into rules

When a hunt fires on real data twice, promote it to a Sigma rule with an owner and a severity. Section 4.5 gives you seven ready rules to start from.

Logs & Patterns

Each threat gets its own block: the logs to use, a realistic sample, and what to look for. Field names differ by product, so treat the shapes as the lesson, not the exact values.

Threat 1 — Full takeover of POS/payment systems via VNC

Threat 2 — Ransomware staging through VNC sessions

Before encrypting, attackers stop protection. Windows records service state changes as Event 7036 in the System log, and Sysmon logs the process that did it.

Log Name:      System
Source:        Service Control Manager
Event ID:      7036
Level:         Information
Computer:      POS-TERM-04.retail.local
Message: The avservice service entered the stopped state.
  (occurred 2026-01-14 06:31:02, during VNC session from 203.0.113.9)

<Event><System><Provider Name="Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon"/><EventID>1</EventID>
</System><EventData>

  • 7036 stop events for security services inside or right after a 5900 session window.
  • The stopping process is a tray or shell parent (explorer.exe), because the click came through the GUI.
  • The same host shows shadow copies deleted or backups failing shortly after.

Threat 3 — Brute force of weak VNC passwords

VNC has no lockout, so guessers loop: connect, try one password, disconnect. NetFlow turns that loop into a clear shape — many short sessions to one port from one source.

log — netflow summary
2026-01-14 03:40:11 exporter=store-edge-01
  src=203.0.113.9 dst=203.0.113.40 proto=tcp dport=5900
  flows=128 avg_duration=0.9s flags=SYN,RST-heavy
  window=22min verdict=credential-guessing-pattern
  • 20 or more short 5900 sessions from one source in 10 minutes.
  • Sessions that reset instead of closing cleanly, which fits the guess loop.
  • A burst that stops the moment one session becomes long — the password worked.

Threat 5 — Unencrypted VNC session sniffing

Classic VNC sends screen updates without TLS (the padlock protocol the web uses). Zeek's vnc.log records which authentication the server offered, which tells you whether the session is protected.

log — zeek vnc.log (no strong auth)
#separator \x09
#fields ts      uid     id.orig_h       id.orig_p       id.resp_h       id.resp_p       client-major-version    client-minor-version    server-major-version    server-minor-version    authentication-method   auth    auth-success    share-flag      desktop-name    width   height
1736845200.108  Cr7tNq2Bv5      10.3.8.17       49811   10.3.8.41       5900    3       8       3       8       None    (empty) T       F       POS-TERM-04     1024    768
  • Servers advertising None as the authentication method — those sessions are wide open.
  • Legacy VNC auth on the same VLAN as payment traffic, where the screen stream can be captured.
  • VNC servers that stayed on version RFB 003.008 with no TLS wrapper in front. Note that the authentication-method field is what Zeek reports; None means no password and no encryption challenge.

Threat 6 — View-only session abuse and insider risk

View-only mode hides the mouse but still shows the whole screen. Some RealVNC builds do write connection lines to the Windows Application log — this is one of the few native VNC logs worth shipping.

  • Session durations that do not match any support ticket.
  • Application-log connections from sources outside the support allow list.

Threat 7 — VNC on non-standard ports to evade watchlists

Attackers move the RFB service to ports like 15900. Port rules stop matching, but the protocol banner does not change, and Zeek still recognizes the handshake on any port where it decodes it.

log — firewall flow (odd port)
Jan 16 02:55:19 fw-edge kernel: ACCEPT TCP 203.0.113.9:44190 -> 203.0.113.40:15900
  rule=any-any-high proto=6 intf=wan bytes_in=41200000 bytes_out=8100000 duration=3610s
  note=zeek marks service=vnc on this 5-tuple (RFB banner RFB 003.008)
  • RFB banners on ports other than 5900-5906 and 5800-5806.
  • New long-lived inbound sessions on high ports to hosts that normally serve nothing.
  • Port changes on hosts that used to listen on 5900 — that move is often the attacker relocating.

Threat 8 — Legit vendor support (false-positive driver)

Real support keeps this rule set honest. An EDR that watches process starts gives you the clean counter-example: a VNC process from its installed path, during a ticketed window.

log — edr (expected support pattern)
2026-01-15 14:02:11 EDR POS-TERM-04 process_start
  image=C:\Program Files\TightVNC\tvnserver.exe
  user=SYSTEM parent=services.exe
  verdict=expected context=support_ticket INC-20841
  net_out=10.3.8.41:5900 -> 198.51.100.23:51244 allowlist=vendor-payments-co
  • Support sessions that match a ticket, an allow-listed source, and the installed binary path.
  • Differences from that pattern (new binary, new source, odd hour) are what make the other rules trustworthy.
  • Feed the allow list from change tickets, not from memory.

Sigma Detection Rules

Each rule below matches one threat from the ranking. Under every rule you find the matching Splunk SPL. Ship Sysmon with a full config on retail endpoints, and make sure store firewalls and switches export flow records — rules 1, 6, and 7 live on the network side.

Rule 1 — Inbound VNC port from an unusual source

Fires on any inbound connection to port 5900-5906 that does not come from your support allow list. This is the core rule for exposed VNC, and it is the rule that would have caught the simulation at step 2.

yaml — sigma
title: Inbound VNC Port from Unusual Source
id: 6e1f2a34-8c05-4b7e-9a12-3f4d5c6b7e80
status: stable
description: >
  Detects an inbound network connection to VNC ports 5900-5906 whose source is
  not an approved support network. Internet-exposed VNC servers with weak
  legacy passwords are a common retail and kiosk foothold.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1021/005/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026/10/27
tags:
  - attack.lateral_movement
  - attack.t1021.005
logsource:
  category: network_connection
  product: windows
detection:
  selection_dst:
    DestinationPort:
      - 5900
      - 5901
      - 5902
      - 5903
      - 5904
      - 5905
      - 5906
    Initiated: 'false'
  filter_support:
    SourceIp|startswith:
      - '198.51.100.'
  condition: selection_dst and not 1 of filter_*
falsepositives:
  - A new support vendor subnet that is not on the allow list yet
  - An internal vulnerability scanner checking 5900

spl — splunk
index=sysmon EventCode=3 DestinationPort>=5900 DestinationPort<=5906 Initiated=false SourceIp!=198.51.100.*
| stats count AS connections, dc(DestinationIp) AS targets, max(_time) AS last_seen BY SourceIp, DestinationPort
| sort - connections

A VNC binary belongs in Program Files. One running from a public or temp folder is a dropped server, and this is the persistence alert from step 5 of the simulation. It should page.

Rule 6 — VNC password brute force indicators

Aggregates short sessions to 5900 per source. VNC has no lockout, so guessers loop openly: 20 or more connections in 10 minutes from one source is a guessing pattern, not a support engineer.

Rule 7 — Large egress right after a VNC session

Adds up the bytes a host and its 5900 sessions move inside the window. A support visit that carries a gigabyte is not support. This is step 6 of the simulation, where the visit becomes a scraper and a copy job.

Real-World Scenario: The Vendor-Support Backdoor

A real 2023 campaign shows the pattern; this store story shows how it lands in retail.

The campaign anchor here is real and publicly reported. In 2023, researchers at Exa, Tenable, and AhnLab described a crew tracked as Chaya_004 (Tenable's name: Water Curupira Pikapika). The crew mass-scanned the internet for exposed VNC servers. It brute-forced their weak legacy passwords and installed XMRig cryptocurrency miners on the Windows machines it won. Unit 42 separately reported Water Galura (2024) using VNC screen sharing in cryptocurrency scams. The store story below is a realistic composite. It extends that documented VNC abuse with the equally documented habit of retail stores keeping vendor-support VNC running on payment terminals. Names and numbers are invented for teaching.

The setup

A regional store chain, 240 locations, runs a payment terminal called POS-TERM-04 in one store. Years ago, the payment vendor asked for remote access, so an installer put TightVNC on the terminal and forwarded port 5900 through the store firewall. The password is support123, shared with two vendors. Nobody wrote it down in the password manager. Nobody removed the rule after the project ended. The store server, STORE-SRV-01, runs the same password because the same installer was there.

Timeline

DAY 1 — 03:12
The scan finds the door

The attacker runs masscan across a consumer IP range and finds 5900 open on 203.0.113.40, the store's edge address, NATed to POS-TERM-04. A banner grab answers RFB 003.008. The attacker now knows a VNC server sits behind the port.

DAY 1 — 03:40
One word list, one password

hydra guesses against the VNC challenge. There is no lockout, and the legacy check reads only 8 characters. After 128 tries in about 22 minutes, support123 works. The VNC server records nothing.

DAY 1 — 04:05
Three hours at the screen

The attacker opens the desktop with vncviewer and browses like a person. They look at the payment app, the folder layout, and the antivirus tray icon. At 06:30 they click Stop on the security service. Windows writes Event 7036. Nothing else on the terminal objects.

DAY 2 — 01:20
Persistence and a scraper

The attacker copies minivnc.exe into C:\Users\Public and adds a Run key so it starts at every login. They also stage a small memory-scrape folder under C:\ProgramData. Sysmon on the terminal logs both, but nobody ships Sysmon from stores yet.

DAY 3 — 02:48
Cash-out and a second visit

robocopy pushes 1.2 GB from the scrape folder to the attacker's box. The same password then opens STORE-SRV-01, where the attacker pokes at backups and store files. The card network data turns out to be chip-and-PIN protected, so no readable card numbers appear in the copy.

DAY 4 — 09:20
The canary speaks

The chain's SOC is mid-rollout of retail flow monitoring. It gets two alerts at once. A canary VNC endpoint on the store VLAN received its first-ever connection. NetFlow also shows 1.2 GB of egress from one terminal. The SIEM case joins both signals with a Run-key hit from the new Sysmon pilot.

DAY 4 — 10:05
Containment

The SOC blocks the attacker's ASN at the edge, isolates the store VLAN, and pulls the terminal from the network with memory intact. The Run key and minivnc.exe are removed, and all vendor-support passwords are rotated chain-wide the same afternoon.

DAY 5-9
Recovery and review

Store terminals are rebuilt from the gold image. The vendor path is replaced by a brokered support tool with MFA. All 240 stores are audited for VNC. A PCI forensic investigator reviews the payment terminal and confirms no card data loss.

Attack chain

1. Scan for 5900→ 2. Brute force weak VNC password→ 3. Full desktop session→ 4. Stop AV from tray→ 5. Hidden VNC persistence→ 6. Scrape and exfiltrate→ 7. Reuse password on server

Tools used: masscan, hydra (vnc module), vncviewer, net.exe, reg.exe, minivnc.exe, robocopy.

How the team communicated

The SOC opened a war-room call within 40 minutes of the canary alert, with store operations, the payment vendor manager, and the CISO's office on it. Because stores were mid-trading day, store operations sent one short message to managers: keep the terminals on, keep receipts, say nothing to customers. The SOC posted hourly updates during containment, even when the update was "no change", so nobody invented their own story. Vendor management handled the hard conversation: the two vendors lost direct access the same day and were re-onboarded through the broker within a week.

Tip: Write the update template before the incident. Hours, facts known, facts not known, next update time. In a screen-sharing incident, store staff will hear rumors fast; silence fills the gap with worse stories.

Mitigation & Defense

Close the door, then watch it anyway — in that order.

VNC defense has two halves. First, remove the reasons attackers succeed: exposure, weak legacy passwords, and unneeded installs. Second, keep the monitoring that would catch a rogue VNC even if a store manager reinstalls one next month.

Priority actions for the enterprise

ActionStops whatEffort
Never expose 5900-5906 or 5800-5806 to the internet. Remove NAT rules for support ports.Scanning, brute force, and the whole exposed-VNC pattern.Low.
Broker vendor access: a jump tool or VPN with MFA and session recording, no direct portsShared passwords, unattributed sessions, password reuse pivots.Medium.
Upgrade or replace legacy VNC: TLS-enabled VNC variants, or a modern remote-support productThe 8-character legacy password check and unencrypted screens.Medium.
Strong passwords plus lockout where the server supports it; firewall rate limits where it does notWord-list guessing without any lockout.Low.
Uninstall VNC where nothing needs it; keep an inventory of every install with an ownerAttack surface you forgot you had.Low.
Segment POS and kiosk networks (PCI scoping) so a VNC win does not spreadLateral movement from a terminal to servers and other stores.Medium.
Ship EDR plus Sysmon from retail endpoints; export flows from store switches; add canary VNC endpointsThe blind spot: servers that log nothing themselves.Medium.
Alert on new VNC installs (7045, Sysmon 1) and on 5900 sessions outside the support allow listRogue re-installs and out-of-band access.Low.
Official MITRE mitigations: ATT&CK lists four mitigations for T1021.005, and every row above puts one of them into practice. Audit (M1047): inventory workstations for unauthorized VNC server software. Disable or Remove Feature or Program (M1042): uninstall any VNC server software where it is not required. Filter Network Traffic (M1037): block the default ports — 5900 for the server, 5800 for browser access, and 5500 for a viewer in listening mode. Limit Software Installation (M1033): only approved user groups may install software, because a VNC server must be installed by a user or by the attacker.

Config examples

Block the ports inbound on store and POS machines, then find every VNC already installed so you can plan removal. Run the discovery per machine or push it with your endpoint manager.

cmd
:: Block all inbound VNC ports on the machine (run as admin)
netsh advfirewall firewall add rule name="Block inbound VNC (POS)" ^
  dir=in action=block protocol=TCP localport=5900-5906,5800-5806

:: Find installed VNC software by product name (slow but thorough)
wmic product where "name like '%%VNC%%'" get name,version,vendor

:: Find VNC in the uninstall registry and in services
reg query HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Uninstall /s /f VNC
sc query type= service state= all | findstr /i "vnc"
Watch out: wmic product is slow and only lists MSI-installed software. The registry and service queries catch portable copies that the installer never registered. Cross-check all three against your software inventory.
Why the firewall rule still matters: Even after VNC is removed, a blocked port stops the next copy someone installs. Defense in depth is cheap here: one rule, no user impact, permanent value.

Layered defense flow

1. Block 5900 at the edge→ 2. Broker vendor access with MFA→ 3. Harden or remove VNC→ 4. Segment POS networks→ 5. Monitor flows, canaries, and installs

Each layer buys time for the next. An exposed port plus a weak password fails at step 1. A brokered session with MFA forces an attacker to steal a session. The monitoring layer is built to see exactly that.

For home users

  • Never port-forward 5900 on your router so you can reach a home computer from outside.
  • Use a mesh VPN such as Tailscale or WireGuard to reach your machines instead; VNC then never touches the internet.
  • Set a long, unique VNC password, and prefer a server version that supports encryption.
  • Uninstall VNC apps you no longer use; a quiet, unwatched server is the one that gets found.

Common Mistakes & Best Practices

Ten habits that keep VNC attacks invisible — and the fix for each one.

Most missed VNC attacks follow the same few mistakes. Read the table as a checklist for your own estate: every row on the left is something a real team has said out loud.

Common mistakes and their fixes

MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
Assuming "VNC logs nothing, so there is nothing to do"The blind spot becomes an excuse. The attacker gets a quiet machine.Build network-first detections: flows, volumes, and canaries carry the case.

Best practices for detection engineers

  • Keep a live inventory of every VNC listener, its port, its version, and its owner, and reconcile it with EDR data monthly.
  • Ship Sysmon with a full config from every retail endpoint, and make rules 2, 3, and 4 of this guide your starting set.
  • Export flow records from every store switch and keep them at least 90 days; flows are the witness when endpoints are silent.
  • Place canary VNC endpoints in retail VLANs and page on any connection — it should never fire falsely.
  • Correlate service stops (7036) with 5900 sessions on the same host within 15 minutes; that join is your staging alert.
  • Alert on egress volume after VNC sessions, not just on the session itself.
  • Fingerprint RFB banners network-wide so a port change cannot hide a server.
  • Feed support allow lists from change tickets, and expire entries automatically.
  • Test every Sigma rule against replayed samples — the log shapes in section 4 are ready-made test data.
  • Rehearse the scenario in section 5 once a year with store operations and vendor management in the room.
Closing thought: T1021.005 is a technique where the endpoint often says nothing and the network says everything. Build your detections on flows, volumes, and the small Windows events attackers create as side effects, and the "logs nothing" problem stops being yours.
