T1584Compromise Infrastructure

Attackers do not always rent their tools. Sometimes they steal someone else's. This technique covers how threat actors take over websites, domains, DNS servers, routers, botnets, and cloud services that belong to other people, and then quietly use them to stage attacks. Because this infrastructure is real and trusted, attacks launched from it are harder to spot and harder to block.

Overview

This section explains the technique in plain words, why it is dangerous, and where it sits in an attack. It also shows the difference between buying infrastructure and stealing it.

What the technique is

T1584 Compromise Infrastructure means an attacker takes over systems that belong to other people and uses them to prepare an attack. The hijacked assets can be internet domains, DNS servers, web and mail servers, virtual private servers (VPS), networks of infected home devices (botnets), popular online services, serverless cloud functions, or office network devices like routers.

The key idea is theft instead of purchase. A stolen server costs the attacker almost nothing, inherits the good reputation of its real owner, and is often already trusted by firewalls, mail filters, and users.

Why it matters to defenders

Attacks that come from trusted infrastructure are much harder to stop. A phishing email sent from a hospital's mail server, or a malware beacon that talks to a clean-looking government website, will pass many security checks. Blocking the IP address can also hurt the innocent owner of that server.

Most of this activity happens before the attacker ever touches your network. That means your best chance to see it is through external monitoring, threat intelligence, and quick reporting from users and partners.

Where it fits in the attack chain

ATT&CK calls this stage Resource Development. It happens before the victim is involved. The hijacked infrastructure then supports many later techniques:

  1. T1584 Compromise Infrastructure
  2. T1566 Phishing (hosting / links)
  3. T1071 Application Layer Protocol (C2)
  4. T1568 Dynamic Resolution (redirectors)
  5. T1041 Exfiltration Over C2 Channel

One compromised server can play several roles at once: phishing page host, malware download site, command-and-control (C2) relay, and data drop location.

Compromise vs. acquire

Do not confuse this technique with T1583 Acquire Infrastructure. In T1583 the attacker buys or rents resources: registers a new domain, rents a VPS, buys a domain name. In T1584 the attacker takes over resources that somebody else already owns.

Why does an attacker prefer theft? Three reasons: it is cheaper, the asset already has history and reputation (an important detail for spam filters), and the true owner becomes an unintentional shield that makes attribution harder.

    The same story in words

    1. Find a weak server. The attacker scans the internet for an unpatched web server. SOC view: scan patterns appear on the target's firewall from an unknown address.
    2. Exploit and take control. A known vulnerability is abused and the attacker gains a foothold. SOC view: the WAF logs the exploit attempt and the web process starts a shell.
    3. Stage attacker tooling. A web shell and phishing pages are uploaded to the server. SOC view: new files appear in the web root and tools are downloaded.
    4. Point the stolen domain. A domain the attacker controls is repointed to this server with a DNS change and a fresh certificate. SOC view: the certificate transparency log shows a new cert.
    5. Send the lure. A phishing email to a target employee links to the fake login page on the trusted-looking domain. SOC view: the mail gateway flags the link.
    6. Harvest credentials. The employee enters their password and the fake page saves it. SOC view: a POST to an unknown page on the domain.
    7. Relay to the attacker. Stolen data moves from the server back to the attacker. SOC view: the server sends data to a network it never talked to before.
    8. SOC responds. Analysts correlate the alerts, block the address, and sinkhole the domain. SOC view: the attacker's route is cut off.

    Red team / hacker perspective

    This section explains how attackers think when they reuse other people's infrastructure. Everything here is described for education and defense only. Practice these ideas exclusively in your own isolated lab.

    When and why attackers use it

    An attacker chooses hijacked infrastructure when reputation matters. A domain registered yesterday looks suspicious to mail filters; a domain that has belonged to a real organization for ten years does not. The same logic applies to servers: a clean VPS from a known "bulletproof" host is on many blocklists, while a compromised municipal website is not.

    It also breaks takedown workflows. When defenders report a malicious VPS, the provider shuts it down. When the malicious content sits on an innocent university server, takedown is slow, legal review is needed, and the attacker simply moves to the next stolen asset.

    How the takeover happens (conceptual)

    • Domain hijack: registrar account takeover, expired-domain re-registration, or social engineering of support staff.
    • Server compromise: exploiting an unpatched web application, weak admin passwords, or abandoned panels.
    • Device compromise: default credentials or old firmware on routers, cameras, and VPN appliances.
    • Service takeover: stealing session cookies or API keys of legitimate web service accounts.

    Where it applies and what it needs

    Lab practice that stays legal

    You can safely experience both sides of this technique in an offline lab with virtual machines. For example: run a deliberately vulnerable web app in a VM, "compromise" it, and then study your own logs to see the evidence. Typical lab steps:

    1. Deploy a vulnerable web server (for example, a training image) inside an isolated network.
    2. Use dig, whois, and nmap against your own lab hosts only.
    3. Simulate a "hijacked domain" by editing your lab DNS zone to point at the lab server.
    4. Watch the logs on the server, the resolver, and the firewall while you repeat the steps.
    Danger. Never scan, exploit, or change DNS records for systems you do not own. Even "harmless" tests against real domains are illegal in most countries.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    • Variation — long stay: keep a low profile for months, use the asset only briefly per campaign.
    • Variation — relay chains: chain several hijacked servers so no single one reveals the attacker.
    • Mistake — IP reuse: using one stolen server across campaigns lets defenders connect them all.
    • Mistake — loud staging: mass tool downloads on a server trigger EDR and admin alerts.
    • Mistake — forgetting certificates: a domain without a valid TLS certificate scares victims and raises flags.

    Blue team / SOC perspective

    Defenders cannot prevent every hijack on the internet, but they can see much of it. This section shows where infrastructure abuse becomes visible, which artifacts it leaves, and a short triage flow for analysts.

    How defenders can see it

    Infrastructure abuse produces two kinds of visibility. Outward signals happen on the internet itself: DNS record changes, new certificates, passive DNS history, and reputation feeds. Inward signals happen when your users or servers interact with the hijacked asset: phishing reports, strange DNS answers, or unexpected connections in your own logs. A mature SOC watches both. If you only watch inside your perimeter, you will see the attack very late — often only after a user has already clicked.

    Artifacts it leaves and where to look

    ArtifactWhere to lookExample signal
    Nameserver / DNS record change on a domain you ownRegistrar audit log, DNS hosting console, Certificate TransparencyA-record changed outside a change window
    Web shell on your internet-facing serverWeb server access logs, WAF, file integrity monitoringPOST to a rare .aspx/.php path returning 200
    Fresh TLS certificate for a lookalike domainCertificate Transparency logs (crt.sh, Censys)Cert issued for portal-yourbrand.com yesterday
    Traffic to a newly hijacked or repurposed serverProxy logs, NetFlow, Zeek connection logsBeacon to an IP that changed ownership this month
    Botnet scan / flood from consumer IPsFirewall logs, DDoS mitigation dashboardsThousands of unique residential IPs hitting one port
    Compromised network deviceDevice syslog, config diffs, SNMP monitoringConfig change at 03:00 from an unknown source

    Why this matters for your organization

    Infrastructure compromise hits you in two directions. As a victim: your brand can be used to attack your customers, which damages trust and may create legal duties. As a target: attacker infrastructure that looks legitimate defeats user training ("check the domain!") and technical filters alike. Fast detection on either side shortens every later attack stage.

    Short triage flow

    1. Alert received (DNS change, cert, phishing report, odd egress).
    2. Validate: is the change approved? Check change tickets and owners first.
    3. Enrich: WHOIS, passive DNS, CT logs, threat intel for the asset.
    4. Scope: who connected, logged in, or received mail linked to it?
    5. Contain: sinkhole/block domain, block IP, revoke sessions.
    6. Report & hunt: add indicators, hunt for past activity, notify partners.
    Good practice. Build a simple "infrastructure change" alert list: nameserver changes on your domains, new certificates for your brand names, and first-time outbound connections from servers. These three cheap alerts catch a large share of T1584 activity.

    5.2 · Threat intelligence

    Because T1584 lives mostly outside your network, external intelligence does the heavy lifting. A simple pipeline works: collect raw signals, analyze them against your assets, apply the result to detections and blocks, then review what worked.

    Sources worth wiring up

    • MITRE ATT&CK: the T1584 pages and citations describe real actor use.
    • Vendor reporting: Cisco Talos, ESET, Microsoft MSTIC, Mandiant blogs publish hijack campaigns.
    • OSINT: passive DNS (e.g., SecurityTrails, RiskIQ), Certificate Transparency (crt.sh, Censys), urlscan.io, Shodan, abuse.ch feeds, Spamhaus.
    • ISACs: sector sharing groups (FS-ISAC, H-ISAC, MS-ISAC) push fast, relevant alerts.
    • Commercial feeds: IP/domain reputation and newly-registered-domain (NRD) feeds.
    • Internal telemetry: your own registrar audit logs, mail gateway reports, proxy logs — the highest-signal source you own.

    Confidence levels that work

    Tag every indicator with a confidence level and keep the meaning strict:

    • High: confirmed by two independent sources or direct observation (e.g., your own logs + a vendor report).
    • Medium: one reliable source, no second confirmation yet — good for detection, not for blocking.
    • Low: rumor, paste-site claim, or machine-only correlation — good for hunting lists only.

    Write the level into the indicator record so the analyst who meets it at 03:00 knows how much to trust it.

    5.5 · Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    The four rules below cover the highest-value threats (TH-01, TH-03, TH-04, TH-07). Each Sigma rule is valid YAML and is followed by a matching Splunk query, a mapping note, and tuning advice. Replace index and sourcetype names with your local naming — the field assumptions are stated under each query.

    SIG-001 · Registrar audit — unauthorized nameserver or A-record change

    Mapping note: registrar:audit is the JSON audit feed from your registrar/DNS provider (Route 53, Cloudflare, GoDaddy all offer one). Field names above assume the JSON keys are used as CIM-style field names — rename in a tstats-friendly props.conf or use spath. False positives: unticketed emergency changes. Tuning: allowlist provider automation actors, and require the on-call process to open tickets retroactively.

    SIG-002 · Web shell access on internet-facing server (TH-03)

    Mapping note: assumes Splunk's IIS field extractions (cs_uri_stem, sc_status, c_ip). False positives: CMS file managers. Tuning: replace the generic regex with your baseline of rarely-seen paths (use tstats over 30 days to build it) and keep filter_known_paths updated.

    SIG-003 · Web server process spawns a shell (TH-03/TH-04)

    Mapping note: uses Sysmon Event ID 1 (process creation). False positives: planned maintenance jobs. Tuning: allowlist known maintenance scripts by hash or command-line prefix; alert within minutes, because this event usually means active hands-on access.

    SIG-004 · Egress beacon to legitimate web service from a server (TH-07)

    Mapping note: assumes a Squid-style proxy log with parsed url_domain and user (change to cs_username for Blue Coat style logs). The 5-minute bucket approximates "fixed cadence". False positives: build servers fetching from GitHub. Tuning: maintain an allowlist of CI identities and their expected domains; treat any other server identity as a finding and verify byte patterns (C2 usually small and regular).

    Warning. Validate every rule in a test index before production, and record the validation result in the rule's comments. A rule that has never fired in a test is a guess, not a detection.

    Real-world case study — Operation Ghost (Turla)

    Between 2013 and 2019, the Turla group ran one of the longest documented infrastructure-compromise operations. ESET researchers called it Operation Ghost and published the full story in October 2021. It is a perfect teaching case because it shows how hijacked servers quietly carry an entire spy platform.

    Context

    Turla is a well-known espionage group, linked by many investigators to Russian state interests. Instead of renting new servers for each campaign, they relied on hacked third-party servers around the world. The stolen servers formed relay chains: victim computer → hijacked server 1 → hijacked server 2 → attacker. No single server ever revealed the attacker's true location.

    Attack chain in short

    1. Compromise internet-facing servers (old CMS apps, weak panels).
    2. Install quiet relay software that forwards traffic to the next hop.
    3. Chain several relays, mixing hosting companies and countries.
    4. Point dormant malware in victim networks at the relay chain.
    5. Send commands through the chain; store files on hijacked hosts.

    Timeline

    1. ~2013
      Infrastructure building. Turla compromises the first servers and connects them into relay chains; some chains stay alive for over six years.
    2. 2017
      Discovery begins. Investigators following a separate Turla case notice that some C2 traffic disappears into servers that look like ordinary small-business websites.
    3. 2019
      Multi-country investigation. Swiss and other authorities, coordinated with ESET, map the relay chains and collect enough evidence for action.
    4. October 2019
      Seizure wave. Police seize the compromised servers in coordinated raids; the relay chains go dark at once.
    5. October 2021
      Public report. ESET publishes "Operation Ghost", documenting the hijacked-server technique end to end.

    Detection point

    The break came from hypothesis-driven correlation: investigators saw small, periodic connections from victim machines to servers with no logical relationship to the victim. The servers had three shared traits — old unpatched web apps, unusually stable uptime for "neglected" machines, and outbound traffic at fixed intervals. Each trait alone was noise; together they were the relay chain.

    Response arc

    • Containment: the relay servers were physically seized, cutting the C2 path instantly.
    • Eradication: hard — the implants sat dormant in victim networks and had to be found by hunting, not by waiting for beacons.
    • Recovery: victims rebuilt trust in their egress paths; Turla partially rebuilt chains on new hijacked servers, proving persistence of the method.

    Impact and team response

    The operation shows the true cost of infrastructure compromise: decade-scale dwell time and intelligence loss that no single victim could see. Response needed rare cooperation — several countries' law enforcement, a hosting industry, researchers, and victims' teams sharing indicators quietly. Communication followed a strict need-to-know pattern until seizure day, because a leak would have let Turla move early.

    Lessons learned

    • Relay servers look boring: patch hygiene on third-party assets matters as much as on your own.
    • Periodic, low-volume egress from servers is a first-class detection target.
    • Infrastructure takedowns work but are slow; detection must not depend on them.
    • Dormant implants require hypothesis-based hunting after any C2 path is cut.

    Who responded — team roles

    SOC analysts (L1/L2)Correlated the small, periodic beacon pattern across hosts and mapped every node in the relay chain

    Mitigation and defense

    ATT&CK lists only M1056 Pre-compromise for this technique, which honestly says: you cannot fully prevent it from inside your network. What you can do is make hijacking harder, see it faster, and limit what a hijacked asset can do.

    Enterprise controls by layer

    Policy & identity

    • Registrar accounts: unique logins, hardware MFA, registry lock, transfer authorization codes stored safely.
    • Written DNS change process with tickets and dual approval.
    • Annual review of who can change DNS, certificates, and cloud DNS zones.

    Network & cloud

    • Egress allowlists for servers: they rarely need broad internet access.
    • Management interfaces (SSH/RDP/Telnet/SNMP) only from a VPN or bastion, never from the internet.
    • Cloud guardrails: alerts on new functions, keys, and DNS zones; no long-lived root keys.

    Endpoint & patching

    • Patch internet-facing applications first; they are the raw material for T1584.004.
    • File integrity monitoring on web roots; alert on new executables.
    • EDR on servers, not only on laptops.

    Logging, monitoring, response

    • Ship registrar, DNS, certificate (CT), web, and device syslog into the SIEM.
    • Run the Section 5 detections; review them quarterly.
    • Practice a "hijacked domain" runbook: verify → sinkhole → revoke sessions → notify customers.

    Home and small office

    • Change default passwords on your router and cameras; enable automatic firmware updates.
    • Turn off remote management on your router unless you truly need it.
    • Register your family or business domain with MFA and auto-renew ON — expiry is the #1 way small domains get stolen.
    • If your site gets defaced or used for phishing: change passwords, restore from a clean backup, and report to your host immediately.

    Response checklist — suspected infrastructure abuse

    • Confirm the asset owner and the change ticket (is it approved?).
    • Screenshot and preserve: WHOIS, passive DNS, certificate history.
    • If your domain: re-point DNS back, force re-authentication of mail users.
    • If attacker asset: block at proxy/EGress, sinkhole domain, add to intel with confidence level.
    • Scope: search 90 days of logs for the domain, IP, and certificate fingerprints.
    • Notify affected partners/customers if your brand was misused.
    • File the incident record with timeline and evidence links.
