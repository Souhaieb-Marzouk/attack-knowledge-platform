MITRE ATT&CK · INITIAL ACCESS

T1199 Trusted Relationship

Why break the door when you can borrow the key? Attackers compromise a vendor, managed service provider, or contractor you already trust - and walk in through legitimate access channels. This page explains the technique and how defenders can spot abuse of their own trust.

  • Tactic: Initial Access
  • Platforms: Windows, Linux, macOS, IaaS, SaaS, Office Suite, Identity Provider
  • Added: 2018 (v2) · checked vs ATT&CK v20
  • Guide version: 2.0
  • Data sources: Cloud/tenant audit logs, VPN logs, Process creation
  • Detection difficulty: Hard (access is legitimate)

What it is

Entering through a partner, vendor, MSP, or contractor that already has trusted access.

Primary risk

One compromise upstream reaches many customers - your defenses never saw it coming.

Where it happens

Vendor VPNs, RMM tools, federated identity, guest accounts, remote support.

Detection difficulty

The credentials are real. Only behavior - geo, timing, mass actions - looks wrong.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    What trusted-relationship abuse means, how it differs from supply-chain attacks, and what this page teaches.

    The idea in plain language

    Modern organizations run on trust. IT vendors hold admin logins. Managed service providers (MSPs) remote into client fleets. Contractors work inside your tenant. Partners exchange data through standing connections. Trusted-relationship abuse means the attacker compromises one of those trusted parties - or simply impersonates them - and uses their legitimate access to reach you.

    It is different from a supply-chain attack (T1195), where software you install is tampered with before delivery. Here nothing is tampered: the access channels themselves - vendor VPNs, RMM consoles, federated logins, guest accounts - are used exactly as designed, just by the wrong person.

    Why it matters

    The attacker arrives with valid credentials inside a trusted identity - so signature tools see nothing. Worse, the blast radius is fractal: one compromised MSP with hundreds of clients becomes hundreds of simultaneous incidents (Kaseya 2021). Defenses must therefore watch behavior of trusted identities, not just unknown ones.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    T1199 is an Initial Access technique. Once inside, it leans on T1078 Valid Accounts. It often enters through remote channels like T1133. When the trusted party is software, it shades into T1195 Supply Chain Compromise.

    Defender hint

    The same story in text

    1. The upstream target. Attackers phish a two-person IT vendor that manages 40 small businesses. The vendor is small; its access is not. Defender hint: your vendor list is your real attack surface map - keep it with risk tiers.
    2. Credentials captured. The vendor's VPN and RMM admin credentials are harvested through an adversary-in-the-middle phishing page. Defender hint: demand MFA - especially phishing-resistant MFA - from every vendor with remote access.
    3. Trusted login. The attacker logs into a customer environment with the vendor's VPN account. The gateway accepts it - it is a real user. Defender hint: baseline vendor logins (hours, geo, device); anything outside the baseline is an alert.
    4. Console access. The RMM console opens: one pane controlling agents on every managed endpoint. Defender hint: RMM is a crown jewel - monitor it like a domain controller.
    5. Mass command. A single command is pushed to 500 agents at once - far outside any normal maintenance pattern. Defender hint: mass-action anomalies (one command, many hosts, no ticket) are the highest-value RMM alert.
    6. Payload deploys. The command drops a ransomware encryptor on client endpoints. Defender hint: process telemetry on endpoints still applies - agent.exe spawning the payload is visible to EDR.
    7. Detection. The SOC correlates the vendor sign-in anomaly with the mass agent command. Defender hint: correlate identity and endpoint views; either alone looks like a bad Tuesday.
    8. Response. Vendor access is cut org-wide, credentials reset, downstream clients notified, IR engaged. Defender hint: in vendor incidents, containment includes the vendor's other customers - share indicators fast.
    3

    Red team / hacker perspective

    How attackers pick and abuse trust channels, in principle. Conceptual and lab-only.

    Why attackers love trust channels

    They invert the economics of intrusion. Compromising one small MSP yields access to dozens of environments at once - each reached with credentials that log security tools see as normal. Vendor relationships also bring paperwork-shaped trust: firewall rules that "must stay open", accounts that "must stay admin", monitoring that "the vendor already does". Attackers need only one weak vendor among your strong ones.

    How trust is abused (concept)

    Vendor phishing: AiTM kits capture vendor passwords and session cookies. RMM abuse: consoles push commands to every managed agent. Help-desk impersonation: calling your help desk as "the vendor" to get a reset or a token. Federation abuse: guest/B2B accounts and tenant-to-tenant settings used to hop boundaries. Subcontractor chains: the vendor's vendor - trust one hop deeper than you assessed.

    Prerequisites

    One reachable trusted party with weak controls (no MFA, shared logins, no behavior monitoring), plus patience: vendor reconnaissance is quiet. The attacker also needs your vendor list to be more generous than your monitoring.

    LAB-ONLY: audit your own trust channels, not someone else's
    # In YOUR test tenant / lab environment:

# 1) Enumerate standing remote access:
#    - List vendor accounts and their MFA status (identity portal export).
#    - List RMM/remote-support agents installed on lab endpoints.
# 2) Check OAuth grants: which third-party apps hold tokens? Any unverified?
# 3) In the RMM audit log, find the last "run on all agents" action.
#    Who did it? Was there a ticket? Would YOU have noticed a fake one?
# 4) Write the two Sigma rules (vendor sign-in anomaly, mass RMM action)
#    and validate them with synthetic events.
# Auditing third parties without authorization is not a test - it's a breach.

    Common variations

    MSP ransomware at scale (Kaseya 2021). Support-tool compromise (Okta support system, 2023). Help-desk social engineering (MGM 2023 pattern). Vendor VPN credentials bought from access brokers. OAuth consent abuse through trusted-looking integration requests.

    Attacker mistakes that help defenders

    Vendor accounts used at 03:00, from new countries, on new devices. Mass RMM actions with no ticket. Access outside the contract's scope - why does the copier vendor read file shares? Reused infrastructure across multiple vendor victims. And OAuth requests that pile permissions.

    Danger

    Testing trust channels against third parties - even your own vendors - without written authorization is illegal. Audit your side of the trust: your tenants, your logs, your policies.

    4

    Blue team / SOC perspective

    The credentials are real - so the defense is behavioral: baselines for trusted identities and mass-action analytics.

    How defenders can see it

    Four behavior views catch trust abuse. Identity: vendor and guest accounts signing in from new countries, devices, or hours. Platform audit: RMM consoles and SaaS admin planes logging mass actions - one command touching hundreds of agents. Endpoint: the trusted agent binary behaving badly (agent.exe spawning shells or payload). Consent plane: new OAuth grants with sweeping permissions requested through trusted workflows.

    Identity artifacts

    Vendor/guest sign-ins with new geo or device; #EXT# guest accounts outside expected flows; cross-tenant setting changes; MFA method changes on vendor accounts.

    RMM / platform artifacts

    Mass command distribution in RMM audit logs; new scripts stored in the console; admin sessions from unusual IPs; agents suddenly running encoded commands.

    Endpoint artifacts

    agent.exe (Kaseya/ConnectWise/ScreenConnect) spawning cmd/PowerShell; payloads written by agent processes; certutil downloads; new services created by agent parents.

    Where to look (log sources)

    • Entra ID / identity provider: sign-in logs (user, geo, device, client app) and audit logs (consents, cross-tenant settings, role changes).
    • RMM audit logs: command history, script library changes, user sessions (Kaseya VSA, ConnectWise, ScreenConnect all keep them).
    • EDR / Sysmon: process creation with RMM-agent parent images; file writes by agent processes.
    • VPN/gateway logs: vendor account sessions vs their baseline.
    • Vendor connection inventory: firewall rules, OAuth grants, and standing integrations - the "who can reach us" list.

    Short triage flow

    1. Confirm the identity. Is this the vendor's real account? Check MFA method, device, and session history.
    2. Check the baseline. Hours, geo, device, and typical actions for this identity - is today normal?
    3. Pause mass actions. If RMM shows an out-of-pattern mass command: hold execution where possible, snapshot scope.
    4. Contain the channel. Disable the vendor account(s), revoke sessions/tokens, close the standing connection.
    5. Scope downstream. Which endpoints received commands? Pull EDR telemetry for each.
    6. Coordinate. Notify the vendor (they may be compromised, not complicit), downstream clients, and your ISAC.
    Warning

    In vendor-compromise incidents the attacker may hold credentials for many customers at once. Your containment must include credential rotation of everything the vendor could reach - and sharing indicators quickly, because the same access may be burning at other victims right now.

    5.2

    Threat intelligence

    Trust-abuse reporting is rich because the incidents are big. Translate it into vendor-program improvements.

    Sources worth following

    MITRE ATT&CK for structure. Vendor incident disclosures: Okta's own support-compromise reports set the standard for transparency. Vendor reporting: Mandiant, Microsoft on MSP/RMM campaigns. CISA advisories: Kaseya-style alerts with IoCs. ISACs: sector-specific vendor warnings. Internal telemetry: your own vendor-account baselines - the most actionable intel you own.

    How to apply it

    After each public vendor incident, run the checklist: do we use this vendor, which version, which accounts, what access? Convert every report into three artifacts: a vendor-register update, a hunt query, and a contract clause or control change. Track RMM platforms used by your MSPs as a specific watch item.

    5.3

    Threats ranking and categorization

    Five realistic threats built on T1199, ranked and categorized. These five are reused in sections 5.4 and 5.5.

    RankThreatSeverityPlatform
    1Compromised MSP/RMM platform reaching all clientsCriticalCloud / Network / Windows
    2Vendor/contractor account credential compromiseHighIdentity
    3Abuse of federated/B2B trust (guest accounts, tenant links)HighIdentity / Cloud
    4Contractor device without EDR used as pivotMediumEndpoint / Identity
    5Malicious OAuth app consented via trusted workflowMediumCloud / Identity

    Threat 1 - MSP/RMM platform compromise Critical

    Description: the management plane itself is breached, turning one login into fleet-wide code execution.

    Preconditions: RMM with weak MFA, unpatched zero-days, or over-privileged agent permissions.

    Impact: simultaneous compromise of every managed customer.

    Justification: proven at maximum scale by Kaseya 2021.

    Threat 2 - Vendor account compromise High

    Description: phished or stolen credentials of a vendor account with standing access to your environment.

    Preconditions: vendor accounts without phishing-resistant MFA or behavior baselines.

    Impact: valid-credential access that blends into normal operations.

    Justification: the Target 2013 pattern; still the most common form.

    Threat 3 - Federated/B2B trust abuse High

    Description: guest accounts, cross-tenant settings, or partner federation used to move between organizations.

    Preconditions: generous guest policies, unmonitored #EXT# accounts, standing tenant links.

    Impact: identity-plane access into resources without any malware.

    Justification: identity analytics lag endpoint analytics in most programs.

    Threat 4 - Unmanaged contractor device Medium

    Description: contractor laptops without EDR/MFA hygiene used as pivots into your tenant.

    Preconditions: BYOD contractor access without device-compliance gates.

    Impact: session theft and quiet pivots through the contractor identity.

    Justification: mitigated by Conditional Access device compliance - where deployed.

    Threat 5 - OAuth consent abuse Medium

    Description: a trusted-looking vendor asks a user to consent an app that then reads mail/files programmatically.

    Preconditions: users allowed to consent unverified apps; no admin-consent workflow.

    Impact: persistent programmatic access without any login events.

    Justification: cheap to prevent (consent policies), invisible without audit monitoring.

    5.4

    Logs and patterns

    For each of the five threats: log sources, key fields, normal vs suspicious behavior, detection logic, and a realistic event example.

    Threat 1 - MSP/RMM platform compromise Critical

    Log sources: Sysmon EID 1 (process creation with RMM agent as parent); RMM audit logs; EDR.

    Key fields: ParentImage, Image, CommandLine, User.

    Normal: RMM agents run service tasks, updates, and scripted maintenance with readable, ticketed commands.

    Suspicious: agent processes spawning shells with encoded commands, certutil downloads, or payload writes.

    Detection logic: alert when an RMM agent binary is the parent of a shell or downloader.

    Example event - Sysmon Event ID 1 (XML)
    <Event>
  <System>
    <Provider Name="Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon"/>
    <EventID>1</EventID>
    <TimeCreated SystemTime="2025-03-19T02:46:31.507Z"/>
    <Computer>RETAIL-POS-11</Computer>
  </System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="ParentImage">C:\Program Files (x86)\Kaseya\VSA\AgentMon.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="Image">C:\Windows\System32\cmd.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="CommandLine">cmd.exe /c powershell -nop -w hidden -enc SQBFAFgAIAAoAE4AZQB3...</Data>
    <Data Name="User">NT AUTHORITY\SYSTEM</Data>
  </EventData>
</Event>

    The RMM agent (AgentMon.exe) running as SYSTEM spawned a hidden encoded PowerShell at 02:46. Maintenance scripts are visible and ticketed; hidden encoders at night are not maintenance.

    Threat 2 - Vendor account compromise High

    Log sources: Entra ID sign-in logs; vendor VPN logs; conditional-access results.

    Key fields: UserPrincipalName, IPAddress, Location, DeviceDetail, ClientAppUsed.

    Normal: vendor accounts sign in from fixed ranges/devices during business hours for defined tasks.

    Suspicious: vendor account from a new country or hosting provider, at night, or with a new device fingerprint.

    Detection logic: baseline each vendor identity (geo, device, hours); alert on first-seen deviations.

    Example event - Entra ID sign-in log (JSON)
    {
  "createdDateTime": "2025-03-19T02:41:12Z",
  "userPrincipalName": "svc-hvacpartner@corp.example",
  "appDisplayName": "SharePoint",
  "ipAddress": "198.51.100.142",
  "location": { "city": "Lagos", "countryOrRegion": "NG" },
  "deviceDetail": { "deviceId": "", "operatingSystem": "Windows 10", "browser": "Chrome 122" },
  "status": { "errorCode": 0 },
  "mfaDetail": null
}

    The facilities-vendor service account, historically logging in from one office range in the same country, just signed in from a new country with no device ID and no MFA detail. Every field is a first - and firsts are the detection.

    Threat 3 - Federated/B2B trust abuse High

    Log sources: Entra ID audit logs (cross-tenant settings, invitations); sign-in logs for guest accounts.

    Key fields: Activity, UserPrincipalName (contains #EXT# for guests), TargetResources.

    Normal: guests invited by named owners, sign in from expected tenants, access scoped resources.

    Suspicious: mass guest invitations, cross-tenant access policy changes, guests accessing unexpected workloads.

    Detection logic: alert on cross-tenant setting changes and on guest invitations outside the approved workflow.

    Example event - Entra ID audit log (JSON)
    {
  "activityDateTime": "2025-03-19T03:02:55Z",
  "activityDisplayName": "Update cross-tenant access settings",
  "initiatedBy": { "user": { "userPrincipalName": "admin@partner-tenant.example" } },
  "targetResources": [ { "displayName": "b2bCrossTenantAccessPolicy", "modifiedProperties": [
      { "displayName": "inboundTrust", "newValue": "\"Allow\": true" } ] } ],
  "result": "success"
}

    A partner tenant's admin just changed cross-tenant trust - the identity-plane equivalent of punching a hole in a firewall. If this activity is not from your own identity team, treat it as incident-grade.

    Threat 4 - Unmanaged contractor device Medium

    Log sources: Entra ID sign-in logs (device compliance state); Windows 4624 type 3 from VPN ranges.

    Key fields: DeviceDetail.isCompliant, DeviceDetail.trustType, DeviceId.

    Normal: contractor access requires compliant, enrolled devices (or VDI only).

    Suspicious: successful sign-ins from unregistered, non-compliant devices, followed by resource access.

    Detection logic: alert on successful access from unmanaged devices to sensitive apps.

    Example event - Entra ID sign-in (JSON, abridged)
    {
  "createdDateTime": "2025-03-19T09:22:41Z",
  "userPrincipalName": "contractor.fin@corp.example",
  "appDisplayName": "Finance Reporting",
  "ipAddress": "198.51.100.77",
  "deviceDetail": { "deviceId": "", "isCompliant": false,
                    "trustType": "Unregistered", "operatingSystem": "Windows 11" },
  "status": { "errorCode": 0 },
  "conditionalAccessStatus": "notApplied"
}

    A contractor identity accessed the finance reporting app from an unregistered, non-compliant device - and conditional access did not apply. That combination is a policy failure visible in one event.

    Threat 5 - OAuth consent abuse Medium

    Log sources: Entra ID audit logs ("Consent to application"); app governance tools.

    Key fields: Activity, TargetResources (app, scopes), initiatedBy.

    Normal: admins consent verified, needed apps through a workflow with tickets.

    Suspicious: user-level consents for unverified apps with sweeping scopes (Mail.ReadWrite, Files.Read.All, full_access_as_app).

    Detection logic: alert on consent grants to unverified apps or with high-privilege scopes.

    Example event - Entra ID audit log (JSON)
    {
  "activityDateTime": "2025-03-19T10:15:02Z",
  "activityDisplayName": "Consent to application",
  "initiatedBy": { "user": { "userPrincipalName": "a.mercer@corp.example" } },
  "targetResources": [ { "displayName": "DocFlow Sync Pro",
      "application": { "appId": "f3c1a9d2-84b7-4e60-9a15-6d8c2b7e4f90" },
      "modifiedProperties": [
        { "displayName": "Scope", "newValue": "Files.ReadWrite.All Mail.ReadWrite offline_access" } ] } ],
  "result": "success"
}

    A regular user consented a third-party app to read and write all their mail and files, with offline access (token survives password changes). The app is unverified. This single consent is persistent programmatic access - revoke and investigate.

    5.5

    Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    One complete Sigma rule and one matching Splunk SPL query per threat, with assumptions, false positives, and tuning notes.

    Threat 1 - MSP/RMM platform compromise Rule level: critical

    Sigma rule (YAML)
    title: RMM Agent Spawning Shell or Downloader
id: 40c7e2a9-6b18-4d53-8f60-1a9d4c7e2b35
status: stable
description: Detects RMM agent processes (Kaseya, ConnectWise, ScreenConnect and
  similar) spawning shells or downloaders. When an RMM platform is compromised,
  mass exploitation flows through exactly this parent-child relationship.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1199/
  - https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-200a
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2024-10-15
logsource:
  category: process_creation
  product: windows
  service: sysmon
detection:
  selection_parent:
    ParentImage|contains:
      - '\Kaseya\'
      - '\ConnectWise\'
      - '\ScreenConnect\'
      - '\Syncro\'
      - '\Atera\'
      - '\N-able\'
  selection_child:
    Image|endswith:
      - '\cmd.exe'
      - '\powershell.exe'
      - '\mshta.exe'
      - '\certutil.exe'
      - '\curl.exe'
      - '\wscript.exe'
  condition: selection_parent and selection_child
fields:
  - ParentImage
  - Image
  - CommandLine
  - User
falsepositives:
  - Legitimate scripted maintenance by the MSP (require ticket references)
level: critical
tags:
  - attack.t1199
  - attack.t1078
  - attack.tactic.initial-access
    Splunk SPL
    index=edr sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational EventCode=1
  ParentImage IN ("*\\Kaseya\\*","*\\ConnectWise\\*","*\\ScreenConnect\\*","*\\Syncro\\*","*\\Atera\\*","*\\N-able\\*")
  Image IN ("*\\cmd.exe","*\\powershell.exe","*\\mshta.exe","*\\certutil.exe","*\\curl.exe","*\\wscript.exe")
| table _time host, User, ParentImage, Image, CommandLine | sort -_time

    Field assumptions: Sysmon process events; extend the parent list with your MSP's actual agent paths. False positives: real maintenance - demand ticket IDs in commands and allow-list those. Tuning: add the mass-action dimension in SPL: count children per ParentImage per hour, page when it exceeds your normal ceiling.

    Threat 2 - Vendor account compromise Rule level: high

    Sigma rule (YAML)
    title: Vendor Account Sign-In from First-Seen Country
id: 8e2b6d40-93a7-4c15-8b82-5f0d3a6c9e71
status: experimental
description: Detects sign-ins of vendor/service accounts (naming pattern svc-*) from
  countries never observed for that account. Vendor identities should have tight
  geo baselines; any first-seen country is a strong anomaly.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1199/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1078/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2024-10-15
logsource:
  product: azure
  service: signinlogs
detection:
  selection_vendor:
    UserPrincipalName|startswith: 'svc-'
  condition: selection_vendor | count() by UserPrincipalName, Country > 1
fields:
  - UserPrincipalName
  - IPAddress
  - Location
  - DeviceDetail
falsepositives:
  - Vendor travel or new datacenter egress (confirm with the vendor)
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1199
  - attack.t1078
  - attack.tactic.initial-access
    Splunk SPL
    index=azure sourcetype=azure:monitor:signin result=success UserPrincipalName="svc-*"
| stats dc(Location.countryOrRegion) as countries values(Location.countryOrRegion) as seen by UserPrincipalName
| where countries > 1
| join UserPrincipalName [search index=azure sourcetype=azure:monitor:signin UserPrincipalName="svc-*"
    | stats latest(_time) as latest_login values(IPAddress) as ips by UserPrincipalName]
| sort - latest_login

    Field assumptions: Entra sign-in logs in index=azure with parsed location fields; vendor accounts named svc-* (adjust to your convention). False positives: vendor network changes - confirm with the vendor, then pin their egress. Tuning: add device-ID first-seen logic; require MFA claim presence on every vendor session.

    Threat 3 - Federated/B2B trust abuse Rule level: high

    Sigma rule (YAML)
    title: Cross-Tenant Access Setting Change
id: a15f8c72-40b9-4e86-9d23-7c5e1b8a0d64
status: stable
description: Detects changes to cross-tenant access settings in Entra ID. These
  changes alter trust between organizations at the identity layer and should only
  occur through the identity team's change process.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1199/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2024-10-15
logsource:
  product: azure
  service: auditlogs
detection:
  selection:
    ActivityDisplayName:
      - 'Update cross-tenant access settings'
      - 'Update a cross-tenant access policy'
      - 'Redeem user invitation policy update'
  condition: selection
fields:
  - initiatedBy
  - TargetResources
  - Result
falsepositives:
  - Approved identity-team changes (allow-list by user and ticket)
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1199
  - attack.t1078
  - attack.tactic.persistence
    Splunk SPL
    index=azure sourcetype=azure:monitor:audit
  OperationName IN ("Update cross-tenant access settings","Update a cross-tenant access policy")
| table _time initiatedBy.user.userPrincipalName, targetResources{}, Result
| sort -_time

    Field assumptions: Entra audit logs with parsed operation and initiator fields. False positives: identity-team maintenance - allow-list with ticket references. Tuning: treat any change initiated from a partner tenant admin as immediate page.

    Real-world case study

    Kaseya VSA, July 2, 2021 (REvil): one RMM platform turned into a ransomware distribution system for ~1,500 businesses.

    Context

    Kaseya's VSA product is a remote monitoring and management (RMM) platform: MSPs use it to administer thousands of client networks from one console. On July 2, 2021 - the US Independence Day weekend - the REvil ransomware group exploited an authentication bypass (CVE-2021-30116) plus a code-injection flaw in the on-premises VSA. They used the platform's own agents to push an encryptor to downstream businesses.

    Timeline

    • Early July 2021Attackers compromise on-premises VSA servers using the auth-bypass chain.
    • 2 July 2021Mass agent commands deploy the encryptor (named msdefrag.exe) across client endpoints; Kaseya shuts down its VSA cloud.
    • 3-5 July 2021~60 MSPs hit; estimates of 800-1,500 downstream businesses affected, including 800+ Swedish grocery stores (Coop) closed.
    • July 2021REvil demands USD 70M for a universal decryptor; negotiations via intermediaries.
    • Mid-July 2021A universal decryptor is obtained via a trusted third party (Kaseya statement, July 11); REvil infrastructure goes dark around July 13; Kaseya begins phased VSA restoration.
    • 2022Law-enforcement pressure follows: REvil arrests and infrastructure seizures.

    Detection point

    Sharp SOCs saw two things: VSA's agent.exe spawning encoded PowerShell and certutil activity, and a mass command hitting hundreds of agents in minutes. Most victims learned from encryption - the RMM audit logs held the full story but few were watching them live.

    Containment

    Kaseya took the entire VSA cloud offline; victims disconnected on-premises VSA servers and blocked agent command channels. MSPs severed management connectivity while assessing which clients were hit - containment at the speed of trust.

    Eradication

    VSA servers were rebuilt on patched builds; the universal decryptor allowed many victims to recover data without paying. Affected businesses restored from backups where they existed; where they did not, downtime stretched for weeks.

    Recovery

    Kaseya restored VSA in phases, with the universal decryptor validated on test machines before customer roll-out. Endpoints came back only after clean checks, and MSPs kept heightened monitoring during restoration. The patched VSA builds and the new MFA requirements stayed in place.

    Impact and consequences

    Roughly 1,500 businesses disrupted through no action of their own; Coop's 800+ closed stores made the story national news in Sweden; the incident became the global reference for RMM risk and supply-of-service attacks. Regulators and insurers rewrote MSP expectations afterward.

    Team response and communication

    MSPs stood between Kaseya and their clients - communication had to flow both ways with partial information. CISA coordinated response guidance; law enforcement's decryptor release changed the economics mid-crisis. The lesson for teams: know your MSP's incident-communication plan before you need it.

    Lessons learned

    1. RMM consoles are crown jewels: patch, MFA, IP-restrict, and audit them like domain controllers.
    2. Mass-action monitoring (one command, many hosts) is a top-tier detection.
    3. Your vendor's compromise is your incident; pre-agree communication channels.
    4. Backup and offline-decryptor realities shape extortion outcomes.
    5. Weekend and holiday attack timing is deliberate - coverage must include it.

    Mitigation and defense

    Trust is an attack surface. Manage it like one.

    Policy

    Vendor register with risk tiers and named owners. Access contracts: MFA, notification duties, incident SLAs. Quarterly access recertification for every third-party identity.

    Identity

    Phishing-resistant MFA for all vendor accounts. Dedicated vendor identities (no shared logins). Time-bound access via PAM; JIT elevation instead of standing admin.

    Endpoint

    EDR process rules for RMM-agent parents. Device-compliance gates for contractor access (or VDI-only). Alert when agent binaries spawn shells.

    Network

    IP allow-lists for vendor management traffic. Dedicated VPN profiles for vendors with least-privilege routing. Retire standing firewall exceptions aggressively.

    Cloud / SaaS

    Admin-consent workflow for OAuth (block user consent of unverified apps). Monitor guest accounts and cross-tenant settings. CASB on vendor integrations.

    Logging & monitoring

    Identity, RMM-audit, VPN, and consent logs centralized. Vendor baselines per account (geo, device, hours). Mass-action analytics on RMM consoles.

    Backup & resilience

    Assume vendor channels can be weaponized: backups must not depend on the same management plane. Offline copies for MSP-managed estates.

    Response

    A vendor-compromise runbook: cut the channel, rotate everything reachable, scope downstream actions, notify per contract, and share indicators.

    Good practice - home and small office

    If an MSP manages your systems, ask three questions: do you have MFA on your RMM console, do you keep audit logs of commands, and how would you tell me within an hour if you were breached? Be careful connecting third-party apps to your email and files - if you do not recognize a permission request, do not click accept.

    Common mistakes and best practices

    Ten blue-team mistakes around trusted relationships, with symptom, root cause, fix, and prevention.

    1. Vendor accounts without MFA

    Symptom Intrusion via a phished vendor account that "didn't work with our MFA app".

    Root cause Exceptions for third-party tools never revisited.

    Fix Enforce MFA for all vendor identities; break-glass with expiry.

    Prevention Contract clauses plus quarterly MFA audits.

    2. No vendor baselines

    Symptom Nobody can say what "normal" looks like for the HVAC vendor's login.

    Root cause Baselines only built for employees.

    Fix Document geo/device/hours per vendor identity; alert on firsts.

    Prevention Baseline required at vendor onboarding.

    3. RMM consoles unmonitored

    Symptom Mass command discovered days later during fraud review.

    Root cause RMM audit logs never shipped to the SIEM.

    Fix Stream RMM audit logs; alert on mass actions and new scripts.

    Prevention Treat RMM as tier-0 in the logging standard.

    4. Standing admin instead of JIT

    Symptom Compromised vendor account had permanent domain admin.

    Root cause "Easier for the vendor" provisioning.

    Fix PAM with just-in-time elevation scoped to tasks.

    Prevention Access reviews question standing rights every quarter.

    5. Shared vendor logins

    Symptom No idea which technician acted during the incident.

    Root cause One account per vendor company.

    Fix Individual named accounts with per-person MFA.

    Prevention Contract requirement: no shared credentials.

    6. User OAuth consent wide open

    Symptom Users grant mail/file apps to lookalike "productivity tools".

    Root cause Default tenant consent settings.

    Fix Admin-consent workflow; app allow-list; user education.

    Prevention Monthly consent audit; revoke stale grants.

    7. Guests without expiry

    Symptom #EXT# accounts from a 2021 project still active.

    Root cause Guest lifecycle never defined.

    Fix Access reviews with expiry on guest invitations.

    Prevention Sponsor + expiry mandatory at invitation time.

    8. Cross-tenant settings unmonitored

    Symptom Trust setting changed; nobody noticed for weeks.

    Root cause Audit log activity not alerted.

    Fix Deploy the section 5.5 audit-log rules.

    Prevention Identity-plane changes treated like firewall changes.

    9. Vendor incidents handled alone

    Symptom Slow response while the same access burns other victims.

    Root cause No sharing channel or contractual duty.

    Fix Pre-agreed notification SLAs; ISAC membership.

    Prevention Incident exercises that include the vendor.

    10. Vendor list not maintained

    Symptom Unknown RMM agent found on servers during a hunt.

    Root cause Register updated only at procurement.

    Fix Reconcile register with observed access quarterly.

    Prevention Register feeds the monitoring scope automatically.

    Daily habits

    • Review vendor sign-in anomalies and RMM mass actions.
    • Check new OAuth consents against the allow-list.
    • Skim guest-account activity for unexpected resources.

    Weekly habits

    • Review RMM audit logs for new scripts/stored credentials.
    • Check cross-tenant and federation setting changes.
    • Pivot public vendor-incident reports against your register.

    Monthly habits

    • Recertify vendor access (people, rights, expiry).
    • Replay section 5.5 test events; validate rules.
    • Tabletop one vendor-compromise scenario with comms.
