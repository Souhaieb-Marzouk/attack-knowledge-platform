T1608.007 AI Result Poisoning

This is the newest technique in MITRE ATT&CK: it was added on 21 September 2026. Attackers use it to poison what an AI answer cites, not only what a search result ranks. This page explains the trick and shows defenders how to catch it.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    This section explains AI Result Poisoning in plain words, shows where it sits in the attack chain, and defines every term at first use. No AI background is needed.

    What the technique is

    AI Result Poisoning means manipulating public web content so that generative AI systems or AI-powered search engines retrieve, cite, or recommend attacker-controlled information. The attacker publishes pages that look ordinary. The AI then quotes them inside a direct answer to the user.

    Classic SEO poisoning fights for a high place in a list of links. This technique aims one step higher, at the synthesized answer itself. When the AI cites the attacker's page, the user sees one confident answer instead of ten choices.

    Why it matters

    An AI answer carries borrowed trust. It reads like advice from a careful colleague, so users click its links without checking. Attackers route that trust into phishing sites, fraudulent stores, malicious downloads, and impersonation pages.

    The attacker never touches the AI system: no model access, no system prompt, no application bug. That makes the attack cheap to run and invisible to AI-side security logs.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    This is a Resource Development technique (environment PRE, before any victim is involved). It sits after T1583 Acquire Infrastructure or T1584 Compromise Infrastructure, which give the attacker the web pages to poison with.

    It works next to two siblings. T1608.005 Link Target stages the page a phishing link points to, and the AI now recommends links much like a phishing mail does. T1608.006 SEO Poisoning is the search-engine version of the same idea.

    Typical outcomes are T1566.002 Spearphishing Link, T1204.001 Malicious Link, and T1204.002 User Execution when the user clicks, signs in, or installs. For developers there is a cousin risk, T1195.001 Compromise Software Dependencies; it appears later as one vector among several.

    New words, explained simply

    AI assistant
    A chat tool that answers questions in natural language, often with links. Coding helpers and general chatbots are the common cases.
    AI-powered search
    A search engine that shows one synthesized answer above the link list, with citations.
    RAG
    Retrieval-augmented generation: the AI fetches web pages at question time and uses their text in the answer. Some assistants use RAG; others search and summarize. The attacker does not care which.
    Citation
    The source link shown under an AI answer. A poisoned citation sends the user to attacker content.
    Impersonation page
    A fake copy of a real sign-in page or portal, built to collect credentials or payments.

    Good practice

    Treat every AI answer as a search result, not a security review. The defender goal on this page is simple: catch the moment an AI-recommended link lands on a young domain and asks the user for a file or a password.

    What happens

    Defender view

    Attacker Poison garden pages AI assistant / AI search Victim side Defender / SOC

    The same story in text (screen-reader and no-JS friendly)

    1. Step 1 — Pick the questions The attacker studies what users ask AI assistants: download invoice template, best PDF converter, company-specific how-tos. Common questions mean many future victims. Defender view: nothing is visible - so far the attacker only reads public prompts. Brand monitoring is the one early sensor you own.
    2. Step 2 — Build the poison garden The attacker publishes several ordinary-looking pages: a help article, a review site, and a fake comparison blog. All recommend one download or sign-in page. Defender view: nothing in victim logs - but every page needs a fresh domain and certificate, which Certificate Transparency records.
    3. Step 3 — Cross-link the sources The pages cite each other so AI retrieval sees several apparently independent confirmations. Structured data and mirrors widen the footprint. Defender view: more new certificates appear on young domains - a CT watchlist flags them in minutes.
    4. Step 4 — The AI answers An AI-powered search summary or chatbot retrieves the poisoned cluster and recommends the attacker's link. Defender view: no local signal yet, because question and answer never touch your network. Threat-intel researchers begin flagging such clusters publicly.
    5. Step 5 — The user clicks An employee asks the assistant at work and clicks the recommended link. The proxy records a referrer from the AI site into a 3-day-old domain. Defender view: the first strong local signal - alert on the AI-referrer plus young-domain join.
    6. Step 6 — The lure lands The user reaches an impersonation page and downloads a fake installer. The file arrives without a mark-of-the-web zone identifier. Defender view: an EDR file-creation event shows the installer in Downloads with no Zone.Identifier.
    7. Step 7 — Payload or phishing runs The installer drops an infostealer, or the user types credentials into the fake portal. The IdP flags a risky sign-in, or EDR sees stealer behavior. Defender view: detection fires - page now, hunt the full chain after.
    8. Step 8 — SOC joins the dots The analyst joins AI-referrer, young domain, download, and sign-in events. The domain is blocked, poison pages are reported to the AI provider, and a brand-monitoring rule goes live. Defender view: response plus feedback loop - the report helps the provider drop the citation for everyone.

    Trust transfer

    A citation makes the attacker's page feel vetted by the AI itself. Users skip the suspicion they still apply to search ads and cold email. The poison borrows the machine's credibility.

    Scale

    One poison garden feeds many assistants and many questions at once. The attacker builds once, and every future ask of the same question delivers the lure again.

    Attribution gap

    No model access means no AI-side logs for defenders to read. The attack exists only as web content - so the evidence lives in your proxy, endpoint, and identity telemetry instead.

    Note

    The simulation is a simplified teaching model. Real poison gardens mix dozens of pages, refresh content often, and imitate local prices and spellings. AI providers also change what they cite, so gardens need constant care.

    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section looks at poisoning through the eyes of an attacker. Knowing why and how they build poison gardens helps you predict what to look for. Everything here is described for defensive learning, and the lab exercise publishes nothing anywhere.

    Why AI answers attract attackers

    The prize is trust laundering: the attacker's page borrows the AI system's credibility the moment it gets cited. Users then skip the suspicion they still apply to search ads and cold mails.

    There is also freshness. AI indexes re-crawl the web often, so new poison pages can enter answers within days. No victim needs to be lured one by one; the assistant delivers the lure for free.

    How the staging works

    The run has three moves. First, publish: several ordinary-looking pages that all recommend one download or sign-in page. Second, cross-link: the pages cite each other, add structured data, and mirror content, so retrieval sees apparently independent confirmations.

    Third, measure: the attacker checks whether assistants and AI search actually cite the cluster, then adjusts wording until they do. Public research by Netcraft and by Lasso Security describes exactly this pattern.

    When attackers use it

    • During operation planning, once target brands and workflows are chosen.
    • After infrastructure is acquired or compromised (T1583 / T1584).
    • Before a phishing wave, so poisoned answers back up the mails.
    • Continuously, because citations decay when content grows stale.

    Where the poison is published

    • Ordinary-looking blogs and help-article farms.
    • Fake review and comparison sites that rank "best" options.
    • Q&A platforms, where user answers carry extra credibility.
    • Documentation mirrors that copy real product docs.

    Lab only — safe practice setup

    You can rehearse the defensive side without publishing anything. Ask an AI assistant or AI search about your own brand the way a user would, then record every source the answer cites. Publish nothing; touch no attacker infrastructure.

    citation_audit.txt — brand citation audit, no publication, no attacker contact
    # LAB ONLY - citation audit for your own brand, nothing is published
# 1. Pick the questions users really ask:
#      "acme supplier portal", "acme invoice download", "acme support number"
# 2. Ask each question to two different AI assistants and one AI search.
# 3. For every citation in every answer, record:
#      domain, domain age (whois or CT log), category, first seen date
# 4. Flag any domain younger than 30 days, or on a cheap TLD, for review.
# 5. Repeat monthly and diff the citation list - new young domains matter.
# Output: an allowlist of domains your AI answers may send people to.
# Feed that list into your proxy and DNS controls.

    The audit gives you a baseline: which citations are healthy, and which ones appeared overnight. The diff between audits is often more interesting than the list itself.

    Attacker mistakes you can catch

    Contradicting claims across mirrors: one page says version 2.4, another says 3.1. Thin citation diversity: all "independent" sources resolve to the same host or registrar. Young domains with no history: certificates and registrations only days old, with nothing before them.

    Where it applies

    Everything happens in the PRE environment on public platforms: blog hosts, review sites, Q&A pages, and doc mirrors. Your network is not touched until a user clicks a cited link - which is exactly when your best telemetry switches on.

    Cost of failure

    When a cluster is reported, AI providers can drop the citation and security vendors can sinkhole the domains. The attacker loses the whole garden at once, which is why defender reports do real damage.

    4

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders cannot watch the attacker's garden, but the attack still crosses two surfaces you own: your users' browsers and your monitoring around your own brands. This section shows what to collect and how to triage an alert fast.

    Surface 1: your users

    Four signals matter here. Proxy referrer chains: a click from an AI site into a young domain is the strongest single signal. DLP queries into AI sites: staff pasting sensitive text into public assistants deserve a policy decision. MOTW telemetry: downloads without a zone identifier skipped a browser safety mark. Sign-in risk: a risky IdP event shortly after a young-domain visit ties the chain together.

    Surface 2: your infrastructure

    Watch the outside, not only the inside. Certificate Transparency monitoring shows new certificates on domains that imitate your brands. Brand monitoring catches fake help pages and lookalike portals early. Young-domain feeds flag registrations only days old. Feed all three into the SIEM as enrichment.

    Artifacts poisoning leaves

    • A proxy record with an AI-site referrer and a young destination domain.
    • An installer in Downloads with a missing Zone.Identifier ADS.
    • A lookalike sign-in host that reuses your brand words.
    • A CT log entry for a fresh certificate on an imitation domain.
    • A burst of external fetches that mirror your help-center content.

    Where to look

    Ship proxy logs with referrer fields, EDR file events, and IdP sign-in logs to the SIEM. Add a domain-age lookup and a CT watchlist for your brand words. Test that alerts fire for office and VPN users, not only for servers.

    Short triage flow for a suspected poisoned citation

    1. 1 — Receive the signal An AI-referrer alert, a young-domain hit, or a risky sign-in arrives in the queue.
    2. 2 — Pull the referrer chain Confirm which AI site sent the click and what question it answered. Screenshot the answer for the report.
    3. 3 — Score the destination Check domain age, certificate history, and reputation feeds. Young plus imitation means hostile until proven otherwise.
    4. 4 — Search org-wide Find every user who visited the domain, downloaded from it, or signed in to it in the last 7 days.
    5. 5 — Contain the users Isolate hosts that ran a download, reset credentials for anyone who typed them, and revoke active sessions.
    6. 6 — Block and report Block the domain at proxy and DNS, then report the poison pages to the AI provider and your brand-protection pipeline.

    Good practice

    Measure the time from "AI-referrer alert" to "credential reset decision". If your pipeline answers within 30 minutes, a relayed session usually dies before the attacker can spend it.

    5.1Regulation mapping

    The poisoning happens on public platforms, but regulators still expect controls that catch its effects: risk-based security measures, monitoring, user training, and fast incident reporting. The table below lists the specific controls auditors look for and the evidence to keep ready.

    Note

    No regulator demands that you detect poisoning on the attacker's platform. They demand risk-based measures, monitoring, training, and fast reporting. Frame your program in those words during audits.

    5.2Threat intelligence

    T1608.007 is brand new - MITRE added it on 21 September 2026 - so the best intelligence comes from active researchers, not from long-running actor dossiers. This section lists sources worth feeding into your program and how to grade confidence before you block something.

    Core sources

    • MITRE ATT&CK — re-check the technique page often; it is new and will gain examples and references over time.
    • Vendor research — Netcraft on AI summaries citing dangerous sites; Lasso Security's "AI engine poisoning" demos; academic slopsquatting studies from 2025.
    • Web-reputation feeds — OpenPhish, URLhaus, and abuse.ch ThreatFox flag young phishing and malware domains fast.
    • Certificate Transparency watchlists — alert on new certificates for domains imitating your brands.
    • Brand monitoring — catch fake help pages, lookalike portals, and copied documentation early.
    • ISACs — sector sharing communities pass along poisoned clusters and domains seen against peers.

    Collection, analysis, application

    Collect by following two or three researchers, subscribing to reputation feeds, and keeping your CT and brand watchlists current. Analyse by joining signals: an AI-site referrer plus a young domain plus a download is a strong pattern before anyone confirms malware.

    Apply in three ways. First, preventive controls: DNS and proxy categories, plus phishing-resistant MFA. Second, detection content: the rules in 5.5. Third, a short intel note that tells staff which citation lures aim at your brand.

    Record every decision. When a cluster you flagged early appears in a researcher write-up, that feedback loop proves the program works.

    ConfidenceMeaningTypical AI-poisoning exampleSafe action
    HIGHThe certificate, domain, or page is verified hostile by researchers or your own checks.Two firms name the same cluster; the domain is 3 days old and mirrors your help center.Block the domains org-wide, hunt every visit and download, report the cluster to the AI provider.
    MEDIUMThe poisoning pattern is observed but hostility is not yet confirmed.AI-site referrer into an uncategorized domain 9 days old, followed by an installer download.Alert on the chain, inspect the file offline, and ask the user what they were trying to solve.

    Actor status

    MITRE lists NO named groups for T1608.007 yet (checked 20 October 2026). The technique is too new, and published reporting describes methods, not attribution. Treat any actor claim you see as vendor-attributed until MITRE or two independent firms confirm it.

    5.3Threats ranking and categorization

    The five threats below are the realistic ways AI result poisoning turns into harm for an organization. Each has a severity, the platform categories it touches, what must be true before it can happen, its impact, and why it was ranked here. Severity reflects combined likelihood and business damage for a typical mid-size organization.

    IDThreatSeverityPlatformPreconditionsImpactWhy ranked here
    TH-01 AI-recommended impersonation page harvests credentials — the assistant cites a fake portal; the employee signs in and hands over credentials. CRITICAL EndpointIdentity A poisoned cluster names a lookalike sign-in page, and the affected accounts lack phishing-resistant MFA. Stolen SSO credentials, relayed sessions, account takeover. The user path is a single trusted click, and credential theft is the direct goal. It stays Critical until MFA is phishing-resistant.
    TH-02 AI-recommended fake installer drops an infostealer — the cited "official download" installs malware on a workstation. HIGH Endpoint The user runs the downloaded installer; mark-of-the-web warnings are absent or ignored. Password and cookie theft, session hijack, a foothold on the endpoint. Silent and fast, but it needs a download plus execution, which MOTW and allowlist controls can interrupt.
    TH-03 AI-recommended malicious package in the developer workflow — the slopsquatting vector: the assistant names a fresh package that attackers published. HIGH CloudEndpoint Developers ask assistants for packages, and install steps run without an allowlist. Stolen tokens and source code, poisoned builds, cloud pivots. Documented in 2025 academic research, so the vector is proven; its scope is narrower, which keeps it below the first two.
    TH-04 Poisoned cluster drives traffic to fraudulent stores — AI answers cite fake shops that imitate your brand or sell nothing. MEDIUM Network A cluster of fake review pages survives long enough to be cited, and users reach checkout. Payment fraud, brand damage, support load from cheated customers. Real money flows to the attacker, but the harm lands mostly on external customers, so internal impact stays limited.
    TH-05 Misinformation about the organization quoted by assistants — fabricated claims about your company enter AI answers. LOW NetworkReputation The attacker publishes fabricated claims that assistants retrieve and repeat. Customer confusion, press questions, slow trust erosion. No direct system compromise, so it ranks low today; it rises toward Medium in regulated or media-sensitive sectors.

    Note

    Ratings are starting points. Adjust them to your sector: for a company whose staff sign in to many niche portals, TH-01 is Critical with a capital C. For a consumer brand with public stores, TH-04 moves up.

    R3 — Sigma: installer written to Downloads and executed without mark-of-the-web

    sigma — t1608007_r3_downloads_no_motw.yml (file_event, windows)
    title: Installer Written to Downloads Folder Without Mark-of-the-Web
id: 6621044a-c083-44fb-9864-ddfb4f584b0d
status: stable
description: >
  Detects an executable installer created in a user Downloads folder. Browsers
  normally add a mark-of-the-web zone identifier to downloaded files; AI-lured

references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1608/007/

author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-20

  selection_ext:
    TargetFilename|endswith:

      - '.msi'

    TargetFilename|endswith: ':Zone.Identifier'
  condition: selection_dir and selection_ext and not filter_motw
fields:
  - TargetFilename
  - Image
  - User
  - Computer
falsepositives:

  - attack.execution

    SPL mapping note: the MOTW check joins the installer write with the matching :Zone.Identifier ADS write, then looks for execution. The Sigma rule alone fires on every Downloads-installer write. Treat it as a queue-saver, not the full story.

    splunk — R3 installer without MOTW, joined with execution
    index=endpoint sourcetype="WinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" EventCode=11
| search TargetFilename IN ("*\\Downloads\\*.exe", "*\\Downloads\\*.msi", "*\\Downloads\\*.scr")
| eval is_motw=if(like(TargetFilename,"%:Zone.Identifier"),1,0)
| eval base=if(is_motw=1, substr(TargetFilename,1,len(TargetFilename)-16), TargetFilename)
| stats sum(is_motw) AS motw_writes, values(User) AS users,
        earliest(_time) AS first_seen by Computer, base
| where motw_writes=0
| join type=left max=0 Computer, base
    [ search index=endpoint sourcetype="WinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" EventCode=1
      | rename Image AS base | fields Computer base CommandLine ]
| table first_seen, Computer, users, base, CommandLine
| sort - first_seen

    Field assumptions: Sysmon EventCode 11 with TargetFilename and EventCode 1 with Image; :Zone.Identifier is 16 characters. False positives: MOTW disabled by policy, or installs pushed by software deployment tools. Tuning: exclude your deployment tool paths, and raise the level to High when the same host had an R1 or R2 hit the same day.

    Real-World Case Study

    T1608.007 is weeks old, so no single breach report tells the full story yet. The scenario below is a clearly labeled composite, grounded in public research. Netcraft found AI summaries citing sites that host dangerous downloads. Lasso Security showed fabricated content ending up inside chatbot answers. Academic studies in 2025 measured AI-recommended malicious packages. Any resemblance to one specific company is coincidental.

    Background (composite scenario)

    A mid-size services company buys from many suppliers. Employees routinely ask an AI assistant for help finding internal tools, and the finance team uses a supplier portal daily. Phishing-resistant MFA covers email, but not every niche portal. Proxy logs were kept in full, yet the referrer field had never been used in a detection.

    What is documented vs. composite

    Documented: AI summaries citing sites that host dangerous downloads (Netcraft); fabricated content ingested into chatbot answers (Lasso Security); attackers registering names that AI tools invent (2025 slopsquatting studies). Composite: the company, the people, the times, and the exact chain below. The timeline is a reconstruction from typical telemetry.

    Attack chain (reconstructed)

    • The attacker registers a domain one hyphen away from the real supplier portal and copies its sign-in page.
    • They publish a help article, two review pages, and a comparison blog that all link the fake portal.
    • The pages cross-link each other and add product markup, so retrieval sees several "independent" sources.
    • An employee asks the company's AI assistant where to find the supplier portal. The answer cites the poisoned cluster.
    • The employee clicks, signs in on the fake portal, and the credentials are relayed to the real site in real time.
    • The MFA push is denied by the real user; the attacker falls back to a relayed session token, and the IdP flags a risky sign-in.

    Tools in the room

    Defender side: proxy logs with referrer fields, EDR on laptops, IdP risk events, a domain-age lookup, and a CT watchlist for brand words. Attacker side, as reconstructed: a page-clone kit, cheap domains, and free hosting for the mirror pages.

    Team response

    One incident channel joined SOC, identity, and network teams. The analyst wrote one page for staff: "find the supplier portal through the bookmarks bar, not through a chatbot". The helpdesk received the lookalike domain list.

    Detection moment

    Two weak signals became one strong alert: an AI-site referrer into a young domain, then a risky sign-in from a new device 29 minutes later. Either alone was a maybe. The join in rule R4 made the call obvious.

    Containment, eradication, recovery

    Containment took under an hour: domain blocked, credentials reset, sessions revoked, and the employee's laptop checked for downloads. Eradication found nothing more - no malware ran in this path. Recovery added phishing-resistant MFA to the supplier portal and a category challenge for young uncategorized domains.

    Impact and communication

    Impact stayed limited to analyst hours: about 30 staff-hours across SOC, identity, and the helpdesk. No data left the company, and no payment was made. Leadership received one daily paragraph with three numbers: users exposed, credentials reset, controls shipped.

    Lessons learned

    • An AI answer behaves like a phishing mail you never scanned: give its links the same scrutiny.
    • Referrer fields are free detection fuel - log them, index them, and alert on the AI-site join.
    • One employee clicking is a user event; several clicking is a campaign. Hunt after every confirmed citation.
    • Reporting the cluster to the AI provider helps every other customer, so always make that report.
    • Phishing-resistant MFA on niche portals matters as much as on email.
    • A CT watchlist on brand words gave the earliest warning, days before the first click.

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop an attacker from publishing poison. You can make sure a poisoned citation runs into hard checks the moment it touches your users. The controls below are grouped by layer, with a home-user card and a priority table at the end.

    Official ATT&CK mitigation

    ATT&CK lists exactly one mitigation for T1608.007: M1056 Pre-Compromise. Staging happens on attacker-side platforms, so you cannot prevent it directly — your controls harden the victim side and shorten the time between staging and abuse.

    Egress, DNS, and web categories

    Challenge young uncategorized domains at proxy and DNS, and keep AI services on a clean, logged category so referrer data stays visible. A new-domain challenge with a fast exception path stops most lure traffic before a page loads.

    Phishing-resistant MFA

    Roll out FIDO2 keys or passkeys, starting with portals that staff reach through search and AI answers. Relay attacks fail when the sign-in is bound to the real site, so this single control defuses TH-01.

    Browser and download controls

    Keep mark-of-the-web warnings on, block executable downloads from uncategorized domains, and allowlist installers for roles that need them. Browser warnings plus a signed-installer policy catch the fake "official download".

    Brand, CT, and takedown

    Run a Certificate Transparency watchlist on your brand words and a brand feed for fake portals. Keep a takedown pipeline ready: registrar abuse contacts, hosting reports, and AI-provider reports all move faster when prepared.

    User guidance on AI answers

    Teach one line: an AI answer is a search result, not a security review. Show how citations can be planted, and give staff a safe path to the portals they actually need - bookmarks beat asking a chatbot.

    SOC feedback loop

    When you confirm a poisoned cluster, report the pages to the AI provider or search engine, not only to the registrar. Dropping the citation protects every other customer and shrinks the attacker's return on the garden.

    Home and hobbyist advice

    When an AI answer hands you a download or a sign-in link, open the real site by typing its address yourself. Check the domain spelling letter by letter, and let browser safety warnings speak. Never turn off mark-of-the-web warnings, and think twice before paying a store you reached through an AI recommendation.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    These are the failures detection teams actually make around AI result poisoning. Each card shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from coming back.

    1. Treating AI answers as vetted sources

    Symptom
    Links from AI answers open freely; nobody records where answers send people.
    Root cause
    The citation format reads like editorial care, so the link inherits unearned trust.
    Fix
    Add AI-referrer and young-domain alerting (rule R1) and say why in the policy.
    Prevention
    Training repeats one line: an AI answer is a search result, not a security review.

    2. Ignoring referrer fields in proxy logs

    Symptom
    The proxy shows the download, but nobody can say what led the user there.
    Root cause
    The referrer field is logged but never indexed or used in rules.
    Fix
    Index referrer as a first-class field and build the R1 and R2 joins on it.
    Prevention
    Referrer coverage appears in the logging matrix with named owners.

    3. No domain-age enrichment

    Symptom
    Alerts fire on obviously malicious domains only after reputation feeds catch up.
    Root cause
    Nobody feeds whois or CT data into the SIEM as a lookup.
    Fix
    Stand up a daily young-domain lookup and use it in rules R1, R4, and R6.
    Prevention
    The lookup freshness is monitored like any other data pipeline.

    4. Hunting only after malware fires

    Symptom
    The infostealer is the first hint that a poisoned citation existed at all.
    Root cause
    The team waits for endpoint verdicts instead of joining weak web signals.
    Fix
    Run weekly hunts for AI-referrer hits, young domains, and odd installer paths.
    Prevention
    The hunt hypothesis list includes poisoned citations by name.

    5. Forgetting niche portals in MFA rollout

    Symptom
    Email is hardened, yet a supplier portal account is taken over.
    Root cause
    Phishing-resistant MFA stopped at the top ten apps.
    Fix
    Rank portals by exposure through search and AI answers; roll out FIDO2 or passkeys in that order.
    Prevention
    New portals cannot go live without an MFA tier decision.

    6. Blocking AI services entirely

    Symptom
    Staff move to personal phones, and visibility drops to zero.
    Root cause
    AI sites were blocked wholesale after one scary demo.
    Fix
    Allow AI services on a clean logged category instead, and alert on the referrer joins.
    Prevention
    Category changes require a SOC review of the detection impact.

    7. No CT or brand watchlist

    Symptom
    Lookalike portals are found by staff, not by monitoring.
    Root cause
    Nobody owns brand-word monitoring as a detection source.
    Fix
    Watch CT for brand-word certificates and alert on young registrations.
    Prevention
    The watchlist is reviewed monthly and after every brand or product change.

    8. Reporting domains but never the citation

    Symptom
    The domain dies, yet the AI answer keeps citing a mirror for weeks.
    Root cause
    Reports go only to the registrar; the AI provider never hears about it.
    Fix
    Report poisoned pages to the AI provider or search engine every time.
    Prevention
    The incident checklist lists the provider report as a required step.

    9. Assuming search defenses cover AI answers

    Symptom
    SEO-era controls pass review while AI-answer lures sail through.
    Root cause
    The team treats AI answers as "just search results" in the threat model.
    Fix
    Add AI citation lures to the threat model and map them to the 5.3 rankings.
    Prevention
    Annual threat-model reviews ask "what do our AI answers cite?".

    10. No path for "I clicked a chatbot link" reports

    Symptom
    Users stay silent after odd clicks, so the SOC loses its earliest sensor.
    Root cause
    No obvious channel exists, so staff wait to see if something breaks.
    Fix
    Publish a one-click report button and a no-blame rule for early reports.
    Prevention
    Drill the path twice a year with a fake citation lure.

    11. One-off training without real citation examples

    Symptom
    Staff can define phishing but still trust a confident AI answer.
    Root cause
    Training predates AI answers and lacks live examples.
    Fix
    Refresh training with real poisoned-answer screenshots and the R1 story.
    Prevention
    Training content is reviewed when ATT&CK updates this technique.

    12. No rule owner and no replay tests

    Symptom
    Rules 1-7 fire once in a demo and then rot untuned for months.
    Root cause
    Detection content ships without owners or test events.
    Fix
    Assign owners and replay the 5.4 events through every rule monthly.
    Prevention
    The detection repo blocks rules that lack an owner and a test case.

    Daily habits

    • Review AI-referrer and young-domain alerts from rule R1.
    • Check blocked or challenged downloads from rule R2.
    • Skim one researcher feed for new poisoned-cluster write-ups.

    Weekly habits

    • Walk one citation alert through the full triage flow.
    • Review CT watchlist hits on brand-word domains.
    • Confirm the young-domain and fresh-package lookups refreshed all week.

    Monthly habits

    • Re-run the brand citation audit and diff the citation list.
    • Replay the 5.4 test events against every rule on this page.
    • Report program metrics to leadership, including takedown turnaround.

    Top detections (from 5.5)

    • AI assistant referrer fetches content from a young domain (proxy, High)
    • Executable download within 5 minutes of an AI-site session (proxy, High)
    • Installer written to Downloads without mark-of-the-web (endpoint, Medium)
    • Risky sign-in shortly after a young-domain visit (identity, High)
    • Fresh low-age package installed after an AI recommendation (proxy, High)
    • Credential POST to a lookalike domain with AI-referrer history (proxy, High)
    • Burst of external fetches mirroring help-center content (webserver, Medium)

    Response checklist when a poisoned citation is confirmed

    • Confirm the referrer chain and screenshot the AI answer for the report.
    • Score the destination domain: age, certificate history, reputation feeds.
    • Search org-wide for visits, downloads, and sign-ins tied to the domain in the last 7 days.
    • Isolate hosts that ran a download; reset credentials for anyone who typed them; revoke sessions.
    • Block the domain at proxy and DNS and add it to your denylists.
    • Report the poison pages to the AI provider or search engine and to your brand pipeline.
    • Brief the affected team in one line: reach portals through bookmarks, not chatbots.
