T1608.005 Link Target

A staged page is useless until someone arrives. Attackers place links in email, chat, forums, and social media so that victims walk into the trap by themselves. Understanding link staging explains most of what a SOC sees every single day.

Tactic: Resource Development Platform: PRE Parent: T1608 Created: 17 March 2021 ATT&CK data: 1.4 (May 2026) Detection difficulty: Medium Guide: v2.0
The bridgeLinks connect attacker infrastructure to real victims. Without a placed link, the most convincing staged page stays empty forever.
Rotated fastLink destinations change hourly to survive blocklists. Stopping one domain barely hurts an operation that owns twenty more.

    1

    Overview

    This section explains what Link Target means in plain words. It shows why a simple URL is the busiest bridge in modern attacks. It also places the sub-technique between attacker preparation and a real compromise.

    What the technique is

    Link Target means the attacker places links to staged content where future victims will click them. The places: email, SMS text messages, social media direct messages, forum posts, blog comments, contact forms, and even QR codes. The staged content is usually a fake sign-in page, a payload download, or both. The link is the delivery bridge.

    Think of it as a scammer printing a signpost that points to their fake shop and planting it on the busiest street in town. The shop was staged earlier (that is the parent technique, T1608). The signpost is this sub-technique. Nothing bad has happened yet, but everything is now one click away.

    Why attackers prefer links

    A link is cheaper and safer for the attacker than an attachment. Mail scanners can inspect a file deeply, but a URL is just a short string; the dangerous content lives on a server the scanner cannot see. The destination can also change after delivery, so a link sent on Monday can point to a fresh page on Tuesday.

    Links also make testing cheap. Attackers send the same lure with three different display texts, watch which one gets clicks, and drop the losers. That A/B testing habit is why your users see endless small variations of the same trap.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    This sub-technique belongs to Resource Development (Tactic PRE), because the links are placed before any known victim is involved. It is the last staging step: after it comes Initial Access. The usual hand-offs look like this:

    Good practice

    Link staging is the one Resource Development sub-technique your sensors see directly. Unlike certificate logs or passive DNS, the click happens on your network with a named user attached. Teams that join mail verdicts, proxy clicks, and identity sign-ins catch link attacks in minutes, not days.

    Defender view

    Attacker Attacker infrastructure Redirect service Victim side Defender / SOC

    The same story in text (screen-reader and no-JS friendly)

    1. Step 1 — Stage the credential page On a domain registered two days earlier, the attacker builds a convincing copy of the company sign-in page. A free TLS certificate is added. Defender view: the issuance appears in public Certificate Transparency logs within minutes — the same early alarm used against T1608.003 certificate staging.
    2. Step 2 — Wire the redirect chain Two throwaway redirect domains are configured to forward to the staged page. If one gets blocked, only the cheap outer layer dies. Defender view: passive DNS shows both redirect domains resolving to the same host that serves the staged page.
    3. Step 3 — Hijack a real thread Using a stolen mailbox, the attacker replies inside a genuine vendor thread and adds the redirect link, riding on months of built trust. Defender view: the send leaves through the compromised account itself, so it passes every sender check — nearly invisible before the click.
    4. Step 4 — Mass send the same lure The identical lure also goes to a purchased address list, one recipient per message. Defender view: the mail gateway flags single-recipient sends whose links point to domains younger than a week, and rewrites the URLs for time-of-click tracking.
    5. Step 5 — Victim clicks the link The employee clicks. The link hops through both redirect domains before landing on the staged sign-in page. Defender view: click telemetry records the user, the time, and the full redirect chain — the map needed for blocking later.
    6. Step 6 — Credentials are stolen The employee types the password into the fake page. The attacker signs in from another country within minutes. Defender view: the identity provider logs an impossible-travel sign-in: two countries, half an hour apart.
    7. Step 7 — Hide the tracks The attacker adds a hidden inbox rule that moves future security messages to the deleted folder, so the victim sees nothing. Defender view: the mailbox audit log records the new rule — one of the most reliable post-phishing signals you have.
    8. Step 8 — SOC responds The SOC resets the password, revokes sessions, removes the rule, blocks the whole chain, and warns the real thread partner. Defender view: historical hunting finds one more clicker who never typed anything — no harm there, but now the team knows and can watch that account.

    Why the redirect layer exists

    Redirect domains are disposable armor. Mail gateways and proxies block fast, so attackers expect to lose outer links daily. A two-hop chain means the block of hop one does not reveal or protect hop two, and the staged page can outlive dozens of blocked front doors.

    Why the thread hijack matters

    A reply inside a real thread carries real trust, real history, and often passes SPF, DKIM, and DMARC because the true mailbox sent it. Filters that judge senders see a clean message. Only content and behavior — an unexpected link, an odd request — remain as signals.

    Why the inbox rule matters

    After a takeover, attackers want silence. A rule that deletes security mail keeps the victim unaware while fraud runs for days. Mailbox audit logs record rule creation with exact parameters, which makes this both a strong detection and a clean indicator of compromise.

    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section looks at link staging through the eyes of an attacker. Understanding why links win, where they are planted, and what a working chain needs helps you predict what to look for. Everything here is described for defensive learning; the lab exercise is harmless and runs only against safe example links.

    Why links beat attachments

    An attachment arrives with its full payload exposed: sandbox it, hash it, disassemble it, block it. A link arrives as a short string of text. The mail scanner sees the URL and maybe fetches the page once, while the real content waits on a server that can check who is asking and serve different answers.

    Rotation makes links stronger still. When a URL is blocked or reported, the attacker swaps the destination in minutes, so yesterday's blocklist does not protect today's click. Attachments cannot change after delivery; links can.

    Where the links are placed

    • Email — the classic channel: mass phishing, targeted spearphishing, and replies inside hijacked threads.
    • SMS text messages — smishing lures about parcels, bank alerts, and MFA requests, often with shortener links.
    • Social media and chat — direct messages on professional networks with "document" links (the Operation Dream Job pattern).
    • Forums and comments — posts in communities the target industry reads, often on compromised or aged accounts.
    • Contact forms and QR codes — form replies that push a link to staff inboxes, and printed or on-screen QR codes that hide the URL entirely.

    What an attacker needs first

    Link staging has two prerequisites. The first is a staged destination: a credential page, a payload host, or a redirect chain, all prepared under T1608 (usually .001, .003, or a rented kit). The second is a distribution channel: a way to place links in front of the right people.

    Channels range from free to stolen. Purchased address lists and spam services give volume. Hijacked mailboxes and social accounts give trust. Botnets give scale. Or the attacker simply registers a fresh account on the platform where the targets gather. The channel choice decides how much the clicks will be worth.

    Variations defenders see

    • Open-redirect abuse — the link starts on a legitimate site that forwards to wherever its parameters point, borrowing that site's reputation.
    • Shortener chains — two or three link-shortening services stacked so the chain must be walked step by step.
    • Calendar-invite lures — meeting invites whose notes hold the link; some clients render notes before any click.
    • QR-code payloads — the link hides in an image, which dodges text-based URL scanning and shifts the click to phones.

    Lab only — safe URL-chain exercise (no phishing, no kit)

    You can train the core defensive skill — comparing display text with the real destination — using three safe links and a public URL-unwrapping service. Do this from your own browser, on links you trust, and never with a lure you did not build yourself. This exercise touches no attacker infrastructure and harvests nothing.

    lab_link_check.md — display text vs destination, three safe examples
    # LAB ONLY - compare display text and true destination on SAFE links.
# Pick links you know are harmless. Nothing is sent, nothing is collected.

# Step 1 - choose three safe examples, for example:
#   a) a long Wikipedia article URL with query parameters
#   b) a news-site link that carries tracking parameters (utm_source=...)
#   c) a public shortener link YOU create that points to example.com

# Step 2 - on a blank test page, write each link with friendly display text
#          that does NOT match the target, for example:
#     a) display: "internal wiki"      target: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phishing
#     b) display: "this week's news"   target: news.example.com/article?id=7&utm_source=lab
#     c) display: "example home page"  target: (shortener URL you made)

# Step 3 - unwrap each: hover to read the browser status bar, or paste the
#          shortener URL into a reputable URL-expander service to see hop 1.

# Step 4 - for each link, write down three lines:
#     display host | final target host | do they disagree? (yes/no)
#     hops observed: ____

# Expected result: you practiced the exact comparison your mail gateway and
# proxy do automatically - and you can now explain why mismatch is the
# strongest single signal for staged links (T1608.005).

    Attacker mistakes you can catch

    Static shorteners that never rotate burn within a day. Operations that skip rotation get their whole chain blocked at once. Generic lures aimed at a targeted organization feel wrong to trained staff. And test clicks from hosting IP ranges leave early footprints in new-domain telemetry.

    Chain games in detail

    An open redirect on a trusted site is worth more than ten fresh domains, because the first hop carries reputation. Shortener chains add work for defenders: each hop needs a separate lookup. Your proxy should record the full chain; if it records only the first hop, ask for that feature.

    Cost of failure

    When a chain is exposed, defenders block every hop, report domains to registrars, and add lure wording to training. The attacker loses staging work and must rebuild. That is why fast reporting — minutes, not days — turns one click into an attacker loss instead of a breach.

    4

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders cannot see the attacker placing links, but the moment before, during, and after the click leaves rich traces across four log families. This section shows what each family records, which artifacts matter, and a six-step triage flow you can run the same hour a lure appears.

    Mail security controls

    Modern mail gateways rewrite every URL in inbound mail to point at a tracking service. This enables time-of-click defense: when the user clicks, the gateway checks the destination at that moment and blocks pages flagged as phishing. It also records the display text versus target pair for every link.

    Check your gateway actually rewrites (many tenants disable it by mistake), keep the verdict logs for at least 90 days, and alert on display-text/host mismatches and young domains. Sender-side posture still matters: enforce SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, and read the DMARC reports to find lookalike senders early.

    Beyond mail: proxy, identity, mailbox audit

    Three more families complete the picture. Proxy click telemetry records who clicked, when, and through which redirect chain. Identity-provider sign-in logs show where accounts are really used — impossible travel and new devices betray relayed credentials. Mailbox audit logs show what happens after a takeover, above all new inbox rules.

    None of these alone is proof. The winning detections join them: a flagged click followed by a risky sign-in, or a risky sign-in followed by a new inbox rule. Sections 5.4 and 5.5 give you the events and the rules.

    Artifacts a staged link leaves

    • Rewritten URLs in mail bodies, with per-click verdict events afterwards.
    • Click events with user, time, and the full redirect chain, including shorteners and open redirects.
    • Impossible-travel or new-country sign-ins shortly after a click, often requesting persistent sessions.
    • Unexpected inbox rules — especially rules hiding security, quarantine, or MFA messages.
    • Replies to a hijacked thread that contain a link where none was expected.

    Where to look, in order

    Start with the mail gateway URL verdict feed: single-recipient sends, mismatched display text, and sub-7-day domain ages. Continue with proxy clicks to the same indicators. Then check the identity provider for risky sign-ins by the clickers. Finally read the mailbox audit trail for rules and forwarding changes.

    Keep domain-age enrichment inside your pipeline. A click log without an age column forces an analyst to do the lookup by hand, and hand lookups do not happen at 3 a.m.

    Short triage flow for a suspected staged link

    1. 1 — Verify the link Read the rewritten URL and the display text. Look up domain age, redirect chain, and certificate history from outside the network. Never open the destination from a corporate device.
    2. 2 — Detonate offline Let your sandbox or a reputable URL-scanning service fetch the page. Note whether it asks for credentials, offers a download, or checks the visitor before deciding.
    3. 3 — List the clickers Search proxy and mail telemetry for everyone who clicked any hop of the chain in the last 30 days. Treat every clicker as exposed until proven otherwise.
    4. 4 — Check sign-ins and rules For each clicker, review identity-provider sign-ins since the click and mailbox audit logs for new rules, forwarding, or delegate grants.
    5. 5 — Reset and revoke For anyone who typed credentials: reset the password, revoke sessions and tokens, remove malicious rules, and re-register MFA if the attacker changed it.
    6. 6 — Block and notify Block every domain in the chain at proxy, DNS, and mail. Report to your blocklist feed and ISAC, and warn the real thread partner that their conversation was abused.

    Good practice

    Measure click-to-containment time. If your pipeline joins a flagged click to sign-in risk in under 15 minutes and your helpdesk can reset within the hour, the attacker's window closes before fraud usually starts. Publish that number to leadership — it improves every quarter it is watched.

The list: mail security with click-time checks, awareness training that covers link lures, monitoring of sign-ins and mailbox changes, and a working report channel.     Note

    No regulator demands that you detect the attacker placing the link. They demand that you operate the mail defenses, training, and monitoring that make the link visible, reportable, and blockable on your side. Frame your link-staging program in those words during audits.

    5.2Threat intelligence

    Link staging is the best-covered part of the Resource Development tactic: vendors report it constantly because it is what victims actually experience. This section lists the sources worth feeding into your program, how to grade confidence before you block something, and the actor groups publicly linked to link-based staging.

    Core sources

    • MITRE ATT&CK — the shared language for link staging (T1608.005) and the techniques that follow it (T1566.002, T1204.001, T1078).
    • Vendor reporting — Microsoft, Red Canary, ESET, Proofpoint, and others publish link-based campaign reports with indicators and lure wording.
    • URL scanning services — public detonation platforms show the final page, the redirect chain, and screenshots for reported links.
    • OSINT feeds — abuse.ch URLhaus and ThreatFox list staged payload links; newly-observed-domain feeds flag young destinations.
    • Mail and click telemetry — your own gateway verdicts and proxy clicks show which staged links actually reached your users.
    • ISACs and peer sharing — sector communities exchange hijacked-thread patterns and blocked chains, often hours before public reports.

    Collection, analysis, application

    Collect by keeping two or three feeds, reading DMARC reports for lookalike senders, and archiving every mail-gateway verdict. Analyse by joining indicators: a young domain plus a mismatched display text plus a single recipient is a strong link-staging pattern even before any scan confirms the page.

    Apply the intelligence in three ways. Set preventive blocks for high-confidence chains. Build detection content from the Sigma rules in section 5.5. Write short intel notes that tell leadership which lure families target your sector this month.

    Grade everything. Blocking on one weak report risks cutting off a real partner domain; the confidence model below keeps that decision honest.

    ConfidenceMeaningTypical staged-link exampleSafe action
    HIGHMultiple independent sources agree, or you observed the full chain and page yourself.Gateway flags mismatch, sandbox confirms a credential page, and the domain is 2 days old.Block the chain at mail, proxy, and DNS; reset exposed users; hunt for earlier clicks.
    MEDIUMOne reliable source or several weak signals combined.Single-recipient send with a young-domain URL, page content not yet confirmed.Alert on any internal click, watchlist the domain, enrich passively; no broad block yet.
    LOWSingle unverified report or weak automated classification.A user report of "looks weird" with no technical confirmation.Keep as enrichment and training input; never auto-block on low confidence.

    Actors and campaigns publicly linked to link staging

    Silent Librarian
    Cloned university and library login pages, staged them, and wrapped them in many URL shorteners for credential-harvesting campaigns (Secureworks COBALT DICKENS 2019, Proofpoint TA407 2019, Malwarebytes 2020). The best-documented group MITRE lists under this technique.
    FIN7
    Created fake links that redirected to an attacker-controlled Dropbox folder holding a malicious executable (BlackBerry, 2024). Documented by MITRE under this technique.
    LuminousMoth
    Used links to Dropbox-hosted files in spear-phishing against Southeast Asian targets (Kaspersky, 2021). Documented by MITRE under this technique.
    Operation AkaiRyū (MirrorFace)
    Used links directing victims to malicious files hosted on OneDrive during operations against Japanese organizations (Trend Micro and ESET, 2024–2025). Documented by MITRE as a campaign.
    NIMBUS MANTICORE
    Registered typosquatted domains and pre-shared credentials together with links to spoofed login pages that serve payloads (Check Point, 2025–2026). Documented by MITRE under this technique.
    Qakbot-era thread hijacks and MFA-relay crews
    Threat activity that distributed the Qakbot loader hijacked real email threads with staged payload links (Microsoft, Red Canary, 2021–2022), and smishing crews now stage MFA-relay pages behind short links. Vendor-documented context.

    Confidence discussion

    Actor attribution for a single lure is usually LOW confidence: kits and address lists are shared, so wording alone proves little. Campaign-level linkage (same kit fingerprint, same redirect host, same send window) reaches MEDIUM. HIGH confidence belongs to what you observed yourself: your own click telemetry plus your own detonation of the page. Act boldly on the latter, carefully on the former.

    5.3Threats ranking and categorization

    The five threats below are the realistic ways a placed link turns into harm for an organization. Each has a severity, the platforms it touches, what must be true before it can happen, its impact, and why the severity was chosen. Severity reflects combined likelihood and business damage for a typical mid-size organization.

    IDThreatSeverityPlatformPreconditionsImpactJustification
    TH-01 Staged credential-harvest link leading to account takeover — a link to a fake sign-in page whose stolen passwords are replayed immediately. CRITICAL IdentityEmailNetwork Staged credential page exists; attacker has a channel (hijacked thread, list, or platform account) and a relay for live MFA codes. Full mailbox access, invoice fraud, lateral movement into the wider tenant. Account takeover from one click is the single most common serious outcome of link staging; recovery costs and fraud risk are high even when MFA exists.
    TH-02 Link directly to a payload download — the destination serves an archive or script instead of a login form, and the user runs it. HIGH EndpointEmailNetwork Payload staged on a host or trusted platform; user must also execute the downloaded file (user execution). Malware installation, info-stealer theft, ransomware entry. Very common in commodity eCrime; needs a second user action, which lowers success rates, but the payload impact justifies High.
    TH-03 Open-redirect and shortener chains hiding the destination — links start on legitimate domains and hop to staged content. MEDIUM NetworkEmail Attacker finds an unpatched open redirect or stacks public shorteners on top of the staged page. Reputation laundering that defeats URL filtering and user inspection alike. An enabler more than a harm by itself, but it multiplies the success of TH-01 and TH-02 and defeats naive blocklists.
    TH-04 Reply-chain hijacking inside ongoing vendor threads — the link arrives as a reply in a real conversation from a real (stolen) mailbox. MEDIUM IdentityEmail Attacker first compromises one mailbox of the thread (often via an earlier link campaign), then replies with a new link. Payment fraud, repeated compromise of both sides of the thread, deep trust damage. Authentication checks pass because the real mailbox sends; severity rises to High in finance-heavy organizations where threads carry payment instructions.
    TH-05 SMS smishing link staging for MFA-relay scams — text messages carry links to pages that proxy one-time codes in real time. LOW MobileIdentity Attacker has the victim's password (from another leak or lure) and an SMS link page with a live relay. Session theft and account access despite MFA; personal-device fallout. Lower likelihood inside organizations that use number matching or hardware keys, but painful where SMS or push MFA remains common.

    Note

    Ratings are starting points. Adjust them to your sector. TH-04 jumps to High or Critical for a finance department that approves payments by email. TH-05 rises for any workforce still using SMS one-time codes for critical systems.

    TH-01 — Sigma: high-risk sign-in after a flagged link click

    sigma — t1608_005_th01_risky_signin.yml (authentication / azure)
    title: High-Risk or Impossible-Travel Sign-in After a Flagged Link Click
id: 6f3d9a41-2c7b-4e58-9a10-3d8ef51c2b74
status: stable
description: >
  Detects a successful sign-in that the identity provider marks as high risk or
  impossible travel. In the Splunk version below, the sign-in is correlated
  with a mail or proxy click to a young-domain or mismatched link for the same
  user within 30 minutes. This chain is the strongest signal that a staged
  link (T1608.005) ended in relayed credentials and valid-account abuse.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1608/005/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/002/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1078/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-19
logsource:
  category: authentication
  product: azure
detection:
  selection_risk:
    riskLevelDuringSignIn:
      - 'high'
      - 'medium'
  selection_reason:
    status: 'success'
    statusReason|contains:
      - 'impossible travel'
      - 'unfamiliar sign-in properties'
  condition: selection_risk or selection_reason
fields:
  - user
  - ip
  - country
  - city
  - deviceDetail
  - riskLevelDuringSignIn
falsepositives:
  - Genuine travel, new VPN exit nodes, and mobile carrier changes.
  - Shared service accounts used from two sites against policy.
level: high
tags:
  - attack.resource_development
  - attack.t1608.005
  - attack.initial_access
  - attack.t1566.002
  - attack.t1078

    SPL mapping note: Sigma user/ip/country map to the Azure AD sign-in schema (UserPrincipalName, IPAddress, Location) — alias them at ingest. The 30-minute click correlation lives in the SPL join, because Sigma single-logsource rules cannot join across sources.

    splunk — TH-01 risky sign-in within 30 minutes of a flagged click
    index=identity sourcetype=azure:ad:signin status=success
| search riskLevelDuringSignIn IN ("high", "medium")
      OR statusReason IN ("impossible travel", "unfamiliar sign-in properties")
| stats earliest(_time) AS risky_time, values(country) AS countries,
        values(IPAddress) AS ips, values(deviceDetail) AS devices by UserPrincipalName
| join UserPrincipalName max=1
    [ search index=mail sourcetype=mail:gateway:url
             (url_domain_age_days<7 OR display_target_mismatch=true)
      | stats latest(_time) AS click_time,
              latest(url_target_domain) AS clicked_domain by user ]
| eval minutes_after_click=round((risky_time-click_time)/60, 1)
| where minutes_after_click >= 0 AND minutes_after_click <= 30
| table UserPrincipalName, click_time, clicked_domain, risky_time,
        minutes_after_click, countries, ips, devices

    Field assumptions: index=identity sourcetype=azure:ad:signin with standard Azure AD fields; index=mail sourcetype=mail:gateway:url carrying url_domain_age_days and display_target_mismatch from a normalization step. False positives: real travel and VPN exits — confirm with the user before lockout. Tuning: for production precision, replace the join with a per-user lookup of recent flagged clicks (updated every 5 minutes) and require the risky sign-in to reference no known device.

    Real-World Case Study

    The case below is a composite built from public reporting patterns (thread-hijack loader campaigns and MFA-relay incidents), not a single named victim. It shows how one reply inside a trusted vendor thread turned a staged link into account takeover and attempted invoice fraud - a busy weekend. It also shows exactly which detections ended it.

    Context

    A mid-size wholesale company runs its payment flow over email: logistics vendors send invoices, the finance team replies with remittance details. The attackers never targeted the company first. They compromised a mailbox at a small logistics vendor using an older link campaign, then waited inside the thread — the classic staging-then-bridge sequence of T1608.005.

    Attack chain

    • Compromise the vendor's mailbox weeks earlier through a credential link (the same technique, one step upstream).
    • Stage a "document portal" sign-in page on a 2-day-old domain behind two throwaway redirect domains.
    • Reply inside the idle Q3 payment thread with the link: "view the updated remittance portal".
    • Finance coordinator clicks, signs in on the fake portal; credentials relay live to the attackers.
    • Attacker signs in from another country, approves MFA push, adds a hiding inbox rule.
    • Attacker sends a payment-change instruction to a second finance employee from the coordinator's mailbox.

    Timeline of the incident

    1. Day 0, 07:58 The hijacked reply lands in the finance mailbox. Authentication checks all pass because the real vendor mailbox sent it. The mail gateway flags it quietly: display text "portal" host differs from the link target, target domain 2 days old, single recipient.
    2. Day 0, 10:14 The coordinator clicks. Click telemetry records the user, the time, and the three-hop chain. The fake portal serves a pixel-perfect sign-in page with a valid padlock.
    3. Day 0, 10:19 The coordinator types the password and approves the MFA push without number matching. The relay passes the code to the attacker instantly.
    4. Day 0, 10:41 The identity provider raises an impossible-travel sign-in: two countries, 22 minutes apart. The SOC page lights up — flagged click plus risky sign-in on the same user within half an hour.
    5. Day 0, 10:52 While the analyst reads the timeline, the attacker creates an inbox rule that deletes messages containing "security", "MFA", and "quarantine". The mailbox audit log records it at 10:52.
    6. Day 0, 11:12 From the coordinator's mailbox, the attacker sends a payment-change instruction to a second finance employee. It is quarantined on the word pattern that evening, and hunting confirms it the same day.
    7. Day 0, 11:30 SOC response: password reset, all sessions revoked, the hiding rule removed, the three chain domains blocked at proxy, DNS, and mail. The vendor's IT contact is warned by phone.
    8. Day 0, 16:10 Hunting across 30 days finds no other clicks and confirms the 11:12 payment-change email was the only fraud attempt. The second employee had not acted yet. Finance confirms no money moved.
    9. Day 1–3 Vendor rebuilds its mailbox infrastructure. Both companies add the lure wording and chain domains to training and blocklists. The finance team adopts a call-back rule for any payment-change request.

    Detection point

    The winning alert was the join, not a single log. A flagged mail link showed a mismatch plus a young domain. A click followed 2 hours 16 minutes later. An impossible-travel sign-in came 22 minutes after that. The hiding inbox rule confirmed takeover and would have silenced the victim if the join had taken longer.

    Containment and eradication

    Reset and session revocation took 14 minutes from page to action. The team removed the inbox rule, re-registered MFA, and checked delegated access and forwarding. The three chain domains were blocked everywhere and reported to the registrars; the vendor side reset its own compromised account the same day.

    Recovery

    The coordinator returned to a clean session by afternoon. Finance re-verified the last 60 days of payment-change requests against call-back records — one earlier change, made before the incident, was verified as genuine. Both companies documented the lure for their next training cycle.

    Impact and consequences

    No money was lost and no regulatory notification was needed, but the potential was direct fraud: the attacker was one approval away from a payment redirect. The organization spent roughly 40 staff-hours on response, and both companies lost a day of finance-team throughput. The attack cost the crew two domains and one rented mailbox — a reminder of how cheap the attempt was.

    Team response and communication

    One incident channel coordinated SOC, finance, IT, and the vendor's contact. A written note to staff at 12:00 said only: "we contained it, no payment was made, please call back any payment change for 30 days." Daily briefings tracked three numbers: accounts reset, rules removed, domains blocked. The calm metric set kept the response steady.

    Lessons learned

    • Threads are trusted until they are not: authentication checks pass on hijacked mail, so behavioral flags must carry the alert.
    • Click telemetry is gold: without user, time, and chain recorded, the join that caught this attack cannot exist.
    • Inbox rules are the attacker's storage closet: audit them first, remove them explicitly, and alert on every creation.
    • Number matching would have stopped the takeover at the MFA step — a same-week fix after this incident.
    • A call-back rule for payment changes is the cheapest fraud control in the whole story.
    7

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop an attacker from placing links. You can make the links expensive: check destinations at click time, make credentials worthless without phishing-resistant MFA, give users a reporting button that gets answered, and rehearse the response. Controls below are grouped by layer, with a priority table at the end.

    Official mitigation position

    ATT&CK lists exactly one mitigation for T1608.005: M1056 Pre-Compromise. Link placement happens on attacker infrastructure and third-party platforms, so it cannot be prevented directly. Every control below therefore hardens what staging feeds — users, mail, web, and endpoints — and shortens the time between staging and abuse.

    Time-of-click URL defense

    Enable URL rewriting and time-of-click checking in your mail gateway, and keep the verdict logs. This is the control that turns a static link into a checkable event at the moment of the click, which is when rotation matters most. Verify it works — many tenants ship with it off.

    Email authentication

    Enforce SPF, DKIM, and DMARC with quarantine-or-reject, and actually read the DMARC reports. Authentication does not stop hijacked threads (the real mailbox sends), but it kills lookalike sender domains, which removes the mass-send half of link staging over months.

    Phishing-resistant MFA and conditional access

    Roll out FIDO2 keys or passkeys for admin and remote access, enable number matching everywhere else, and use conditional access to block sign-ins from impossible locations. Relay pages lose most of their value when a stolen password plus a proxied push cannot produce a session.

    Report button with a fast SLA

    Put a one-click report button in every mail client, route reports to the SOC queue, and answer within minutes during work hours. Every early report is click telemetry you get before the click. Publicly thank reporters — the habit spreads faster than any training slide.

    Block young domains at click time

    Feed your proxy and DNS with a newly-observed-domain list and apply an extra risk step when a link's target is under 7 days old. Allowlist the rare business needs. This one policy covers credential pages, payload hosts, and smishing targets at once.

    Session token binding

    Where your platform supports it, bind session tokens to devices and short refresh windows, and revoke refresh tokens on password change. Stolen passwords then produce sessions the attacker cannot keep, which shrinks the value of every credential page on the internet.

    Home and small-office advice

    Hover a link to preview the true destination before clicking, and read the domain backwards — the part just before the first single slash is the real site. Let your password manager refuse to fill credentials on lookalike domains: if it will not offer the login, the site is almost certainly wrong. Turn on number matching, or use a security key for your main email account. Never approve a push you did not trigger. Report suspicious messages with the built-in report button instead of replying to them.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    These are the failures detection teams actually make against staged links. Each card shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from coming back.

    1. Trusting display names

    Symptom
    Staff read "M. Chen <m.chen@vendor.example>" and click without checking the thread.
    Root cause
    Mail clients render the chosen display name, not the address.
    Fix
    Train on the address, not the name; flag external display names that mimic internal ones.
    Prevention
    Tag external mail in the client so every address is visible at a glance.

    2. Allowlist-by-sender-domain thinking

    Symptom
    Lures from a trusted partner's real domain sail through every filter.
    Root cause
    Policy treats sender domains as the trust signal; hijacked mailboxes break that model.
    Fix
    Judge behavior (unexpected link, thread idle, request type), not just the sender.
    Prevention
    Remove "trusted domain = safe" rules; keep allowlists only for known marketing flows.

    3. No time-of-click defense

    Symptom
    Clean-at-delivery links stay clean forever in the log, no matter where they point now.
    Root cause
    URL rewriting was never enabled, or verdict logging was turned off to save space.
    Fix
    Enable rewriting and click-time verdicts; keep 90 days of verdict history.
    Prevention
    Monthly config audit that proves rewriting is active in every tenant.

    4. Ignoring internal-only thread hijacks

    Symptom
    The lure moved between two internal mailboxes and no external filter ever saw it.
    Root cause
    Detection scope starts and ends at the mail perimeter.
    Fix
    Apply the same link checks to internal mail and watch mailbox audit for senders' odd sign-ins.
    Prevention
    Include internal east-west mail in the link detection scope statement.

    5. URL-rewrite alert fatigue

    Symptom
    Thousands of rewritten-URL events per day; analysts stop reading them.
    Root cause
    Every rewritten link looks like an event, with no scoring layer.
    Fix
    Score and group: only mismatch, young domain, or risky chain reach the queue.
    Prevention
    Review queue volume weekly; tune with documented exclusions.

    6. Short click-log retention

    Symptom
    During response you cannot answer "who clicked this last month?"
    Root cause
    Proxy and verdict logs kept for 7 to 14 days only.
    Fix
    Raise retention to 90+ days for click and verdict events; archive cold data cheaply.
    Prevention
    Retention table reviewed with legal and storage costs each year.

    7. Assuming MFA stops everything

    Symptom
    "We have MFA" closes the phishing ticket; relay kits still get in.
    Root cause
    Training never covered relay and fatigue attacks that proxy codes in real time.
    Fix
    Enable number matching, prefer keys or passkeys, and alert on odd approval patterns.
    Prevention
    Add relay-kit lures to the annual phishing simulation scenarios.

    8. No rule owner

    Symptom
    The young-domain rule silently broke when the age lookup expired.
    Root cause
    Detections have no named owner, no review date, no health check.
    Fix
    Assign owners and monthly health checks; show lookup freshness on a dashboard.
    Prevention
    Detection-as-code repository with required owner metadata per rule.

    9. Never running sanctioned lure simulations

    Symptom
    Nobody knows whether the pipeline catches the lures you worry about.
    Root cause
    Simulations stopped after one bad experience or privacy debate.
    Fix
    Run consented, documented purple-team link exercises quarterly.
    Prevention
    Standing rulebook: scope, consent, safe payloads, measured report SLA.

    10. Blocking sender IPs only

    Symptom
    The block works for a day; the same lure returns from a new host.
    Root cause
    Response stops at the IP, which attackers rotate hourly.
    Fix
    Block the whole chain (every domain and hop) at mail, proxy, and DNS.
    Prevention
    Runbook step: "enumerate and block all chain hops, then verify."

    11. Missing inbox-rule detection

    Symptom
    Takeover found by chance; the hiding rule had run for weeks.
    Root cause
    Mailbox audit logs not collected, or New-InboxRule events not alerted.
    Fix
    Ship the unified audit log and deploy the TH-04 rule from section 5.5.
    Prevention
    Add inbox rules and forwarding to the quarterly detection coverage review.

    12. No user-report loop

    Symptom
    Users forward suspicious mail to colleagues or just delete it.
    Root cause
    No report button, or reports vanish into a mailbox nobody reads.
    Fix
    One-click report to the SOC queue with a minutes-level SLA and feedback.
    Prevention
    Monthly report-metrics review: count, time-to-answer, true-positive rate.

    Daily habits

    • Review flagged-link queue: mismatch, young domain, deep chain.
    • Check risky sign-ins joined to recent clicks.
    • Confirm report-button messages were answered within SLA.

    Weekly habits

    • Walk one live lure through the six-step triage flow.
    • Review alert volume and exclusion drift on the five rules.
    • Check that time-of-click rewriting is still enabled in every tenant.

    Monthly habits

    • Replay the 5.4 event examples against all five rules.
    • Review new-domain feed freshness and lookup hit rates.
    • Report click-to-containment time and simulation SLA to leadership.
