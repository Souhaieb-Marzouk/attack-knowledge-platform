T1608.006 SEO Poisoning

Search engines feel like neutral ground. Attackers game them so their malicious page ranks first when your staff searches for a contract, an invoice template, or a driver. The victim does not fall for a trick - they arrive on their own.

Tactic: Resource Development Platform: PRE Parent: T1608 Created: 30 September 2022 ATT&CK data: 1.1 (Apr 2025) Detection difficulty: Hard Guide: v2.0
Reverse phishingVictims come to the attacker through search, not the other way. No email is sent and no link is pushed.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    This section explains SEO poisoning in plain words: what it is, why it works so well, and where it sits in the attack chain. Two short cards define the two core tricks - doorway pages and cloaking - because every later section uses those terms.

    What the technique is

    SEO poisoning means an attacker bends search results so their own pages rank high for words they choose. SEO stands for search engine optimization - the normal craft of making a page easy for search engines to find and rank. The attacker abuses that same system for a malicious goal.

    The victim never receives a phishing email. They search for something they need for work - a contract template, an invoice example, a device driver - and the attacker's page sits near the top of the results. The victim arrives on their own, in good faith.

    Why it matters

    Search captures intent. Someone who types "purchase agreement template Texas" has told the attacker exactly what to offer, so the fake page fits perfectly. Mail security never sees the attack, because there is no mail.

    Organic results also feel neutral. Many people believe a top position must have been earned, so the page borrows trust from the search engine. That trust costs the attacker nothing and defeats most training that focuses on suspicious links.

    Doorway pages in plain words

    A doorway page is a page built only to rank in search results and push visitors onward - not to help readers. The attacker generates hundreds or thousands of them, each one aimed at one long-tail phrase such as "employee data retention policy template Ohio".

    Long-tail phrases are specific, rarely-searched word combinations. Big legitimate sites do not compete for them, so a small poisoned page can reach rank one with almost no effort.

    Cloaking in plain words

    Cloaking means showing different content to different visitors. The doorway page checks who is asking. A visitor arriving from the search engine in the target country sees a fake forum. An automated scanner that visits directly sees a harmless article.

    Fake forums are staged discussion pages. Invented accounts "ask" about the document the victim searched for and "answer" with a file download. Because scanners never see this page, reputation systems stay quiet for weeks.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    The chain starts with T1584 Compromise Infrastructure: the attacker breaks into many small websites with outdated software and turns them into a farm. Then comes this technique, T1608.006 SEO Poisoning: the farm's doorway pages are groomed so search engines rank them.

    When a victim arrives, the fake forum convinces them to download and open a file. That step is T1204.002 User Execution: Malicious File. The file is usually a ZIP archive containing obfuscated JavaScript that installs a loader, which can end in ransomware. A modern cousin is T1608.007 AI Result Poisoning, which targets AI search assistants and chat answers instead of classic result lists.

    Good practice

    You cannot detect the poisoning while it happens on attacker-controlled sites. Your two best sensors are the click moment (proxy referer chains) and the execution moment (endpoint script rules). Teams that only hunt file hashes will miss this attack class.

    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section looks at SEO poisoning through the eyes of an attacker: why search beats email, how a staging farm is built and cloaked, and which mistakes get farms discovered. Everything here is described for defensive learning; the lab exercise is harmless and runs only against sites you own.

    Why search beats email

    Email attacks fight three layers of defense: the gateway, the mailbox filter, and the trained suspicion of the user. Search-driven attacks skip all three. There is no email to scan, no link in a message, and the victim's own intent carries the attack forward.

    Trust is also different. A stranger's email asks for action; a search result feels like the engine's own recommendation. Attackers exploit that borrowed trust, and they can measure exactly what victims want because the doorway pages rank for the phrases the attacker picked.

    How a staging farm is built

    The farm starts with volume. Attackers compromise hundreds of small sites - old CMS installs, forgotten plugins, weak admin passwords - and leave silent backdoors instead of defacing anything. Each site then receives template-based doorway pages. One template is filled with hundreds of long-tail keyword titles copied from real job documents: contracts, standard operating procedures (SOPs), and policy templates.

    Sitemap grooming comes next: the farm submits fresh sitemap files so crawlers index every doorway page quickly. Then the operator waits for ranking. Keyword research is aimed at work documents, because people search for those files with a city or state name, which makes the phrase unique and easy to own.

    The cloaking playbook

    Cloaking rules decide who sees the fake forum. Four checks do most of the work:

    • Referer: the page is shown only when the visitor comes from a search engine link.
    • User-agent: real browsers pass; headless scanners and crawlers get the harmless page.
    • Geo: only visitors from the target country see the payload path.
    • Cookie or frequency cap: the fake forum appears once per person, so repeat checks by researchers see nothing.

    Together these checks hide the attack from every scanner that does not arrive exactly like a real victim.

    Prerequisites and conditions

    • Access to many compromised websites (T1584) or, less often, owned domains with clean history.
    • A working payload: usually a ZIP with obfuscated JavaScript and a loader backend.
    • A keyword list built from real job documents: contracts, NDAs, SOPs, policies, drivers.
    • Patience: ranking takes days to weeks, so the operator plans a quiet month.
    • Redirect or staging infrastructure to serve the fake forum and the ZIP download.

    Lab only — safe referer exercise

    This harmless exercise shows the field that cloaking abuses. Using a page you own or administer, compare what your web server records when you visit directly versus when you arrive from a search result. No attack tools are involved, and nothing leaves your lab.

    lab_referer_check.sh — runs only against a site you own
    # LAB ONLY - inspect referer behavior on your own page. Harmless and read-only.
# Step 1: Visit your page directly in a browser, then again by clicking a search
#         result that links to it. (No automation needed.)
# Step 2: Read the last lines of your web server access log and compare referer.

tail -n 20 /var/log/nginx/access.log | awk '{print $NF}'

# What you should see:
#   direct visit   -> referer shows "-" (nothing)
#   search visit   -> referer starts with https://www.google.com/ (or another engine)
#
# This single field is what attackers use to cloak, and what your proxy uses to
# expose the chain: search engine -> long-tail blog -> archive download.

    Common variations

    Paid-ads duplication: the same landing pages are also pushed through paid search ads, which sit above organic results. Image-search abuse: poisoned thumbnails of document previews pull victims from image search. Fake driver sites: staged support-style pages offer device drivers or "missing codec" downloads for hardware names victims actually own.

    Attacker mistakes you can catch

    Identical doorway templates reused across hundreds of sites create cluster fingerprints - one report can burn the whole farm. Cloaking misconfigurations (a wrong referer list, a forgotten test URL) show researchers the real page. Ignoring mobile layouts breaks the illusion, because the fake forum renders wrong on phones.

    Cost of failure

    A burned farm loses months of patient ranking work in a day. That is why operators rebuild silently, reuse templates sparingly, and rotate ZIP payloads. When you report a farm to the search engine, you delete the attacker's most expensive asset: the rankings.

    4

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders cannot watch the attacker's farm, but the attack must pass two choke points you own: the click (your proxy) and the execution (your endpoint). This section shows what each choke point records, how to check your own estate, and how to triage a suspected poisoned result in minutes.

    Follow the referer chain in proxy logs

    The referer is the address of the page the user clicked from, and your proxy records it on every request. The poisoned chain has a clear shape: a search-engine referer, followed within seconds by a request to a long-tail domain, followed by an archive download from that same host.

    Legitimate document downloads rarely look like that. Join the three events into one chain (Sigma and SPL in section 5.5 do exactly this) and the attack stands out even when every single event looks harmless alone.

    Endpoint rules that pay off

    The execution step is loud if you watch for it. Windows Script Host (wscript.exe or cscript.exe) runs a .js, .jse, or .vbs file from the Downloads or Temp folder. Few legitimate programs do that.

    Files downloaded by a browser carry a mark-of-the-web (MOTW) - a small Windows marker that records the internet zone a file came from. Rules that combine MOTW with a script host in a user-writable path are the single highest-value control against this technique.

    Check your own estate

    Any website you run can become the attacker's staging site without your knowledge. Search your own sites for unexpected forum pages, new directories in web roots, unknown admin users, and sitemap entries you did not publish. Web-root file integrity monitoring (FIM - it alerts when web files change) turns this into a standing control.

    If your CMS is compromised, attackers get your domain's trust for free: your real site then serves the fake forum, and your brand appears in the poisoned results.

    Use search-engine webmaster tools

    Webmaster tools are free consoles that search engines offer to site owners. Register every domain you operate there: the engine then alerts you when it finds hacked content, injected pages, or cloaking on your sites - often before your own scans notice.

    The same console is your reporting channel in the other direction: when users report poisoned results to you, forwarding them through the engine's spam and security reports accelerates delisting.

    Artifacts this technique leaves behind

    • ZIP archives whose names match the search phrase almost word for word.
    • Fake forum threads that repeat the exact query in the title, with inventable "helpful" accounts.
    • Doorway templates repeated across many unrelated long-tail domains.
    • A proxy chain: search referer, then long-tail domain, then archive download within seconds.
    • wscript.exe or cscript.exe running a script from a user-writable folder.
    • Fresh long-tail TLD domains (for example .icu, .top, .click) resolved right before the click.

    Short triage flow for a suspected poisoned result

    1. 1 — Receive the signal A user report ("this forum looked odd"), an EDR script alert, or a proxy rule hit arrives in the queue.
    2. 2 — Confirm the chain in proxy Pull the user's last 30 minutes: search-engine referer, long-tail host, archive download. Three matching events make the case.
    3. 3 — Detonate offline Fetch and open the sample only in an isolated sandbox or VM with no corporate access. Never log in to the page or download on a work device.
    4. 4 — Find other clickers Hunt 90 days of proxy and DNS logs for the staging network: the host, its sibling domains, and its hosting IP.
    5. 5 — Isolate and respond Isolate any endpoint that downloaded or executed content from the network; rotate credentials that user could reach.
    6. 6 — Block and report Block the staging network (domains, IPs, certificate subjects). Report the poisoned results to the search engine and the sites to their hoster.

    Good practice

    Measure "time from click to block". The farm works only while its results stay ranked. So reporting to the search engine is not a courtesy. It is the control that ends the campaign for every victim, including other companies.

    5.2Threat intelligence

    The farm is built outside your walls, so outside data carries the early warning. This section lists the sources worth feeding into your program. It explains the cycle of collection, analysis, and action. It also shows how to grade confidence before you block something.

    Core sources

    • MITRE ATT&CK — the shared language for this technique (T1608.006), its parent (T1608), and what follows (T1204.002).
    • Vendor reporting — Mandiant's Gootloader series, plus Proofpoint, eSentire, Sophos, and others, publish fake-forum templates, ZIP names, and staging-network indicators.
    • OSINT services — URL scanning services (for example urlscan.io) to inspect suspicious pages from outside, passive DNS, and search-engine spam-report forums where researchers discuss farms.
    • Webmaster tools — free consoles from the search engines; their hacked-content and cloaking alerts are first-party intel about your own estate.
    • ISACs — sector sharing communities circulate fake-forum screenshots and sibling-domain lists seen against peers.
    • Internal telemetry — your own proxy referer chains and EDR history show which poisoned results actually reached your staff.

    Collection, analysis, application

    Collect by subscribing to two or three vendor feeds, enrolling your domains in webmaster tools, and saving every user report of an odd search result. Analyse by joining signals: a search-referer download, a ZIP named like the query, and a vendor screenshot of the same forum template is a confirmed campaign.

    Apply the intelligence in three ways. Set preventive blocks for high-confidence staging networks. Build detection content from the Sigma rules in 5.5. Write short intel notes that tell leadership which professions in your company are being targeted right now.

    Record every decision. When a blocked sibling domain later appears in a vendor report, that feedback loop proves your pipeline works.

    Actors and campaigns known for SEO poisoning

    Mustard Tempest
    Poisoned search results so fake browser updates ranked for popular software queries, feeding the SocGholish chain (Microsoft, Red Canary). The group MITRE documents under this technique.
    NIMBUS MANTICORE
    Registered dozens of domains that link to a typosquatted domain to build link reputation, and used keyword stuffing to improve ranking (Check Point, 2026). Documented by MITRE under this technique.
    Gootloader
    Documented by Mandiant's DFIR team, Sophos, and others across 2020–2022. The pattern: SEO poisoning for contract and SOP template searches, fake forums, ZIP downloads, the GootKit loader, later Cobalt Strike — see the case study below.
    SolarMarker / Jupyter
    Infostealer crew documented in 2021 public reporting using SEO campaigns: fake download pages for PDF tools and office templates, delivered through poisoned results. Vendor-documented context.

    Confidence discussion: public reporting is very reliable about mechanics (fake forums, cloaking, ZIP naming) because researchers dismantled farms and wrote them up. Attribution is weaker - staging networks overlap between crews, and operators rent, so treat campaign names as labels for behavior clusters, not as fixed groups. Always date-stamp reporting: a farm described last year may have changed its whole network.

    5.3Threats ranking and categorization

    The five threats below are the realistic ways SEO poisoning turns into harm for an organization. Each has a severity, the platforms it touches, what must be true before it can happen, its impact, and why the severity was chosen. Severity reflects combined likelihood and business damage for a typical mid-size organization.

    IDThreatSeverityPlatformPreconditionsImpactJustification
    TH-01 Full chain: poisoned result to loader to ransomware — a staff member finds a fake forum through search and downloads a ZIP. The loader it installs opens the door to ransomware. CRITICAL EndpointNetwork Attacker runs a ranked doorway farm; a working loader payload is attached to the fake forums. Initial access, credential theft, ransomware deployment across file servers. This is the complete Gootloader-style chain documented in public reporting: every step is realistic, victim intent is high, and the end state can be company-wide encryption.
    TH-02 Your own websites compromised to host doorway pages — attackers stage the farm on your CMS, so poisoned results rank with your brand on them. HIGH Cloud Your web estate runs outdated CMS software or weak admin credentials; no web-root integrity monitoring. Brand abuse, SEO delisting of your real pages, your visitors attacked, compliance exposure. Likelihood is moderate, but the blast radius includes brand trust: victims and customers see your domain recommend malware. Cleanup and search-recovery take weeks.
    TH-03 Poisoned results for high-value professional keywords — fake forums target finance, HR, legal, and IT document searches by job role. HIGH Network Doorway farm ranked for phrases used by your job roles; victim searches for a real document need. Critical-role users download malware during normal work; attackers land inside finance and legal teams. Intent-driven victims and full bypass of mail defenses give a high success rate per click; volume is lower than commodity spam, which keeps it below Critical for most organizations.
    TH-04 Fake driver and software downloads found through search — staged support pages offer device drivers or tools that employees search for. MEDIUM Endpoint Attacker stages download pages for hardware names or common utilities; a user cannot find the file on the vendor site first. Infostealer infection, credential and session theft, usually on one machine. Very common against consumers and happens at work too, but technical staff verify vendors more often, so impact usually stays contained to one user.
    TH-05 Paid-ads duplication of organic poisoning — the same poisoned landing pages are pushed as paid search ads, a malvertising bridge above the organic results. MEDIUM Network Attacker funds an ad account and passes ad-platform review long enough to run; keywords have commercial intent. Poisoned pages reach more users at the very top of the page; "skip the ads" habits fail for high-intent searches. Ad spend and platform review shorten the window, but placement multiplies clicks; ads also inherit search trust, so per-click success stays high.

    Note

    Ratings are starting points. Adjust them to your sector. TH-03 jumps toward Critical for a law firm or financial services company whose staff search legal and finance templates daily. TH-02 jumps if your public website is also your sales channel.

    Real-World Case Study

    The Gootloader operation is the best-documented public example of SEO poisoning at scale, described in detail by Mandiant and other researchers from 2020 onward. This section explains how the fake-forum mechanic works, then walks through a composite victim-organization timeline that we reconstructed from that public reporting.

    Context: what Gootloader did

    Gootloader operators targeted professionals who search for business documents: contracts, non-disclosure agreements, operating agreements, and policy templates, often combined with a city or state name. Instead of sending email, they made their staged pages rank for those exact phrases, so the victim came to them. The group ran a farm of compromised WordPress sites, and public reporting showed the same fake-forum template appearing across hundreds of them.

    The fake-forum mechanic

    When a search-referred visitor opened a doorway page, it rendered a convincing forum thread whose title repeated the visitor's search phrase word for word. Invented members "asked" for the document, and other accounts "replied" with advice and one attached ZIP. The forum even carried plausible dates and avatars. Everything except the file was theater - and it worked, because the page matched the victim's intent exactly.

    Cloaking and ZIP naming

    The fake forum was shown only under strict conditions: the visitor had to arrive from a search engine in the target region, use a real browser, and visit only once. Direct visits from scanners, researchers, and reputation bots saw a plain harmless article. The ZIP file name matched the searched document almost word for word. For example, an operating agreement search returned a file named like the agreement. That removed the last doubt a cautious user might have had.

    The loader chain

    Opening the archive exposed an obfuscated JavaScript file. Windows Script Host ran it, and the script fetched and installed the GootKit loader. From there, operators performed reconnaissance, deployed Cobalt Strike for hands-on access, and in documented cases moved toward ransomware deployment. Public reporting also showed the operators quietly building out new farms while old ones burned.

    Sourcing note

    The timeline below is a composite: it merges patterns that Mandiant and other researchers documented across many Gootloader victims into one coherent walkthrough for teaching. It is labeled as reconstructed, not as a single confirmed incident with those exact times.

    Timeline of one victim organization (reconstructed from public reporting)

    1. Day 0, 09:58 A finance employee searches for a data-retention policy template with a city name. She clicks the first organic result - a small blog she has never seen, running the fake forum.
    2. Day 0, 10:02 The thread "recommends" a ZIP named like the policy. She downloads and opens it; obfuscated JavaScript runs through Windows Script Host, and the GootKit loader installs quietly.
    3. Day 0, 10:11 EDR raises the first detection: Windows Script Host executing an obfuscated script from the user profile. The SOC starts triage at 10:20.
    4. Day 0, 11:00 Proxy history shows the full chain - search referer, long-tail blog, ZIP download - plus four sibling domains on the same staging network.
    5. Day 1–2 Investigators confirm the loader tried credential theft and lateral movement; phishing-resistant MFA on critical accounts blocks the stolen-password reuse.
    6. Day 3 The workstation is rebuilt, and credentials the user could reach are rotated. Six staging domains are blocked enterprise-wide. The farm is reported to the search engine and the hoster.
    7. Day 10 The search engine delists the poisoned results after the report. Awareness training gains a real fake-forum screenshot from the incident.

    Detection point

    The winning alert was the endpoint rule: Windows Script Host running a script from a user profile folder. The proxy chain - search referer to a long-tail domain serving a ZIP - provided the independent second signal that connected the event to SEO staging instead of generic malware.

    Containment and eradication

    The host was isolated within 40 minutes of the EDR alert. Investigators removed the loader, scheduled tasks, and browser extensions, revoked web-mail sessions to kill stolen-cookie reuse, and reset every credential the user profile could reach.

    Recovery

    The user returned to a rebuilt machine the next morning. Blocklists covered the staging domains and their shared hosting IP, and the SOC added a standing hunt for search-referred archive downloads across all office roles.

    Impact and communication

    No ransomware detonated in this reconstruction, but the intrusion reached the credential-theft stage, cost roughly 120 staff-hours, and disrupted finance-team work for a day. A single incident channel coordinated IT, legal, and communications; daily briefings tracked three numbers - hosts affected, credentials rotated, staging domains blocked - which kept leadership calm and informed.

    Lessons learned

    • SEO-poisoned staging targets work needs; training must show real fake-forum screenshots, not phishing-email clichés.
    • Blocking Windows Script Host on Downloads was worth more that week than every signature update.
    • The staging network reused hosting: one confirmed domain led to five more through IP neighbors.
    • Reporting the farm to the search engine accelerated delisting - the attack channel itself was shut down.

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop attackers from poisoning search results. You can make the whole investment worthless. Block the delivery at the proxy. Stop the execution at the endpoint. Keep your own sites out of the farm, and use the search engine itself as an ally. Controls below are grouped by layer, with a priority table at the end.

    Official mitigation position

    ATT&CK lists exactly one mitigation for T1608.006: M1056 Pre-Compromise. The SEO farm lives outside your estate, so it cannot be prevented directly. Every control below therefore hardens what staging feeds — users, mail, web, and endpoints — and shortens the time between staging and abuse.

    Policy and people

    Write a download policy for office roles: approved sources first, a simple request path for exceptions. Train staff with real fake-forum screenshots and teach the pattern that matters: a forum-style page that appeared through search and offers a file matching your query word for word. Give them a one-click report button for odd search results, and answer every report.

    Endpoint

    Run EDR in block mode with mark-of-the-web-aware rules that stop script hosts (wscript.exe, cscript.exe) from executing anything written to Downloads or Temp. Keep browsers and the OS patched, remove local admin rights, and consider blocking archive extraction for roles that never need it.

    Network and web

    Enforce a proxy category policy that treats long-tail TLDs (.icu, .top, .click, .cyou) as a risk factor to score, not a blanket ban. Alert on search-referer archive downloads. Consider blocking archive downloads entirely for office roles - most never need one. Filter DNS with a newly-observed-domain feed.

    Protect your own estate

    Patch your CMS and plugins, and enforce two-factor authentication on all admin accounts. Put a WAF in front of public sites. Run web-root file integrity monitoring so injected doorway pages are caught within hours. Review sitemap files and admin user lists monthly - a farm entry you never published is the alarm.

    Use the search ecosystem

    Register every domain you operate in the search engines' webmaster tools to receive hacked-content and cloaking alerts. When users report poisoned results, forward them through the engine's spam and security report forms the same day - delisting is the control that ends the campaign for everyone.

    Logging, monitoring, response

    Ship proxy, DNS, and EDR logs to the SIEM and run the chain rules from section 5.5. Keep the triage flow from section 4 rehearsed, and test the SEO scenario end to end once a quarter with a purple-team exercise: one poisoned click, one download, one isolation.

    Home and small-office advice

    Beware of forum-style download pages that appear in search results, especially when the file name mirrors your search words - that match is the attacker's fingerprint. Type vendor URLs directly instead of searching for downloads, never open scripts inside downloaded archives, and keep OS and browser auto-update on. Use the built-in protection of your platform, turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it is offered, and back up important files to a service that keeps version history.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    These are the failures detection teams actually make against search-borne attacks. Each card shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from coming back.

    1. Blocking search engines outright

    Symptom
    A proxy block of all search engines breaks work within the hour.
    Root cause
    The team reacts to one poisoned click by removing the whole channel.
    Fix
    Revert the blanket block; detect the chain (search referer + long-tail host + archive) instead.
    Prevention
    Policies state that search is business-critical and is never blocked wholesale.

    2. Whack-a-mole domain blocklists

    Symptom
    New sibling domains appear weekly; the blocklist never catches up.
    Root cause
    Blocking single domains without chain logic or sibling discovery.
    Fix
    Block by behavior and expand blocks via hosting IP and template clusters.
    Prevention
    Every block entry records its cluster so the next sibling is found automatically.

    3. No mark-of-the-web policy

    Symptom
    Downloaded scripts run freely; only signature hits get caught.
    Root cause
    EDR rules ignore the internet-zone marker on downloaded files.
    Fix
    Enable MOTW-aware blocking for script hosts on user-writable paths.
    Prevention
    Endpoint baseline includes MOTW rules on every Windows build image.

    4. Ignoring your own estate

    Symptom
    The company blog quietly serves doorway pages for months.
    Root cause
    Nobody checks whether the organization's own sites host injected content.
    Fix
    Search your sites for forum-like paths, unknown sitemap entries, and unknown admins; enroll in webmaster tools.
    Prevention
    Web-root FIM and monthly sitemap review are standing controls.

    5. No user-report path for odd search results

    Symptom
    Users screenshot a suspicious forum page and share it in private chats.
    Root cause
    No easy reporting button, or reports disappear unanswered.
    Fix
    Add a one-click report action and answer every report within a day.
    Prevention
    Report volume is tracked and praised in team meetings.

    6. Alert fatigue from raw category blocks

    Symptom
    Thousands of daily "suspicious category" alerts; analysts auto-close them.
    Root cause
    Category blocks without risk scoring create noise instead of signal.
    Fix
    Score by chain context: referer, TLD, file type, user role.
    Prevention
    Every policy change ships with its expected alert volume.

    7. Hash-only matching

    Symptom
    The same farm delivers a new ZIP next week and nothing fires.
    Root cause
    Detection keyed to file hashes, which attackers rotate.
    Fix
    Detect behavior chains and template clusters, not identities.
    Prevention
    Quarterly review removes hash-only rules from the delivery class.

    8. No proxy/EDR chain correlation

    Symptom
    Two quiet tickets - a proxy note and an EDR note - never joined.
    Root cause
    Proxy and endpoint alerts live in separate queues with no common case.
    Fix
    Correlate by user and time window; open one case for the chain.
    Prevention
    The SIEM correlation rule from 5.5 is owned and tested.

    9. No rule owner

    Symptom
    Rules break silently after a schema change; nobody notices for months.
    Root cause
    Detections exist without a named owner or review date.
    Fix
    Assign an owner and review date to every rule in 5.5.
    Prevention
    A dashboard shows last validation date per rule.

    10. Never validating rules

    Symptom
    The chain rule never fired although reporting shows the campaign hit your sector.
    Root cause
    No replay testing with realistic events.
    Fix
    Replay the 5.4 event examples through the pipeline each quarter.
    Prevention
    Purple-team scenario includes one poisoned search click.

    11. Assuming email is the only delivery channel

    Symptom
    Gateway statistics look perfect, yet users still get infected.
    Root cause
    The program equates "delivery" with "email" and ignores search.
    Fix
    Add the search-referer chain to the delivery dashboard.
    Prevention
    Threat model reviews cover Resource Development techniques, not just phishing.

    12. Ignoring mobile users

    Symptom
    Incidents start on phones; proxy coverage for mobile browsing is zero.
    Root cause
    Rules and logging assume desktop browsers only.
    Fix
    Route mobile browsing through the same proxy and apply the same chain logic.
    Prevention
    Log coverage matrix includes managed mobile devices.

    • ZIP download from long-tail TLD referred by a search engine (proxy, High)
    • Windows Script Host executes a script from Downloads or Temp (process, High)
    • Crawler burst on forum-like paths of your own site (webserver, Low, hunting)
    • Fresh long-tail TLD domain queried right after search traffic (DNS, Medium, enrichment)
    • Archive in Downloads followed by a script file within two minutes (file_event, Medium)

    Response checklist when SEO poisoning is confirmed

    • Confirm the chain in proxy: search referer, long-tail host, archive download.
    • Detonate the sample offline in a sandbox VM with no corporate access.
    • Hunt 90 days of proxy and DNS logs for other clickers and sibling domains.
    • Isolate every endpoint that downloaded or executed content from the staging network.
    • Block the staging network: domains, IPs, and certificate subjects, including siblings.
    • Report the poisoned results to the search engine and the sites to their hoster.
    • Log the case with the search phrase used, and add the fake-forum example to training.
