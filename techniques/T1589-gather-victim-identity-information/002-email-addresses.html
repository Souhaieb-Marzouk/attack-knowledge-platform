    Overview

    This section explains the technique in plain words, why a list of addresses is worth money, and where the work sits in an attack. It ends with a map of everything this page covers.

    What the technique is

    Email Addresses (T1589.002) means that an attacker collects the email addresses of an organization's staff. They read public team pages, professional profiles, and published documents. They also learn the naming format — the rule a company uses to build addresses, such as first.last@aureliapay.example.

    Many attackers then verify the list. They ask the mail server which addresses really exist by sending small test messages. The mail command that names a recipient is called RCPT TO, and this testing is called recipient probing. The server answers "user unknown" for fake addresses, so the rejections tell the attacker exactly which list entries are real.

    MITRE ATT&CK places this under Gather Victim Identity Information, sub-technique 002. The parent guide explains the whole family; this page stays on addresses only.

    Why it matters

    A verified address list is working material for email crime. Phishing works better when the name and the format are real. Business email compromise (BEC) — criminals posing as executives or suppliers to move money — needs correct addresses to look credible. Spammers pay for verified lists, because every valid address is worth more than a guessed one.

    The technique is cheap and mostly quiet, but it is not invisible. The harvesting step touches your public web server, and the verification step touches your mail server. Those are two systems whose logs you control. Almost every detection idea in Section 5 is built on that simple fact.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Reconnaissance is the first tactic in the ATT&CK Enterprise matrix, and it runs on the PRE platform: the activity happens outside victim systems, before any intrusion. An address list built today can feed an attack months later.

    Step 1
    Reconnaissance (TA0043) — harvest names and addresses, guess the format, verify the list. This technique lives here.
    Step 2
    Resource Development — register the lookalike domain, warm up the sending infrastructure, prepare the lure.
    Step 3
    Initial Access — send targeted phishing to the verified list (T1566 Phishing), or run BEC fraud that needs no malware at all.
    Step 4
    Impact and follow-up — wire fraud, credential capture, or a full intrusion that now starts with trusted, real addresses.

    The same list also feeds T1656 Impersonation, where attackers pretend to be a person or brand from the victim organization.

    Official reference

    The MITRE page for this technique lists procedures, mitigations, and detection notes: attack.mitre.org — T1589.002. ATT&CK marks detection for this technique as indirect, which matches everything below.

    Key fact: simple steps, high volume

    Address recon has no exploit code and no malware. It is reading pages and asking a mail server simple questions — which is why it scales across thousands of companies at once, and why lists are sold in bulk. For defenders this is good news: the two steps that create value, scraping and probing, are exactly the steps that touch systems you control. Publish less and log the edges well, and this technique becomes boring.

    Good to know

    The strongest control is not a detection at all. Publishing role aliases such as info@ and accounts@ instead of personal addresses removes most of the value of format guessing, because a verified list of aliases is worth very little for targeting people.

    Step 1 — Study the target company

    The attacker picks Aurelia Pay, a growing payments company, and reads its news, job ads, and team pages. Target selection needs no tools and leaves no trace on company systems. The SOC sees nothing, because nothing has happened on company property yet.

    Step 2 — Scrape public pages

    A script walks the team and leadership pages while the attacker browses professional profiles. The result is roughly 300 names with roles, enough to see who works in finance and HR. Fast scraping can appear in web server logs as a burst of page requests from one source.

    Step 3 — Mine published documents

    The attacker downloads press releases and reports as PDF files and reads the document properties. Author fields often contain internal names or addresses that never appear on the website. The downloads look like normal traffic, so this step stays invisible in logs.

    Step 4 — Guess the naming format

    The attacker combines the names with common formats such as first.last@ and checks the format against any address already known, for example one from a public mailto: link. The output is a list of candidate addresses. This step happens entirely on the attacker's side.

    Step 5 — Probe the mail server

    The attacker connects to the mail server named in the domain's MX record and sends RCPT TO tests for each candidate address. Unknown users are rejected, so 40 rejections mark 40 addresses that do not exist. These rejections land in mail logs, waiting for someone to count them.

    Step 6 — Build and trade the list

    The attacker keeps the addresses that were not rejected and ranks finance and HR first, because they move money and handle payroll. The list is used directly or sold to other criminals. A paid email-verification service may have done the probing instead, which hides the step behind a normal-looking tool.

    Step 7 — The SOC links the signals

    An analyst reviews the week and finds two signals that agree: a burst of page requests from one client, and a wave of rejections from one IP. Each one alone looks like noise. Together they describe harvesting followed by verification — a campaign being prepared.

    Step 8 — Defenders respond

    The SOC rate-limits and blocks the probing source, reviews what the public pages and documents reveal, and briefs the helpdesk and finance about a likely phishing wave. Recon cannot be undone, but the attack that depends on the list can be blunted before it starts.

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section explains how professional attackers think about address harvesting. The goal is defensive understanding: if you know why and how lists are built and verified, you can lower the value of your own addresses. All examples here are lab-only and use fictional targets.

    When and why attackers use it

    Every email campaign needs addresses that actually receive mail. A guessed list wastes effort and burns sender reputation — the trust score that mail providers give to sending servers. Too many bounces, and future mail lands in spam folders before the campaign even starts. Verification raises the success rate and lowers that reputation burn, so serious crews verify before they send.

    The technique is also low risk. Reading a public page is legal almost everywhere, and a single small test message is hard to attribute and rarely investigated. Attackers exploit exactly this gap between harmless-looking steps and harmful outcomes.

    Where it applies and preconditions

    The technique applies to any organization whose staff names appear in public, which means effectively all of them. Preconditions on the attacker side are small: public names, a reachable mail server, and patience. The richer your public footprint — staff pages, press quotes, conference talks, job ads — the better their raw material.

    Verification is cheapest when the mail server answers differently for real and fake users. Systems that reject unknown users clearly are the easiest to probe; catch-all systems that accept everything make verification slow and expensive, which is itself a defense.

    How it is performed

    Public website

    Team pages, "about us" pages, and support portals reveal names, roles, and formats. mailto: links — the kind that open your mail program with the address inside — publish addresses as plain text that scripts collect in seconds.

    Document metadata

    PDF and Office files carry properties: author, creator, company, sometimes internal file paths. A report written by a finance employee can leak a direct internal address that the website never shows.

    Professional networks

    Professional profiles give org charts, new hires, and job titles. Signatures quoted in public posts, conference speaker lists, and patent filings add more names, and email formats travel between these sources.

    Recipient probing

    The attacker connects to the mail server named by the domain's MX record and issues RCPT TO tests. Rejections reveal which users exist. Paid email-verification services do the same at scale and sell the results, which hides the step behind a normal-looking service.

    Leaks and brokers

    Old breach dumps contain working addresses, and criminal brokers sell verified lists by industry and role. Buying skips the work and the logs — the defender's first hint may be the phishing itself.

    Pastes and code repositories

    Paste sites and public code repositories sometimes hold exported contact lists or configuration files with mail settings. A search for the company domain often turns up material the company forgot it published.

    Lab-only rule

    Everything on this page is educational. Harvest only from fictional sites you own. Never probe a real mail server without written authorization: recipient probing against a system you do not own is unauthorized access in many countries, even when every single test message is tiny. Never buy real stolen lists, and never use recon output against real people. Practice on a fictional company site in a lab instead.

    A safe lab exercise

    Publish a small fictional company site in a lab: five staff pages with mailto: links, one PDF report with realistic-looking metadata, and one job ad that names the team structure. Then audit it as a defender. Count every mailto: link, open each published file in a metadata viewer, and list every name and address that leaks.

    Next, play the attacker offline. From the leaked names, write the two most likely address formats and describe how you would verify them without touching a real server — for example, which paid service or which log-free route you would use. Fix what leaks, run the audit again, and measure the difference. The exercise takes an hour and shows exactly why publication choices matter, with no risk to any real system.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    Variations

    • Passive only — read pages, never test. Safest for the attacker, but the list stays unverified and bounces burn reputation later.
    • Active probing — send RCPT TO tests or use verification services. Fast and reliable, but it writes rejections into your logs.
    • Buying data — use brokers or verification-service output to skip the work entirely.
    • Interactive — pose as a recruiter or partner and ask for a direct address, a step toward T1598 Phishing for Information.

    Mistakes attackers make

    • Probing too loud — thousands of tests from one address in minutes is the clearest signature this technique ever produces.
    • Reusing the same origin network, such as one hosting provider or one autonomous system, across victims, so one block and one detection cover many campaigns.
    • Ignoring catch-all domains that accept every address, which turns the whole probe run into useless "valid" results.
    • Trusting document metadata too much, when authors often used personal or generic addresses that pollute the list.

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders rarely see the harvesting itself. They see the two steps that touch company systems: scraping on the public web server and probing on the mail server. This section shows what those steps look like in logs, where to look, and what to do in the first hour after a wave appears.

    How defenders can see it

    Four channels carry most of the signal. First, MX and authentication logs — the MX record names the server that receives mail for your domain, and its logs record every accepted or rejected recipient. Second, public web server logs, which record page scraping and document downloads. Third, mail gateway analytics: bounce, quarantine, and delivery trends that move when someone abuses your addresses. Fourth, DNS logs, where bulk lookups of MX and mail-related records can precede a probe run.

    What artifacts it leaves

    • Waves of 550 5.1.1 recipient rejections from one source on the MX.
    • Bursts of requests for team, leadership, and careers pages from one client.
    • Bounce spikes — mail that only pretends to come from you, bounced back to you (backscatter).
    • Out-of-office replies sent to unexpected external recipients, which confirm live addresses.
    • Resale traces: your domain's addresses turning up in paste sites, spam-trap reports, or broker listings.

    Where to look

    • MX and smtpd logs in the SIEM: rejection counts per source, per recipient, per hour.
    • Web server access logs: requests against /about, /team, /careers, and document paths.
    • Mail gateway analytics: bounce digest, quarantine volume, and delivery trends.
    • DNS query logs: resolver lookups of your MX and TXT records in odd volumes.
    • Leak and brand monitoring: your domain appearing in dumps or list sales.
    • Helpdesk tickets: calls or messages that use real staff names — evidence the list already works.

    Why it matters and where it fits

    Harvesting precedes every email wave. A rejection wave is dated, addressable evidence that someone is preparing an attack against specific people — usually finance and HR first. Acting on it means briefing the right departments before the first lure is sent, not after the wire transfer.

    Two controls blunt the technique directly: a catch-all mailbox that accepts every address removes the rejection signal attackers need, and MX rate limiting slows probing until it is too slow to be worth it. Log retention makes the history searchable when the phishing finally arrives months later.

    First-hour triage flow

    1. Validate
    Open the raw log lines. Is it one source sending real 550 5.1.1 rejections, or something harmless such as greylisting or a misconfigured internal system?
    2. Scope
    How many rejections, which addresses were tested, and over how long? Did the same source also pull team pages? Which departments do the tested names belong to?
    3. Contain
    Rate-limit or block the source at the MX and the web edge. Keep the evidence — export the log lines before queues rotate.
    4. Assess exposure
    List the addresses that were confirmed to exist. Review what the tested names reveal about the attacker's list, and check what your public pages and documents still publish.
    5. Harden
    Apply tarpitting, review catch-all settings, brief the helpdesk and finance, and record the wave for trend analysis and for the response that may follow.

    This triage takes under an hour when roles are clear. The checklist in Section 9 repeats it in a compact form.

    Warning

    A probe wave means someone is building a list, not that an attack has started. Do not reset accounts, and do not reply to anything yet. But do not sit on it either: the wave is dated evidence, and targeted phishing usually follows within days or weeks. Store the addresses that were probed — when the wave arrives, you will know exactly who to warn first.

    Evidence tip

    Auditors accept indirect detection for address recon — if you document the reasoning. Write one page: harvesting is external and mostly invisible; the two touchpoints we control are the public site and the MX; therefore we review published exposure quarterly and monitor rejection and scraping waves continuously; findings go to the security lead monthly. Add one concrete story if you have one — the week a rejection wave was caught and the helpdesk briefed is worth more than any policy quote. That page satisfies most evidence requests and doubles as your detection strategy.

    5.3 Threat ranking and categorization

    Below are the realistic address-recon threats for a typical mid-size organization. Ranking combines likelihood (how often it happens) with impact (how bad it gets). Every detection you build should trace back to one of these rows, so the ranking also becomes your detection backlog.

    TH-1 — SMTP recipient probing Medium

    Description: a script connects to the mail server named by the domain's MX record and sends RCPT TO tests for guessed addresses. Rejections reveal which addresses exist; silences confirm the rest.

    Preconditions: public names to guess from; a mail server that answers differently for valid and invalid users.

    Impact: a verified list — the raw material for targeted phishing and BEC.

    Justification: common, and it is the step that turns a guess list into a working one. Visible in MX logs, but only if those logs are kept and read.

    TH-2 — Public directory scraping Medium

    Description: scripted downloading of team, leadership, and careers pages to collect names, roles, and mailto: links into an org chart.

    Preconditions: staff pages published without rate limits or bot filtering.

    Impact: a name list and a role map; enables TH-1 and TH-4 by showing who to target first.

    Justification: near-certain to happen at some volume. Individually less damaging than an intrusion, but it makes everything else that follows stronger.

    TH-3 — Metadata leaks in published documents Low

    Description: document properties — author, creator, company, sometimes file paths — ship inside PDFs and Office files, and bulk downloads harvest them.

    Preconditions: files published without a scrubbing step in the export pipeline.

    Impact: internal names and sometimes direct addresses that never appear on web pages.

    Justification: lower frequency today, but one annual report can feed a whole campaign, and the fix is nearly free.

    TH-4 — Verified list used for BEC High

    Description: criminals use a verified list to run executive-impersonation and supplier-fraud mail — business email compromise, where a trusted-looking message asks finance to move money.

    Preconditions: verified addresses; public knowledge of roles; a payment process that accepts email requests.

    Impact: direct financial loss attempts; each success is expensive, public, and slow to unwind.

    Justification: the most damaging end state of this technique; losses reported to authorities are measured in billions of dollars per year.

    TH-5 — Bounce and out-of-office analysis Low

    Description: attackers send light test mail or read the bounces and auto-replies from a first wave, learning which addresses are alive and who is on leave.

    Preconditions: a list from TH-1 or TH-2; willingness to send a first small wave.

    Impact: list quality improves; out-of-office replies reveal colleagues, deadlines, and contact names.

    Justification: quiet and slow, but it improves the list right before the main attack, which makes the main attack worse.

    TH-6 — Cloud directory and autocomplete enumeration Medium

    Description: with any foothold — a stolen session, a malicious app consent, an abused mail connector — attackers pull the full address book from the cloud directory or read mailbox autocomplete caches.

    Preconditions: some access to identity or mail services; weak app governance.

    Impact: the complete internal list, including aliases, groups, and people who were never public.

    Justification: needs a foothold first, but it returns far more than external harvesting ever can, so it appears in most serious intrusions.

    Each line answers the question "does this address exist?" with "no". 203.0.113.77 is the probing source, from=<verify@bulkcheck.example> is the sender the tool used, and to=<…> is the guessed address.     Both count bounce events in a 15-minute window and alert above 50.
    Assumptions
    The gateway writes one event per bounce, or a digest line per window, into index=email sourcetype="mailgw:bounce_digest" with fields such as ReferencedSender. Point the query at your gateway's bounce index and rename fields to match its schema.
    False pos.
    Legitimate campaigns, password-reset mail to old addresses, and seasonal list churn. Exclude known campaign senders.
    Tuning
    Split the alert by ReferencedSender: bounces referencing an internal sender nobody used point to spoofing and deserve a higher severity.
    Severity
    Medium — a bounce spike often marks list abuse or a spoofing wave in progress.

    S4 — Published-document scraping

    Mapping
    Same document extensions, same window, same threshold of 20 downloads.
    Assumptions
    Standard Apache access logs land in index=web sourcetype="access_combined" with CIM fields. If documents live behind a CDN, run the same query against CDN logs.
    False pos.
    Search indexers and research tools. Allowlist verified crawler ranges after reverse-DNS checks.
    Tuning
    Pair the rule with metadata scrubbing in the publishing pipeline — detection shows the interest, scrubbing removes the value.
    Severity
    Low — a bulk download alone leaks only if metadata or content still does.

    S5 — Executive display-name impersonation attempt

    Mapping
    Same logic: internal display name, external sender domain.
    Assumptions
    The gateway exports DisplayName, SenderDomain, and Direction fields. Add your executives' names to the match list, and keep the brand term in one place so both rules stay in sync.
    False pos.
    Legitimate partners that carry the brand name. Maintain a partner allowlist and review matches monthly.
    Tuning
    Raise severity when the recipient is in finance or HR, or when the subject contains payment keywords.
    Severity
    High — this is the classic opening move of BEC built on a verified list.

    S6 — Mail authentication enumeration burst

    Mapping
    Same failure pattern, same 10-minute window, same threshold of 10 failures per source.
    Assumptions
    Dovecot auth lines land in index=email sourcetype="dovecot:imap"; the rex extracts the remote IP from the rip= field. For Exchange or other IMAP providers, match their auth-failure events instead.
    False pos.
    One user with a wrong saved password can reach the threshold on their own. Enrich with user context before escalating.
    Tuning
    A high users_tried count from one source means enumeration, not a fat-fingered password — treat it as the stronger signal.
    Severity
    Medium — shows someone testing which mail accounts exist and which passwords stick.

    Validation note

    Test every rule before production: replay a sample event from Section 5.4 against the query, confirm it fires, then confirm a known-good event does not. Record the test in your detection inventory with a date and an owner. A rule without a test is a hypothesis, not a detection.

    Real-World Case Study

    The story below is a composite: a fictional regional manufacturer, Northgate Components, with a timeline assembled from patterns that repeat in public BEC incident reports. It shows how a probe wave that nobody counted turned into a wire-transfer attempt — and how one alert could have changed the story.

    Context

    Northgate Components is a fictional manufacturer of industrial fasteners with 420 employees across three sites. Security was one part-time IT generalist, an email gateway with default settings, and no SIEM. The public website listed the leadership team with direct addresses, and the annual quality report was published as a PDF with its original document properties.

    Attack chain at a glance

    Stage 1
    Address recon: staff pages, PDF metadata, format guessing (this technique)
    Stage 2
    RCPT TO probing against the mail server to verify 300 guessed addresses
    Stage 3
    Validated list of 88 addresses, finance and HR ranked first
    Stage 4
    Executive display-name spoof mail to the finance team, requesting an urgent supplier payment
    Stage 5
    Near-miss wire transfer, report, and response

    Timeline

    TimeEventVisible signal at the time
    Day 0–6Attacker scrapes the leadership pages and downloads the annual PDF report; document properties give two internal addresses.Web server logged 214 requests from one client; nobody reviewed those logs.
    Day 7Attacker probes the mail server with 300 guessed addresses over 40 minutes.MX logs recorded 212 rejections from one IP; no alert existed.
    Day 8–9Attacker finalizes a validated list of 88 addresses and marks finance and HR.Invisible — the work happens off company systems.
    Day 16, 09:12Spoof mail arrives, "from" the managing director via an external domain, asking finance to pay a new supplier account today.Gateway passed the message: SPF alone was checked, display names were not.
    Day 16, 14:30The controller starts the payment of 96,400 dollars; a second finance employee questions the supplier name on a call.The payment is paused — a human question, not a control, saves the day.
    Day 16, 16:05The controller reports the message to the IT generalist, who forwards it to the gateway vendor; the SOC-style investigation starts.First human report — nine days after the probe wave was logged.
    Day 17Containment: sender domain blocked, all mailboxes searched for the same thread, bank informed, payment cancelled.No other recipient had replied; the attack is stopped before loss.
    Day 18–30Eradication and recovery: DMARC policy hardened, directory and PDFs cleaned, MX rate limiting added, finance briefed.Rebuild and lessons learned.

    The detection point that mattered

    The MX rejections on Day 7 gave nine days of warning before the first spoof mail on Day 16. A single rule that counted rejections per source would have flagged the probing run, and a briefing to finance that same week would have made the spoof mail look familiar instead of urgent. The evidence existed the whole time — it just sat in a log nobody read.

    Containment

    The sender domain and its host were blocked at the gateway, every mailbox was searched for the same thread, and the bank was informed within the hour so the pending transfer was cancelled cleanly. The probing IP range was added to the block list as a precaution.

    Eradication

    The team confirmed no malware was involved — this was a pure identity attack. They removed direct addresses from the public site, scrubbed the PDF metadata, enabled gateway checks on display names, and filed a takedown request for the spoofing domain.

    Recovery

    Finance got a thirty-minute briefing using this real thread as the example, a payment-change rule (call-back to a known number) went into force, and DMARC reports were reviewed weekly. MX logs now flow into a small log store with a weekly rejection review.

    Impact and consequences

    No money was lost, but the costs were real: about 12 staff-days of response and cleanup, takedown fees, a delay to a customer audit while controls were documented, and a management team that learned its org chart was public working material. The controller's comment in the retrospective: the fake mail knew our supplier process better than some of our own staff did.

    Team response and communication

    The small team kept it simple: one person led, one wrote everything down, one talked to staff and the bank. They briefed finance and the helpdesk the same day, used plain language in every update, and confirmed facts before naming the spoofing domain to anyone outside. Clear roles, not tools, kept the response calm.

    Lessons learned

    • MX rejection logs are cheap telemetry; keep them and count rejections per source.
    • Display-name checks belong at the gateway, not only in user training.
    • Published documents leak metadata; scrub before upload.
    • Role aliases instead of personal addresses would have shrunk the validated list dramatically.
    • A probe wave is dated early warning; store the probed addresses and warn those people first.
    • Finance needs a second-channel rule for payment changes; the near-miss came from one employee's question, not from a control.

    Home and small-office advice

    • Use an email alias for signups so your main address is harder to harvest and easier to rotate.
    • Limit where your address appears in public; a contact form protects better than a mailto: link.
    • Keep the external out-of-office text plain — no names, colleagues, or deadlines.
    • Check your address on breach-notification services and change reused passwords.
    • Turn on the strongest MFA your mail provider offers; a phished password alone then buys nothing.

    The top three actions here cost almost nothing: aliases, a payment-change rule, and metadata scrubbing are process fixes, not purchases. They remove the value of a verified list even when the harvesting itself succeeds — which is the correct goal, because the harvesting usually will succeed.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    Most teams do not miss this technique because the signals are hidden. They miss it because the logs are short-lived, the thresholds are wrong, and nobody owns the rules. Each mistake below shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from returning.

    1. No MX log retention

    SymptomYou cannot answer "has anyone probed our addresses?" even after a BEC attempt.

    Root causeMail logs rotate in days, or the MX never ships logs to the SIEM at all.

    FixShip smtpd and auth logs to the SIEM and keep 12 months of history.

    PreventionLog-source inventory with freshness checks; retention reviewed twice a year.

    2. Ignoring small probe waves

    SymptomWaves of 10–30 rejections a day from rotating sources never trip an alert.

    Root causeA fixed threshold was chosen once and never tested against low-and-slow behavior.

    FixBaseline rejections per day, alert on deviation, and add a weekly review of the trend.

    PreventionTest rules at low volume; attackers read public write-ups about thresholds too.

    3. Treating bounces as noise

    SymptomThe bounce queue grows and shrinks; nobody looks at it as security data.

    Root causeBounce handling is seen as a mail-admin chore, not as attack telemetry.

    FixRoute the bounce digest to the SIEM and deploy rule S3 with a per-window threshold.

    PreventionMonthly bounce-trend review together with the mail team.

    4. Public mailto links everywhere

    SymptomThe attacker's list mirrors your contact pages address for address.

    Root causeThe convenience of one-click mail beats any exposure review.

    FixReplace personal mailto: links with contact forms or role aliases.

    PreventionA security review step in the website release process.

    5. Metadata in PDFs unreviewed

    SymptomThe annual report ships with internal author addresses in its properties.

    Root causeThe publishing pipeline has no scrubbing step and nobody samples the output.

    FixScrub properties at export; keep a clean template for external documents.

    PreventionRandom monthly sample of published files opened in a metadata viewer.

    6. No scraping detection

    SymptomYou learn about harvesting from the phishing wave months later.

    Root causeWeb server logs exist, but no rule reads them for directory-page bursts.

    FixDeploy rules S2 and S4 with a one-week baseline before enforcement.

    PreventionDetection backlog covers public assets, not only internal systems.

    7. Over-blocking SEO bots

    SymptomSearch ranking drops, marketing complains, and the scraping rule gets disabled.

    Root causeThe edge rule was written in reaction mode with no allowlist for declared crawlers.

    FixVerify major crawlers by reverse DNS, allowlist them, and keep the rule active.

    PreventionChange control for CDN and WAF rules with a test window before enforcement.

    8. Untested Sigma rules

    SymptomA rule fires on everything, or worse, never fires at all.

    Root causeRules were written once and deployed without replaying real events.

    FixReplay the sample events from Section 5.4 against each rule; fix the logic until both sides pass.

    PreventionDetections in version control with one test event per rule.

    9. Detections without owners

    SymptomRules break silently after a gateway upgrade; nobody notices for months.

    Root causeDetections were treated as a one-time project, not maintained products.

    FixCreate an inventory with an owner, a review date, and a test status per rule.

    PreventionOwnership review as part of the quarterly detection health check.

    10. Catch-all misconfiguration masking invalid-address signals

    SymptomAfter catch-all was enabled, rule S1 went quiet and everyone assumed probing stopped.

    Root causeThe edge no longer rejects, so rejection counts drop to zero while probing continues blind.

    FixSwitch detection to internal-filter counts and gateway quarantine metrics instead of rejections.

    PreventionDocument acceptance-policy changes as detection-affecting changes, with a rule review attached.

    11. Helpdesk unaware of harvest waves

    SymptomCallers citing real staff names get trusted, because the desk has no context.

    Root causeRecon alerts stay inside the SOC and are never shared with frontline teams.

    FixAdd a "brief helpdesk and finance" step to the wave-response playbook, with a short note about expected follow-up mail.

    PreventionAfter-action reviews include a check that frontline teams were informed.

    Habits that keep you ready

    Daily

    • Check rejection counts per source against the daily baseline.
    • Scan the gateway quarantine for address-testing patterns.
    • Review new matches from the display-name rule.

    Weekly

    • Trend scraping and probing against baseline; note every deviation.
    • Review DMARC reports for senders forging your domain.
    • Sample a few published documents for leaked metadata.

    Monthly

    • Validate a third of your detection rules against sample events.
    • Walk the public site as an attacker would, and file cleanup tickets.
    • Review staff-directory and alias changes together with HR.

    Response checklist

    • Validate the wave in raw log lines — real rejections, not greylisting or an internal system.
    • Scope it: which addresses, how many, how long, and did the same source scrape the website?
    • Contain: rate-limit or block the source at the MX and the web edge; export the evidence first.
    • Assess exposure: list confirmed addresses; review public pages and document metadata again.
    • Harden: tarpitting, catch-all review, and a DMARC report check for senders forging your domain.
    • Brief the helpdesk and finance about the expected phishing wave and the payment-change rule.
    • Record the wave and update baselines, allowlists, and the detection inventory.
