Employee Names T1589.003

Before an attack, adversaries write down the names of the people who work at a target organization. Names come from team pages, professional networks, conference lists, and old breach data. Attackers use them to build email addresses and to make lures sound personal. This page shows how that research works, what it leaves behind, and how defenders can still catch it.

    1

    Overview

    This section explains what the Employee Names sub-technique is, why a simple list of names is powerful, and where it sits in an attack. It ends with the sources attackers use and a map of the whole page.

    What the sub-technique is

    T1589.003 Employee Names means that an attacker collects the names of your staff before touching any system. MITRE describes it simply: employee names are used to derive email addresses, to guide other research, and to make lures more believable. A name is small, but it connects everything: who to target, who to pretend to be, and which address format will reach them.

    The official ATT&CK page puts this in Reconnaissance, on the PRE platform: the work happens outside your network, before any intrusion. The official entry is attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1589/003, and the parent guide covers the whole identity-research family.

    Names rarely stay alone. Attackers pair each name with a role from a profile page, then feed the pair into the Email Addresses and Credentials sub-techniques. That chain — name, then address, then secret — is how most targeted email attacks begin.

    Why it matters

    Almost every convincing attack quotes something true. A message that says your project lead Sarah Chen asked for this report works because Sarah is real. Voice calls to the helpdesk work because the caller knows the real IT manager's name. Names turn generic phishing into personal pressure.

    The technique is also nearly free and low risk. Reading a public page is legal in most places, and the collecting step leaves nothing on your systems. Attackers exploit exactly this gap: the most valuable reconnaissance happens where defenders have no logs.

    Defenses still work, just differently. Publishing less makes the list thin. A verified helpdesk process makes borrowed names weaker. Watching your edges and feeds catches the moments when the research does touch you. This page builds those defenses into concrete detections.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Reconnaissance is the first tactic in the ATT&CK Enterprise matrix. Employee-name research usually runs before the attacker has any infrastructure aimed at you, and its output feeds almost every later step.

    Before
    Target selection (T1589 parent) — the attacker picks your organization. Read the parent guide.
    Here

    Next

    A name list built today can still be in use years later, because staff lists change slowly and people reuse the same platforms.

    Key fact: what ATT&CK says about detection

    ATT&CK's official detection strategy for this technique (DET0857, analytic AN1989) is honest about the limits: the activity has a very high false-positive rate and often happens outside the victim's visibility, so detection efforts should focus on related stages such as Initial Access. In practice you detect the side effects: a scraping burst on your people pages, a lure that quotes a real name, a helpdesk caller who knows too much, and feed matches on your staff's names. Every rule in Section 5 follows that idea.

    Good to know

    The strongest control is not a detection at all. Publishing the minimum — names and roles only, no photos, no direct addresses — and running a helpdesk that verifies callers with a callback make a stolen name list far less valuable. Both cost almost nothing.

    The same story, written out

    Step 1 — Pick the target

    The attacker picks Aurelia Pay because it is growing and appears in fintech news. They open a worksheet with one column: people. Target selection needs no tools and leaves no trace on company systems, so the SOC sees nothing.

    Step 2 — Scrape the staff pages

    A script walks every public team, leadership, and careers page and saves names, photos, and titles. Forty-two names in twenty minutes. If the site has no bot limits, the only trace is a burst of page requests from one client in the web server logs — Sigma rule S1 watches exactly there.

    Step 3 — Read professional networks

    The attacker reads public professional profiles and conference speaker lists, then adds roles, projects, and reporting lines to the names. This step happens on other companies' platforms, so your logs show nothing. It is also where the list turns into an org chart: who controls money, who resets passwords, who approves invoices.

    Step 4 — Cross-check breach data

    The attacker searches old breach dumps for the company domain. Twelve Aurelia Pay addresses appear, which confirms the email format first.last@aureliapay.example and pairs several names with real inboxes. A leak monitoring feed is the only practical way for defenders to learn that this step happened.

    Step 5 — Build the persona

    Using the list, the attacker creates a believable identity: an IT service desk employee with a real manager's name, a lookalike sender address, and a story about an account check. The persona is strong because every detail in it is true. Nothing touches company systems yet.

    Step 6 — Send targeted lures

    Messages go to the finance team and the helpdesk, each quoting real colleagues and real projects. The mail gateway logs a brand-new sender and a new link domain. This is the first corporate telemetry of the campaign, and Sigma rules S2 and S6 live there.

    Step 7 — An employee reports the lure

    One finance employee notices the message uses a colleague's name who left the company last year. They press the report button. The report lands in the security queue with full headers — the single most useful artifact of the whole campaign, because it shows which names the attacker used.

    Step 8 — Intel confirms, defenders respond

    The leak feed confirms staff names in a fresh dump, and the SOC links it to the report and the scraping burst. The sender and domain are blocked, exposed staff get a briefing, the helpdesk is told to expect calls citing real names, and the public staff pages are cut back to names and roles.

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section explains how professional attackers build name lists. The goal is defensive understanding: once you know which pages and habits feed their list, you can thin the list out. All examples here are lab-only and use fictional targets.

    When and why attackers use it

    Name research is the quiet first hour of almost every targeted attack. It is cheap, it needs no tools beyond a browser, and it carries little risk: reading public pages is legal in most places. The payoff is large, because a name converts a mass email into a personal message and a cold call into a warm one.

    Professional crews treat the list as an asset with versions. They refresh it before each campaign, mark who left the company, and rank people by value: money movers, password resetters, executives with authority. A stale list burns lures; a fresh one pays for weeks of work.

    Where it applies and preconditions

    The technique applies to any organization whose staff names appear anywhere in public, which means effectively all of them. Preconditions on the attacker side are minimal: time, patience, and free sources. The richer your public footprint — team pages with photos, profiles with roles, conference bios, press quotes — the better their raw material.

    Two habits make the list especially strong. First, staff who repeat the same name and employer across many platforms are easy to enrich. Second, public organizational details — who reports to whom, who runs which tool — let the attacker aim the right story at the right person.

    How it is performed

    Staff pages

    Team, leadership, and support pages give names, titles, photos, and sometimes direct addresses. A scripted walk collects them in minutes. Photos matter more than people think: they become avatars for fake profiles that look familiar later.

    Professional networks

    Public profiles show roles, projects, tools, and manager names. New-hire posts are prized: a person in their first weeks knows few voices and few faces, which makes impersonation easier. Job ads name the tools and sometimes the team size.

    Conferences and publications

    Speaker lists, attendee lists, webinar registrations, and published PDFs all carry real names with employers. Author fields inside documents add internal names that never appear on any website. Webinar question rounds reveal who works on what.

    Breach and leak data

    Old dumps pair names with working email addresses, which confirms the company format and marks who is still reachable. Criminal markets sell such lists by industry. Attackers treat a dump match as a quality check on their hand-built list.

    Data brokers

    Commercial services sell contact lists scraped from many sources. Buying skips the slow work and the logs entirely. The defender's first hint may be the lure itself, months later, quoting a name that was sold in bulk.

    Chat communities

    Open Slack or Discord communities around your products give display names, helpful staff, and internal tool names. A polite stranger who asks simple questions collects real names from real employees without sending a single email.

    Lab-only rule

    Everything on this page is educational. Only ever profile organizations you own or are authorized to test, in a written engagement with clear rules. Never build profiles of real staff, never buy real lists, and never use recon output against real people. To practice, publish a fictional company site in a lab and profile that instead.

    A safe lab exercise

    Publish a small fictional company site in a lab: five staff pages with photos and titles, one press page, and one conference brochure naming your fictional speakers. Then let a teammate play the attacker for twenty minutes with only a browser. Their job is to answer three questions: Who controls money? Who resets passwords? What is the email format? They will almost always succeed, and the exercise shows exactly which pages leak the most. Strip the site back to names and generic roles, run the drill again, and measure how much harder it got. That difference is your exposure reduction, proven in one afternoon.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    Variations

    • Passive only — read public pages and never contact anyone. Slowest and safest for the attacker.
    • Scraping — script the collection across many pages at once. Fast, but it can leave bursts in your web logs.
    • Buying data — use brokers or breach dumps to skip the collecting work entirely.
    • Interactive — chat with staff under a false identity, a step toward T1598 Phishing for Information.

    Mistakes attackers make

    • Scraping too fast, which logs their address in your web server access logs.
    • Reusing the same fake persona across victims, so researchers can link campaigns together.
    • Trusting old lists — lures that name employees who already left raise suspicion instead of lowering it.
    • Sending lures too soon after scraping, which makes the timeline obvious in a joint investigation.
    4

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders almost never catch the collecting itself. They catch its shadows: a scraping burst on the people pages, a reported lure quoting a real name, a feed match on staff addresses, a helpdesk caller who knows too much. This section shows what those shadows look like and what to do in the first hour.

    How defenders can see it

    Four channels carry most of the signal. First, your own edges: public web servers, which record scraping bursts on people pages, and document download logs. Second, people: employees who report lures that quote real colleagues — the report tells you which names the attacker used. Third, outside feeds: leak monitoring for staff addresses and brand watching for fake support identities. Fourth, late identity telemetry: risky sign-ins that appear once the lures convert.

    What artifacts it leaves

    • Web access logs full of directory-page requests from one client, fetching no images or scripts.
    • Bulk downloads of PDFs and slides that carry author names.
    • Reported phishing that names real managers, projects, or colleagues.
    • Helpdesk tickets where the caller used correct staff names but failed verification.
    • Staff names and addresses appearing in breach dumps and broker listings.

    Where to look

    • Public web server logs: request bursts against /team, /about, /careers, and document paths.
    • Mail gateway and report queue: senders, display names, and subjects that borrow internal identities.
    • Helpdesk tickets: failed verification attempts, especially callers citing real names.
    • Leak and brand monitoring: staff addresses in dumps; support-brand lookalikes.
    • Identity provider logs: the moment name-based lures turn into sign-in attempts.
    • Chat platform audits: guests who collect names and roles in public channels.

    Why it matters and where it fits

    Name signals are the earliest warnings you will get for a targeted social attack. A scraping burst today predicts a personalized phishing wave next week. Acting early means briefing the people whose names were harvested and hardening the helpdesk before the first call, not investigating a takeover afterwards.

    In the ATT&CK chain these signals sit in Reconnaissance (TA0043), one full stage before Initial Access. ATT&CK's own guidance (analytic AN1989) says the direct activity is hard to detect and to verify, so treat every channel above as a partial sensor — and treat a combination of two as a case.

    First-hour triage flow

    1. Validate
    Is the signal real? Replay the log lines: is the burst one client walking people pages, or a search crawler? Is the reported lure quoting a name that is actually employed?
    2. Scope
    Which names could be exposed? Check the public pages, the scraped paths, and any dump matches. Search the gateway for other lures quoting the same people.
    3. Contain
    Rate-limit or block the scraping client, block the lure sender and link domain, and pull the message from other mailboxes.
    4. Assess exposure
    List the names, roles, and addresses the attacker most likely holds. Flag the high-value ones: finance, IT, helpdesk, executives.
    5. Harden
    Brief the helpdesk to expect calls citing real names, remind the named staff what a lure may look like, and file tickets to reduce what the public pages reveal.

    This flow fits in an hour when roles are clear. Section 9 repeats it as a checklist you can paste into your incident tool.

    Warning

    Do not dismiss a scraping burst or a single report as noise, and do not punish the person who was named in a lure. The list already exists; shame only stops the next report. A helpdesk call that passes on names but fails verification is your strongest late signal — log it as an incident, not as a user mistake.

    For each campaign report, ask three questions. Which name sources fed the lures: staff pages, profiles, conference lists, dumps? The answers become new Sigma rules and tuning entries — that loop is the whole discipline.

    5.3 Threat ranking and categorization

    Below are the realistic employee-name threats for a typical mid-size organization. Ranking combines likelihood (how often it happens) with impact (how bad it gets). Every detection you build should trace back to one of these rows, so the ranking also becomes your detection backlog.

    TH-1 — Mass name harvesting Medium

    Description: a script walks every public team, leadership, and careers page and collects names, titles, and photos into a structured list.

    Preconditions: staff pages published without rate limits or bot filtering.

    Impact: the raw list. On its own it is noise, but it is the input for every other threat here, and it is the one step most likely to touch your logs.

    Justification: near-certain to happen at some volume for any visible organization, and reliably visible in web logs if anyone reads them.

    TH-2 — Name-to-email derivation High

    Description: the attacker combines harvested names with your address format, confirms candidates with the tricks of T1589.002, and builds a validated, personalized mailing list.

    Preconditions: a name list; a guessable address format; one confirmed address from any source.

    Impact: targeted phishing and BEC waves that greet people by name; far higher success rates than generic spam.

    Justification: this is the bridge from names to intrusion, and it appears in nearly every documented targeted email campaign.

    TH-3 — Key-person profiling High

    Description: deep tracking of the few people who matter: who moves money, who resets accounts, who approves exceptions, and what they say in public.

    Preconditions: executives and function owners with public profiles; press coverage; conference talks.

    Impact: convincing impersonation (T1656), well-timed fraud, and pressure on the exact people whose mistakes cost the most.

    Justification: lower volume, but each success is expensive and public; every major BEC case starts here.

    TH-4 — Fake IT-support persona High

    Description: the attacker builds an identity for "IT support" decorated with real staff and manager names, then calls or messages employees to harvest approvals, codes, or MFA re-enrollments.

    Preconditions: harvested names and reporting lines; a helpdesk or staff who trust caller knowledge of real people.

    Impact: account takeover without any malware; the attacker logs in with the victim's own reset credentials.

    Justification: the documented pattern of LAPSUS$ and Scattered Spider intrusions; email defenses never see it.

    TH-5 — Conference and publication mining Medium

    Description: collecting speaker and attendee lists, webinar registrations, and author names inside published PDFs and slides to enrich the org chart.

    Preconditions: your staff speak, publish, or attend visible industry events; documents ship without metadata scrubbing.

    Impact: internal names and project details that never appear on the website, neatly tied to roles.

    Justification: steady and low-risk for the attacker; one good conference page can fill a department.

    TH-6 — Resold name lists Medium

    Description: names and addresses of your staff circulate in breach dumps, paste sites, and broker catalogs, ready for other crews to buy.

    Preconditions: any third-party breach that included staff addresses; broker listing of your sector.

    Impact: your exposure grows without any action by you; new attackers start with work you never saw happen.

    Justification: invisible until you subscribe to a feed, which is exactly why feed monitoring is on the priority list.

    These four event-shaped examples come from the sources above, using the fictional company aureliapay.example. Read the notes under each one to see exactly what a detection would use.

    Three directory pages in two seconds with no images, scripts, or CSS fetched is a scripted walk, not a person. The python-requests user agent is a hint, but attackers can fake browsers — so the rate per client is the more reliable signal in Sigma rule S1.

    Operation: ReportPhish is the audit action written when a user presses the report button. The subject quotes a real, senior colleague, which is exactly what harvested names enable.     Mapping

    index=identity sourcetype="azure:aad:signin" from your cloud identity add-on. Field names such as RiskLevelAggregated depend on your ingestion; keep the names consistent with your other identity rules.
    False pos.
    Travel, hobby VPN use, mobile-carrier geolocation errors. Enrich with an approved-travel lookup.
    Tuning
    Suppress verified-benign users for 24 hours; correlate with recent name-based reports for a stronger case.
    Severity
    High — often the first successful step after a name-based campaign.

    S4 — Bulk download of published documents

    Mapping
    Same document extensions, same 30-minute window, same threshold of 20 downloads.
    Assumptions
    Standard Apache access logs land in index=web sourcetype="access_combined" with CIM fields. If documents live behind a CDN, run the same query against CDN logs.
    False pos.
    Search indexers and research tools. Allowlist verified crawler ranges after reverse-DNS checks.
    Tuning
    Pair the rule with metadata scrubbing in the publishing pipeline — the detection shows the interest, scrubbing removes the value.
    Severity
    Low — a bulk download leaks names only if metadata or content still carries them.

    S5 — Staff names in a credential dump feed

    Mapping
    Same single-match logic; SPL summarizes volume, affected users, and the first and last sighting.
    Assumptions
    Leak feeds vary by product, so point the query at your feed's index and rename fields such as Email and BreachName to match its schema. Replace aureliapay.example with your own domain.
    False pos.
    Aggregators re-reporting old breaches. Keep a record of handled incidents and dedupe on the breach name.
    Tuning
    Route to a playbook: brief the named staff, check their recent sign-ins, and force resets where reuse is likely.
    Severity
    High — names plus working addresses are the shortest silent path to a believable persona.

    S6 — One sender reported by several users

    Mapping
    Both count report events per sender in a 24-hour window and alert above three; SPL adds the distinct-reporter count for confirmation.
    Assumptions
    M365 unified audit logs land in index=o365 sourcetype="o365:management:activity" and the report integration preserves SenderAddress. Group by link domain instead if your pipeline keeps URLs rather than senders.
    False pos.
    Simulation and training emails. Exclude your awareness-test sender domain explicitly.
    Tuning
    Read the reported subjects aloud in triage: quoted colleague names reveal exactly which part of the list the attacker is using.
    Severity
    High — several humans reporting one persona means a name-based wave is live.

    Validation note

    Test every rule before production: replay a sample event from Section 5.4 against the query, confirm it fires, then confirm a known-good event does not. Record the test in your detection inventory with a date and an owner. A rule without a test is a hypothesis, not a detection.

    Real-World Case Study

    The story below is a composite: a fictional game studio, Frostpine Games, with a timeline assembled from patterns that repeat in public incidents where attackers first harvested employee names and then impersonated IT support. It shows how a public staff page became the blueprint for an account takeover.

    Context

    Frostpine Games is a fictional studio with 320 staff, known for a popular online game. Its website lists every team with photos, names, and roles, and its developers speak at industry conferences. The helpdesk resets MFA after a short call. MFA is push-based without number matching. Security has four people, and no leak or brand monitoring was in place at the start of this story.

    Attack chain at a glance

    Stage 1
    Name recon: staff pages, conference bios, professional networks (this technique)
    Stage 2
    Persona built: "IT service desk" identity citing real manager names
    Stage 3
    Voice phishing calls to staff; one executive approves an MFA re-enrollment
    Stage 4
    Cloud mail and build-server access; source assets staged for extortion
    Stage 5
    Extortion threat with proof, detected response, recovery

    Timeline

    TimeEventVisible signal at the time
    Day 0–8Attacker scrapes the staff pages, conference speaker lists, and professional profiles, building a 60-name org chart.Web server saw ~350 people-page requests from one client in 40 minutes; nobody read those logs.
    Day 9Attacker registers a lookalike support domain and sets up phone numbers and a mail sender for the "IT service desk" persona.No brand or domain monitoring existed.
    Day 10–12Test calls to the helpdesk and three staff. Callers cite the real support manager's name and an old ticket number; verification is a friendly chat, not a process.Helpdesk tickets note "caller knew internal names" but there is no rule that treats this as an incident.
    Day 13, 10:05Vishing call to a finance director: the "desk" walks her through an MFA re-enrollment, citing her real team lead and current project.The reset ticket looks ordinary; the callback field is optional and left empty.
    Day 13, 10:40Attacker signs in to the executive's cloud mail from a residential proxy.The identity provider raises a medium-risk sign-in; it lands in a backlog.
    Day 14Attacker reads mail, finds build-server credentials, and stages design assets and source snapshots for exfiltration.A DLP warning on a large upload is seen the next morning.
    Day 15, 09:12Extortion email arrives at the executive's own inbox, quoting her reporting line and org chart — proof the name list was the weapon.First human report. The quoted details make the source of the leak obvious within minutes.
    Day 15–17Containment: sessions revoked, MFA re-enrolled with number matching, helpdesk reset process frozen, lookalike infrastructure blocked and reported.Attacker access ends. No assets were published.
    Day 18–30Eradication and recovery: staff pages cut to names and roles, conference policy changed, helpdesk verification rebuilt with manager callbacks, leak and brand monitoring purchased.Rebuild and lessons learned.

    The detection point that mattered

    Four signals existed before the extortion note: a scraping burst on Day 0–8, helpdesk tickets whose callers "knew internal names" on Day 10–12, a risky sign-in on Day 13, and a DLP warning on Day 14. Each one was recorded; none was owned. The case only opened when a human read the extortion email and recognized her own org chart in it. That is the cost of unwatched name signals.

    Containment

    Sessions and refresh tokens for the executive account were revoked, and the re-enrolled authenticator was replaced with a hardware key. The lookalike domain and phone numbers were blocked and sent to the takedown service. The helpdesk froze all MFA resets until the new verification process was live.

    Eradication

    The team confirmed the attacker touched no servers — the whole intrusion was identity abuse. They removed the staged uploads, checked the build system for new tokens, and confirmed no other staff had completed "desk" re-enrollments by auditing every reset ticket from the previous month.

    Recovery

    Access returned through clean admin accounts with phishing-resistant MFA. The studio announced the incident internally in plain language, warned peer studios through the industry ISAC, and rebuilt the public staff page with names and generic roles only.

    Impact and consequences

    No source code was published and no ransom was paid, but the costs were real: about 25 staff-days of response, a delayed content update, an extortion threat hanging over the team for two anxious weeks, and a funder conversation that turned suddenly serious. The retrospective note read: they knew our org chart better than our intranet did, because our website was their intranet.

    Team response and communication

    Four people used the same simple model as bigger teams: one incident commander, one communicator, one scribe, one investigator. They briefed leadership twice daily in plain language, gave the helpdesk a script for staff questions, and reported the incident to the regulator within the required window because the timeline had been written down from day one.

    Lessons learned

    • A public staff page with photos and roles is attacker working material; publish names and generic roles only.
    • A caller who knows real names is not verified. Verification means a callback to a number from the HR system, every time.
    • Number matching and hardware keys for executives were installed within the week; push approvals were the real vulnerability.
    • Helpdesk tickets saying "caller knew internal names" are incidents, not curiosities — they needed a rule and an owner.
    • Scraping bursts and risky sign-ins were both recorded days before the harm; signals without owners are decoration.
    • Leak and brand monitoring would have shown the persona being built on Day 9, four days before the first call.

    Home and small-office advice

    • Keep your job title and employer off public profiles, or limit them to broad terms.
    • Never approve a login or MFA prompt you did not start, no matter who the caller claims to be.
    • If "IT support" calls you, hang up and dial the official number yourself.
    • Use a password manager and strong MFA so a guessed address plus pressure still fails.
    • Be careful in public chat communities: friendly strangers collecting names are part of this technique too.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    Teams rarely lose to a name list directly. They lose to the habits around it: what gets published, how callers are verified, and who owns the weak signals. Each mistake below shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from returning.

    1. The staff page publishes the full org chart

    SymptomThe attacker's target list mirrors your public team page exactly, photos included.

    Root causeMarketing owns the website; security never reviews what it publishes.

    FixReduce staff pages to names and generic roles; remove photos, direct emails, and reporting lines.

    PreventionAdd a security review step to the website release process.

    2. No social-media or speaking guidance

    SymptomConference bios and profiles reveal reporting lines, tools, and project names.

    Root causeStaff were never told what is safe to share, or why it matters.

    FixPublish a one-page guide: share your craft, not your org chart. Offer generic team names for bios.

    PreventionInclude the guide in onboarding and review speaker submissions with a checklist.

    3. Conference and attendee lists published in full

    SymptomA single PDF hands the attacker a department's worth of names with employers.

    Root causeEvent tooling defaults to public attendee pages nobody asked to review.

    FixTurn off public attendee lists; publish speakers with generic affiliations where possible.

    PreventionAdd "name exposure" to the event launch checklist alongside venue and catering.

    4. A caller who knows names is treated as verified

    SymptomHelpdesk resets MFA because the caller cited the real manager and an old ticket number.

    Root causeVerification by conversation feels social-proofed; the script is optional.

    FixMake callback verification to the HR-system number mandatory; never accept names as identity.

    PreventionLog every "caller knew internal names" event as an incident with an owner and a trend line.

    5. Push-approval MFA without number matching

    SymptomStaff approve prompts during convincing calls because the persona knows their names.

    Root causeApproval fatigue plus social pressure defeats plain push.

    FixEnable number matching tenant-wide and move executives and IT to hardware keys.

    PreventionTrack the MFA-method mix monthly and retire SMS and plain push as factors.

    6. Scraping alerts tuned into silence

    SymptomWeb bursts against people pages are auto-closed as "probably a crawler".

    Root causeEarly noise was never tuned with context, so analysts stopped trusting the rule.

    FixAdd allowlists for declared crawlers, split thresholds by path type, and require a closure reason.

    PreventionMonthly tuning review with closure-reason statistics, as in Section 5.5 rule S1.

    7. No leak or brand monitoring

    SymptomYou learn that staff names circulate in a dump — or that a fake support brand exists — from the incident itself.

    Root causeExternal monitoring was classified as nice to have and never budgeted.

    FixSubscribe to a leak feed and brand watch, and wire matches into rules S5 and the takedown playbook.

    PreventionQuarterly exposure review that covers dumps, paste sites, and lookalike registrations.

    8. Display-name checks missing at the gateway

    SymptomImpersonation mail passes because SPF and DKIM are technically valid on the attacker's own domain.

    Root causeAuthentication was treated as identity; display names were considered cosmetic.

    FixDeploy rule S2 with a protected-name list: brand, support team, executives.

    PreventionSync the protected-name list with HR monthly; review matches with a partner allowlist.

    9. Detections without owners

    SymptomRules break silently after a schema change; nobody notices for months.

    Root causeDetections were treated as a one-time project, not maintained products.

    FixCreate a detection inventory with an owner, a review date, and a test status for each rule.

    PreventionOwnership review as part of the quarterly detection health check.

    10. Measuring only simulation success

    SymptomTraining click rates look great while real name-based lures still work.

    Root causeUsers learned to spot simulated lures, not real ones; the metric rewards gaming.

    FixTrack real report rates, helpdesk verification failures, and time-to-triage as primary metrics.

    PreventionReport simulation and real metrics together to leadership, always.

    11. Blaming the named employee after an incident

    SymptomThe retrospective focuses on the person who approved the reset, not on the exposure that enabled it.

    Root causeA visible human error is easier to point at than a systemic publishing gap.

    FixRun the review on controls: what did we publish, what did the helpdesk require, what alerts had no owner.

    PreventionThank reporters publicly; track how many incidents start with a report, and make that number grow.

    Habits that keep you ready

    Daily

    • Triage every phishing report within the target time.
    • Review risky sign-in alerts with fresh eyes, checking device and location.
    • Check helpdesk tickets for verification failures or callers citing real names.

    Weekly

    • Review scraping and document-download trends against baseline.
    • Confirm leak and brand feeds still report zero unhandled matches.
    • Walk the public website and job ads as an attacker would, and file cleanup tickets.

    Monthly

    • Validate one third of the Section 5.5 rules against sample events.
    • Audit reset tickets for skipped verification fields.
    • Sync the protected display-name list with HR changes.
