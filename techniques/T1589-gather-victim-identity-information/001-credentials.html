    Overview

    This section explains what the Credentials sub-technique is, why it matters, and where it sits in an attack. It also maps the three collection routes you will meet on the sibling pages.

    What the sub-technique is

    T1589.001 Credentials means that an attacker obtains login secrets of your staff before touching a single system. Three routes dominate. First, credential phishing: a fake login page that copies the real one and records what the victim types. Second, leak dumps: buying email-password pairs from old breaches of other companies. Third, MFA abuse: collecting one-time codes through fake pages or tired approvals.

    The most effective version is the adversary-in-the-middle kit (AiTM). It sits between the victim and your identity provider, relays the login, and steals the password, the MFA code, and the new session cookie in one step. A session cookie is the temporary ticket a browser holds after login, which is why even correct MFA does not stop a replay of it.

    MITRE ATT&CK places this in Reconnaissance, on the PRE platform: the activity happens outside your network. The official page is attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1589/001, and the parent overview is T1589 Gather Victim Identity Information.

    Why it matters

    Credentials are the shortest path from research to intrusion. No exploit, no malware, no alarm at the endpoint: the attacker simply logs in as your employee. Every stolen account also becomes a platform for the next step, because the attacker can read mail, reset other accounts, and send new lures from a trusted address.

    The cost difference is extreme. A credential campaign costs almost nothing to run and can be rented as a service, while a single successful account takeover can lead to ransomware. That is why detection engineers treat risky sign-ins and phishing reports as top-priority signals, even though the technique itself is invisible to most sensors.

    Defenses still work. Phishing-resistant MFA, session revocation, and a report button with fast triage remove most of the value of any captured password. This page builds those defenses into concrete detections.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Credential collection is the bridge between identity research and initial access. The parent technique builds the target list; this sub-technique turns list entries into working logins.

    Key fact: why detection is indirect

    ATT&CK's official detection strategy for T1589.001 (DET0813) is indirect: it notes a very high false-positive rate and warns that the activity often happens outside the victim's visibility, so it is best caught at the related Initial Access stage. The phishing site, the leak market, and the mail relay all belong to the attacker, so your SIEM — your central log platform — never sees the capture itself. You detect the side effects: the lookalike domain in certificate logs, the reported email, the risky sign-in minutes later, and the MFA denial burst. Every rule in Section 5 follows this idea.

    Good to know

    The strongest control is not a detection at all. Phishing-resistant MFA — such as passkeys (a login method stored on the device that proves identity with cryptography instead of a shared secret) — means a phished password alone opens nothing. Add easy reporting, and the attack chain breaks before it starts.

    The same story, written out

    Step 1 — Register the lookalike domain

    The attacker registers a typo-squatted domain, for example one that swaps or doubles a letter in the company name, and adds words like sso or login. On it they host a pixel-perfect copy of the Aurelia Pay single sign-on page. From this moment, certificate transparency logs contain the domain — the first thing defenders can see.

    Step 2 — Harvest the employee list

    Using the parent technique, the attacker collects names, roles, and the email format of the finance team from public profiles and old leak data. The finance team goes first, because finance mailboxes see invoices, approvals, and payment details. Nothing in this step touches company systems.

    Step 3 — Send the credential lure

    A freshly rented mail relay delivers a believable security alert to finance staff: confirm your payment account before payroll closes. The link points to the fake SSO page. The mail gateway logs a new sender and a new link domain, which is the first corporate telemetry of the campaign.

    Step 4 — The victim enters the password

    One finance employee clicks the link and types the work password into the fake page. The page looks and behaves exactly like the real one, right down to the error messages. The identity provider has seen nothing yet, so no alert can fire on this step.

    Step 5 — The kit relays and grabs MFA

    The adversary-in-the-middle kit forwards the login to the real identity provider and shows the genuine MFA prompt. The employee approves it, because the prompt matches the page they just used. The kit records the password, the one-time code, and the new session cookie. To the identity provider this looks like an ordinary successful login.

    Step 6 — Sign-in from a new location

    The attacker replays the stolen session cookie through their proxy network and lands inside the mailbox. The identity provider risk engine compares the sign-in with the employee's usual country and device and flags impossible travel. The alert fires — whether anyone reads it in time is another matter.

    Step 7 — The SOC connects the dots

    A second employee reports the phishing email. In the queue it meets the risky sign-in alert for the first employee, sent minutes apart. Two weak signals, correlated, become one strong case: who was targeted, who was taken, and from where the attacker is working.

    Step 8 — Respond and block

    The SOC revokes all sessions for the affected account, forces a password reset, removes the inbox rule the attacker created to hide payment mail, and blocks the lookalike domain at the gateway, the proxy, and DNS. A takedown request shortens the life of the fake portal.

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section explains how professional attackers capture credentials. The goal is defensive understanding: once you know which weak designs they depend on, you can remove them. All examples here are lab-only and use fictional targets.

    When and why attackers use it

    Credential capture is the cheapest bridge from recon to access, and it is low risk for the attacker. They never touch your infrastructure until the final sign-in, which comes from rented proxies. A captured finance mailbox also keeps paying: it reads future invoices and can launch the next fraud from a trusted sender.

    Criminal markets even sell the work as a service. AiTM kits, fresh lookalike domains, and pre-validated password lists are products with support tickets. Defenders should therefore expect the pattern to repeat with new packaging, not expect it to disappear.

    Where it applies and preconditions

    The technique works against any organization with a cloud identity provider, which means effectively all of them. On the victim side, four preconditions decide success: weak MFA design (plain push approvals, no number matching), password reuse from old breaches, users who approve prompts to make them stop, and staff trained to trust any page that looks like the login portal.

    Remove one precondition and the attack usually fails. Remove two and it fails reliably. This is why Section 7 spends most of its space on MFA design and reporting habits.

    How it is performed

    AiTM phishing kits

    An adversary-in-the-middle kit is a fake login page with a proxy inside. The victim logs in on the copy; the kit forwards each request to the real identity provider and records the password, the MFA code, and the session cookie. Modern kits also check the visitor, so researchers and scanners see a harmless page.

    Fake OAuth and SSO consent

    OAuth is the standard that lets one application use another service on your behalf. Attackers register an application named like a known tool and ask an employee to click Allow. One click can hand the attacker a long-lived token that works without a password or any further MFA prompt.

    MFA push fatigue

    With a known password but no second factor, the attacker spams approval prompts, often at night, until a tired or annoyed user presses Approve. Number matching — typing a code from the screen into the app — defeats most of this, which is why attackers prefer targets without it.

    Breach-dump purchases

    Email-password pairs from old breaches of other companies cost little in bulk markets. Attackers replay them against your VPN, mail, and SSO logins, counting on reused passwords. There is no phishing email in this route, so mailbox signals never appear.

    Password spraying

    Spraying tries one weak or leaked password against many accounts, instead of many passwords against one account. Because each account sees only a few tries, lockout rules stay silent. Spray waves often follow a big public breach, when everyone updates the same guessed passwords.

    Smishing portals

    SMS phishing (smishing) sends the same fake portal by text message. Phones sit outside most corporate telemetry, and text links hide behind URL shorteners. The captured credentials end up on the same identity provider, so the risky sign-in signals in Section 5 still apply.

    Lab-only rule

    Credential phishing against real people is a crime in almost every jurisdiction, and creating real phished credentials creates real risk. Practice only in a lab: host fake pages on localhost only, use throwaway accounts such as student@lab.invalid, and never enter a real password into a test page. Never buy or download real stolen credentials, and never point a kit at a real identity provider.

    A safe lab exercise

    Build a fake login page that runs only on localhost — the machine's own loopback address, reachable from nothing else. Copy the layout of your real portal, then show it to two colleagues and ask them to say what feels wrong. Most will find nothing, which is the lesson: users cannot judge a page by its looks, so your controls must not depend on them doing so. Second, take one known phishing email from a training corpus and read its headers in a disconnected sandbox: the sending server, the authentication results, and the reply-to address that does not match the sender. Third, log every step you took and map each step to the signals in Section 5.4. You now understand both sides of the hunt without touching a real credential.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    Variations

    • Password replay — use dump passwords quietly, no lure at all. Hardest to see.
    • Session replay — skip the password, replay the stolen cookie from the AiTM kit.
    • Hybrid vishing — phish first, then call the user as the helpdesk while the prompt is still on their screen.
    • OAuth lures — skip the password entirely and ask for a consent click instead.

    Mistakes attackers make

    • Reusing the same lookalike-domain pattern across victims, so one blocklist entry covers many campaigns.
    • No rate limiting on their own kit, which lets defenders flood it and fingerprint its behavior.
    • Lures too generic — no harvested names or real projects, so success rates collapse.
    • Signing in immediately from a far country, tripping impossible-travel alerts in the first minutes.
    • Letting phishing domains burn: once reported to blocklists and takedown services, a domain is dead within days.

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders never see the capture itself, but a credential campaign must touch your identity provider and your mailboxes to be worth anything. Those touches are visible. This section shows the channels, the artifacts, and the first-hour plan.

    How defenders can see it

    Five channels carry most of the signal. First, people: employees who report suspicious messages, often before any sensor does. Second, the mail gateway: new senders, new link domains, and quarantine records. Third, the identity provider risk engine: impossible travel, anonymizing IP addresses, and new devices. Fourth, outside feeds: certificate transparency logs and leak monitoring. Fifth, your own MFA telemetry: denial bursts that end in one approval.

    What artifacts it leaves

    • The phishing email itself, in mailboxes, quarantines, and the report queue.
    • A newly registered lookalike domain with a valid certificate.
    • Risky sign-ins: impossible travel, anonymizer addresses, new devices.
    • MFA anomalies: several denials followed by one approval.
    • Attacker-created inbox rules that move or delete payment mail.
    • New OAuth consents that nobody on the security team requested.

    Where to look

    • Mail gateway and report queue: sender, links, recipients, verdicts.
    • Identity provider sign-in logs: risk levels, locations, device details.
    • MFA activity logs: denial counts per user per hour.
    • OAuth consent audit: every Consent to application event.
    • Certificate transparency feeds: your brand plus login keywords.
    • Leak monitoring: your domain in fresh dumps.
    • Mailbox audit: inbox rules and delegation changes on sensitive accounts.

    Why it matters and where it fits

    Credential signals are the earliest reliable indicators of a targeted intrusion, because the attacker must log in to profit. Acting in the first hour means ending the campaign before the attacker has read a single invoice. In the ATT&CK chain these signals sit in Reconnaissance (TA0043), one full stage before Initial Access, so every saved minute buys preparation time for the whole incident response process.

    There is also a scoping rule unique to credentials: the attacker's access ends when the session does, not when the password changes. That single fact drives the containment steps below.

    First-hour triage flow

    1. Validate
    Open the reported message in a sandbox, confirm the lookalike domain in certificate logs, and read the risky sign-in details. Is the story consistent?
    2. Scope
    Search the gateway for the same sender and link across all mailboxes. Search the identity provider for other sign-ins from the same IP, device, or session ID.
    3. Contain
    Revoke sessions and refresh tokens first, then force the password reset. Remove attacker-created inbox rules. Block the domain at gateway, proxy, and DNS.
    4. Assess exposure
    List what the mailbox saw while the attacker was inside: approvals, payment details, other staff names. Check OAuth consents granted in the window.
    5. Harden
    Add the lookalike pattern to detections, brief the helpdesk about likely follow-up calls, and confirm number matching is on for the whole tenant.

    This flow fits in an hour when roles are clear. Section 9 repeats it as a checklist you can paste into your incident tool.

    Warning

    Do not treat a password reset as containment on its own. A stolen session cookie and a stolen refresh token stay valid after the password changes, so the attacker can quietly re-enter. Revoke sessions and tokens together with the reset, every time, and verify with fresh sign-in logs that no new session appears.

    5.3 Threat ranking and categorization

    Below are the realistic credential-collection threats for a typical mid-size organization. Ranking combines likelihood (how often it happens) with impact (how bad it gets). Every detection you build should trace back to one of these rows, so the ranking also becomes your detection backlog.

    TH-1 — AiTM credential and MFA capture Critical

    Description: an adversary-in-the-middle kit copies the real login page, relays the authentication to the identity provider, and records the password, the MFA code, and the session cookie in one flow.

    Preconditions: a target list from identity recon; a lookalike domain with a valid certificate; MFA that a user will complete on the attacker's page.

    Impact: full account takeover even with MFA enabled, because the stolen session cookie works without a second prompt; mailbox access leads to fraud from a trusted sender.

    Justification: the dominant pattern in public incident reports since AiTM kits became commodity services; highest likelihood of success per sent lure.

    TH-2 — MFA push fatigue High

    Description: the attacker, already holding the password, spams MFA approval prompts until a tired or annoyed user accepts one, often at night.

    Preconditions: a valid password from phishing or a dump; push-based MFA without number matching; users who believe prompts are routine.

    Impact: direct account access; the approval is the only step the attacker needs, and it happens on the victim's own device.

    Justification: cheap, proven, and visible in logs as a denial burst — yet many tenants still lack number matching, so the exposure is real.

    TH-3 — Breach-dump password replay High

    Description: staff email-password pairs from third-party breaches are replayed against your VPN, mail, and SSO logins, in quiet trials or in spray waves.

    Preconditions: a staff address in any old dump; reused passwords; no unique per-service passwords and no leaked-credential screening.

    Impact: silent access with a valid password and no phishing email to warn you; often discovered weeks later, if at all.

    Justification: independent of user awareness — one reused password from years ago is enough, so the risk persists even in well-trained teams.

    TH-4 — Malicious OAuth application consent High

    Description: the attacker registers an application with a trustworthy name and asks an employee to click Allow, receiving a token that reads mail or files without any password.

    Preconditions: tenant settings that allow user consent to third-party apps; employees who approve consent screens without review.

    Impact: long-lived access that survives password resets, because the token is separate from the credential; easy data theft through APIs.

    Justification: a well-documented escalation used by both criminal and state groups; often missed because consent events hide in audit logs.

    TH-5 — Executive and finance-team phishing for BEC High

    Description: business email compromise (BEC) crews phish finance staff and executives specifically, to read approvals and redirect payments from inside the mailbox.

    Preconditions: harvested org-chart knowledge; credible lures around payroll, invoices, or deal deadlines; users who act on urgent finance mail.

    Impact: direct financial loss through payment redirection; regulatory and customer fallout when funds cannot be recovered.

    Justification: BEC remains one of the costliest attack classes measured by financial loss, and it starts with exactly this sub-technique.

    TH-6 — Smishing with a fake portal Medium

    Description: the same fake portals delivered by text message, often with URL shorteners and urgent payment or parcel stories, aimed at credentials and payment details.

    Preconditions: a phone number list; a fake portal already built for TH-1; staff reachable on personal devices.

    Impact: captured credentials with no mail-gateway signal at all; personal-device compromise risks for executives.

    Justification: lower volume than email phishing, but the mobile channel bypasses most corporate telemetry, which raises the residual risk.

    S1 — Risky successful sign-in with a new device or anonymizer

    Mapping
    Same logic in both languages: success, then anonymizer OR risk-plus-new-device. SPL groups per user so analysts see the whole story in one row.
    Assumptions
    index=identity sourcetype="azure:aad:signin" from your cloud identity add-on. Field names RiskLevelAggregated and RiskEventTypes_v2 follow the Microsoft schema. IsNewDevice is a normalized field: your log pipeline derives it from deviceDetail.isNew in the raw sign-in event, as the example above shows. Rename all three to match your ingestion.
    False pos.
    Travel on new devices, privacy relays, and mobile-carrier geolocation errors. Enrich with an approved-travel calendar lookup.
    Tuning
    Suppress verified-benign users for 24 hours; consider auto-revoking sessions on anonymizer sign-ins if your policy allows.
    Severity
    High — this is often the first successful step after credential capture.

    S2 — MFA push fatigue

    Mapping
    Sigma counts denial events per user in a one-hour window. A Sigma aggregation cannot also require the later approval, so the SPL adds it: one-hour buckets, denials plus approvals per user. An analyst confirms the same pattern in the raw events.
    Assumptions
    Your pipeline writes an MfaResult field with values denied and approved into the sign-in stream (the Azure AD sign-in details carry MFA status). Map your vendor's exact field name and values.
    False pos.
    A user fumbling notifications, or MFA testing on shared accounts. Exclude rollout-team accounts during migrations.
    Tuning
    Start with 8 denials per hour; lower only after weeks of clean data. Enabling number matching removes most of the attack class itself.
    Severity
    High — an approval after a denial burst is very likely an attacker, not the user.

    S3 — One phishing domain reported by several users

    Mapping
    Sigma counts report events per link domain in a 24-hour window; the Sigma language cannot count distinct values, so the SPL adds the stricter form: rex extracts the domain and dc(UserId) requires three or more different reporters. Use the SPL form where your SIEM supports it.
    Assumptions
    Your reporting pipeline copies the original link into a Url field (most security-awareness and gateway integrations do). If not, extract it from the message body in your pipeline first and state that in the rule.
    False pos.
    Simulation and training emails. Exclude your awareness-test sender domain explicitly.
    Tuning
    Group lookalike domains by family (brand plus keyword) so a campaign cannot evade by rotating subdomains; keep the daily bucket, which is wide enough for slow reporters.
    Severity
    High — three or more independent humans reporting one domain means an active wave.

    S4 — User consent to an untrusted OAuth application

    Mapping
    Both match the consent operation and drop admin consents (ConsentType: AllPrincipals), which follow a change process.
    Assumptions
    M365 unified audit logs land in index=o365 sourcetype="o365:management:activity" with Operation and ConsentType fields. In Microsoft Purview the event is called Consent to application, workload AzureActiveDirectory.
    False pos.
    Legitimate SaaS tools consented by their owners. Keep an allowlist of reviewed application IDs and subtract it.
    Tuning
    Pair the alert with a tenant policy that blocks user consent for unverified apps; then the alert mostly catches policy exceptions and direct API abuse.
    Severity
    High — one click grants token access that survives password resets.

    S5 — Company domain in a credential dump feed

    Mapping
    Same single-match logic; SPL summarizes volume, affected users, and the first and last sighting.
    Assumptions
    Leak feeds vary by product, so point the query at your feed's index and rename fields such as Email and BreachName to match its schema. Replace aureliapay.example with your own domain.
    False pos.
    Aggregators re-reporting old breaches. Keep a record of handled incidents and dedupe on the breach name.
    Tuning
    Route to a playbook: identify affected users, check recent auth anomalies for them, and force resets where reuse is likely.
    Severity
    High — dumped credentials are the shortest silent path from recon to access.

    S6 — Password spraying against the identity provider

    Mapping
    Sigma counts failed sign-ins per source address in a 15-minute window. Sigma cannot count distinct users, so the SPL adds the stronger check: distinct targeted users per source in 15-minute buckets, which separates spraying (many users) from plain brute force (one user). Both fire on a spray wave.
    Assumptions
    ResultType 50126 means invalid username or password, and 50053 means the account was locked or sign-in blocked — the two codes spraying produces. Rename codes if you ingest from another identity provider.
    False pos.
    One broken device retrying one account will not match, but a shared NAT exit can. Enrich the IP with known office ranges and service accounts.
    Tuning
    Lower the distinct-user threshold only with smart lockout enabled; otherwise you will lock out more users than the attacker does.
    Severity
    Medium — preparation and noise, but a success after a spray wave is the real emergency.

    Validation note

    Test every rule before production: replay a sample event from Section 5.4 against the query, confirm it fires, then confirm a known-good event does not. Record the test in your detection inventory with a date and an owner. A rule without a test is a hypothesis, not a detection.

    Real-World Case Study

    The story below is a composite: the fictional company Aurelia Pay, with a timeline assembled from patterns that recur in public incident reports of adversary-in-the-middle credential campaigns. It shows how a finance-team credential capture became an attempted payment fraud, and which signals could have ended it sooner.

    Context

    Aurelia Pay is a fictional payments company with about 600 employees and a cloud identity provider with push-based MFA, but without number matching. Security has five people. The finance team of nine works with monthly supplier payments, and the public website lists every finance role with full names. No leak monitoring and no domain monitoring were in place at the start of this story.

    Attack chain at a glance

    Stage 1
    Identity recon: finance roles harvested from public pages (parent technique)
    Stage 2
    Lookalike domain registered and a fake SSO portal built with an AiTM kit
    Stage 3
    Phishing email to the finance team: confirm your payment account
    Stage 4
    Password, MFA approval, and session cookie captured through the kit
    Stage 5
    Inbox rule hides payment mail; attacker reads approvals and prepares a redirect

    Timeline

    TimeEventVisible signal at the time
    Day 0–6Attacker profiles the finance team from the public site and professional networks.Nothing on company systems; the site itself was the source.
    Day 7, 21:40Lookalike domain with a doubled letter and -sso gets its first certificate.Certificate transparency logs published it within hours; nobody watched.
    Day 8, 09:12Phishing email reaches all nine finance mailboxes, styled as a payment-account security check.Gateway saw a brand-new sender domain; no rule covered new lookalike senders.
    Day 8, 09:31One finance employee opens the link, logs in, and approves the real MFA prompt.The kit captures password, code, and session cookie; the IdP logs a normal success.
    Day 8, 09:52Attacker replays the session from a proxy in another country and creates an inbox rule that moves mail with the word invoice to an archive folder.Risk engine raises a medium-risk alert for impossible travel; it lands in a backlog. The rule change is in the mailbox audit log.
    Day 9–10Attacker reads supplier threads, learns the approval process, and drafts a payment-redirect message from a lookalike supplier domain.Mailbox audit shows unusual search and forwarding-activity patterns; not monitored.
    Day 11, 08:55A second finance employee notices their archived invoice mail and reports the original phishing email to security.First human report, three days after capture.
    Day 11, 09:40SOC correlates the report with the Day 8 risky sign-in alert and starts the incident.Two signals, one case; the session is still alive.
    Day 11, 10:15–12:00Containment: all sessions revoked, tokens invalidated, password reset, inbox rule removed, lookalike domain blocked and reported for takedown.Attacker access ends before any payment moves.

    The detection point that mattered

    Four signals existed before the report: a new lookalike certificate on Day 7, a brand-new sender domain on Day 8, a medium-risk impossible-travel alert minutes after capture, and a mailbox-rule change in the audit log. Any one of them, triaged on the day it fired, would have caught the account while the attacker was still reading. The report worked because a human noticed something small — archived invoice mail. Detections make that noticing automatic.

    Containment

    Sessions and refresh tokens for the affected account were revoked first, then the password reset, so the replayed cookie died with the session. The inbox rule was removed, OAuth grants were reviewed, and the lookalike domain was blocked at gateway, proxy, and DNS and sent to the takedown service.

    Eradication

    The team confirmed the attacker created no other rules, rules on other finance mailboxes, or new consents. Supplier contacts were warned about the attempted redirect, and the fake portal was verified dead from three networks. No malware was involved, so no reimaging was needed.

    Recovery

    The affected employee regained access with a re-enrolled, phishing-resistant authenticator. Finance switched the next payment run to dual approval out-of-band confirmation, and monitoring for that supplier thread continued for thirty days. Normal operations resumed the same week.

    Impact and consequences

    No money left the company, which makes this a success story only in hindsight. The costs were still real: about 20 staff-days of response, a payment run delayed by 48 hours, a supplier relationship that needed explaining, and the purchase of domain and leak monitoring after the fact. The retrospective note read: the attacker understood our approval flow better than two of our own new hires.

    Team response and communication

    Five people ran a clean model: one incident commander, one communicator, one scribe, two investigators. The finance director was briefed in plain language twice a day, the reporting employee was thanked publicly, and a short note went to all staff with one screenshot of the fake page. Clear roles, not tools, kept the response calm.

    Lessons learned

    • Number matching was enabled within the week; push-approval MFA invites fatigue attacks regardless of user training.
    • Certificate and sender-domain monitoring would have given three days of warning for the price of one feed.
    • Session revocation before password reset is now written into the runbook, with verification steps.
    • Mailbox audit logs (rule changes, forwarding, delegation) belong in the SIEM for every finance account.
    • The report button worked; triage of risk alerts needed the same hourly standard as reports.
    • Out-of-band payment verification (a callback to a known number) turned payment fraud from likely to unlikely.

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop an attacker from hosting a fake login page. Mitigation works in three layers: make captured credentials worthless, cut the capture channels, and watch the places where the campaign must touch you. ATT&CK lists no mitigations for T1589.001 itself — which is exactly why a layered plan matters.

    Home and small-office advice

    • Use a password manager so every site has a different password; one leak then cannot unlock the rest.
    • Turn on the strongest MFA each service offers — passkeys or an authenticator app with number matching, not SMS when you can avoid it.
    • Check your email address on breach-notification services and change any reused password immediately.
    • Before approving an MFA prompt, ask: did I just try to log in? If not, deny it and change the password — it means someone has it.
    • Never approve a login you did not start, no matter how many times the prompt appears.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    Most blue teams do not lose to the phishing kit itself. They lose to ownership, tuning, and follow-through problems. Each mistake below shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from returning.

    1. Dismissing phishing reports as noise

    SymptomReports wait days for triage; users stop reporting and start deleting instead.

    Root causeThe report queue has no owner and no service level, so it becomes a mailbox nobody reads.

    FixRoute reports into the SIEM as events, assign an analyst queue, and set a one-hour triage target.

    PreventionMeasure time-to-triage weekly; in the case study it was the signal that finally worked.

    2. Password reset without session revocation

    SymptomThe attacker returns hours after the reset, using the same stolen session cookie.

    Root causeThe runbook treats reset as containment and forgets tokens outlive passwords.

    FixRevoke sessions and refresh tokens first, then reset, then verify with fresh sign-in logs.

    PreventionAutomate revoke-then-reset as one workflow; audit a sample of resets monthly.

    3. Trusting SMS MFA

    SymptomAccounts protected only by text-message codes keep getting taken over.

    Root causeSMS is readable by SIM-swap fraud and the codes are relayable by AiTM kits like passwords.

    FixMove staff to authenticator apps with number matching, then to passkeys or FIDO2 keys.

    PreventionTrack the MFA-method mix monthly and retire SMS as a factor, not just de-prioritize it.

    4. Alert fatigue on risky sign-ins

    SymptomImpossible-travel alerts are auto-closed as user traveling, including real attacks.

    Root causeEarly noise was never tuned with context, so analysts stopped trusting the rule.

    FixAdd a travel-calendar lookup, split severity by risk level, and require a closure reason.

    PreventionMonthly tuning review with closure-reason statistics, as in Section 5.5 rule S1.

    5. No leak or domain monitoring

    SymptomYou learn about dumped staff credentials or a fake portal from customers or journalists.

    Root causeExternal monitoring was classified as nice to have and never budgeted.

    FixSubscribe to certificate-transparency matching and a leak feed, and wire both into rules S5 and the takedown playbook.

    PreventionQuarterly exposure review that covers dumps, domain registrations, and phishing-URL trackers.

    6. Sigma rules that were never tested

    SymptomA rule fires on everything, or worse, never fires at all.

    Root causeRules were written once and deployed without replaying real events.

    FixReplay the sample events from Section 5.4 against each rule; fix logic until both sides pass.

    PreventionKeep detections in version control with one test event per rule and an owner.

    7. Missing OAuth consent audit logs

    SymptomNobody can say which third-party applications have access to mail and files.

    Root causeConsent events were never ingested, so rule S4 had nothing to read.

    FixEnable AzureActiveDirectory audit ingestion and backfill the current grant list.

    PreventionQuarterly log-source review that walks every SaaS in use, not just servers.

    8. Detections without owners

    SymptomRules break silently after a schema change; nobody notices for months.

    Root causeDetections were treated as a one-time project, not maintained products.

    FixCreate a detection inventory with an owner, a review date, and a test status for each rule.

    PreventionOwnership review as part of the quarterly detection health check.

    9. Measuring only simulation success

    SymptomSimulation click rates look great while real phishing still captures accounts.

    Root causeUsers learned to spot simulated lures, not real ones; the metric rewards gaming.

    FixTrack real report rates and time-to-triage as the primary program metrics.

    PreventionReport simulation and real metrics together to leadership, always.

    10. Thresholds too high for slow attacks

    SymptomA seven-denial MFA burst, or a spray of nine users, passes under thresholds of 8 and 10.

    Root causeFixed numbers chosen once, never re-tested against low-and-slow behavior.

    FixUse adaptive baselines and add a secondary low-and-slow rule for trusted-device patterns.

    PreventionTest rules at low volumes; attackers read public write-ups of your thresholds too.

    11. Helpdesk verification that exists only on paper

    SymptomReset requests from a confident caller are processed anyway, and MFA gets re-enrolled.

    Root causeThe verification script is optional in the ticketing tool and audits never check it.

    FixMake the verification fields mandatory in the ticket workflow; block approval without them.

    PreventionMonthly spot audits of reset tickets with feedback to the desk.

    Habits that keep you ready

    Daily

    • Triage every phishing report within the target time.
    • Review risky sign-in alerts with fresh eyes, checking device and location.
    • Check overnight MFA denial bursts and new consent events.

    Weekly

    • Review certificate-transparency and leak-feed matches and their follow-up.
    • Confirm the session-revocation runbook still matches current tenant settings.
    • Scan mailbox-audit logs of finance accounts for new rules or forwarding.

    Monthly

    • Validate one third of the Section 5.5 rules against sample events.
    • Review OAuth grants and remove anything unexplained.
    • Search the web as an attacker would: your brand plus sso, login, pay.

    Response checklist

    • Validate: sandbox the reported email, confirm the lookalike domain, read the risky sign-in details.
    • Scope: search mailboxes, sign-ins, and MFA logs for the same sender, domain, IP, and session.
    • Contain: revoke sessions and tokens first, then reset the password, and verify no new session appears.
    • Clean: remove attacker inbox rules, delegation, and OAuth grants; check who received payment mail.
    • Block: gateway, proxy, and DNS for the lookalike domain; start the takedown playbook.
    • Follow up: check for password-spray patterns from the same infrastructure, then tune rules and record the campaign.
