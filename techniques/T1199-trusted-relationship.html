T1199 – Trusted Relationship

🎯Tactic: Initial Access (TA0001) 💻Platforms: IaaS · Identity Provider · Linux · Office Suite · SaaS · Windows · macOS 🧩Complexity: Low 🔍Detection difficulty: High

Some attackers do not break down the wall. They walk through a door that a partner has already opened. Trusted Relationship (T1199) means an attacker gets into your company by first taking over, or abusing, a third party that already has legitimate access to your systems. Think of an IT services contractor, a managed service provider (MSP) that runs your servers, a refrigeration company with remote access for billing, a cloud reseller with delegated admin rights, or a federated partner identity. The stolen account is real. The password works. Security tools may see it as normal business traffic, so alarms stay quiet. ATT&CK describes this as breaching or leveraging organizations that have access to the intended victim, because their connection often gets less scrutiny. The most famous example is Target in 2013: attackers used credentials stolen from Fazio Mechanical, a small HVAC contractor, to enter Target's network and steal about 40 million payment card numbers. In this guide you will learn how the trick works, what it looks like in logs, and how to close that door without hurting the business.

Think like the attacker

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

3.1 When attackers use this

Attackers pick Trusted Relationship when the victim's own front door is hard to break. A big company may have strong email security, patching and MFA (multi-factor authentication, a second login proof) for staff. But its partners are often smaller, with weaker security. The attacker also gets a head start: they inherit a live connection instead of building one. This technique usually appears early in an attack, at Initial Access, but the work starts weeks before. The attacker first builds a list of partners: MSPs, IT contractors, HVAC and elevator firms, staffing agencies, law firms, auditors, cloud resellers. Public sources help here: press releases about partnerships, job ads that name tools, certificate transparency logs (public records of website certificates), and even Shodan searches for exposed remote management portals.

Timing matters. A vendor VPN login at 2 a.m. from an unknown city is odd; the same login during the contracted maintenance window is normal. That is why patient attackers study the pattern first, sometimes for weeks, before they touch anything. When the logon finally comes, it looks almost right. CISA has warned for years that attackers target IT service providers precisely because one provider can open many customers at once.

3.2 How attackers do it, step by step

  1. Map the partner landscape. Collect every company with a reason to connect: vendors, MSPs, resellers, auditors. Look for partner portals, VPN portals and remote support links on the victim's domain.
  2. Pick the weakest partner. A 20-person contractor with no security team is a better target than the victim. Old VPN software, no MFA, or a public RMM instance are strong signals.
  3. Get into the partner. Spear phishing works well, and so does buying credentials from an infostealer market (malware that steals saved passwords from infected computers). The partner's staff reuse passwords, so one leak can be enough.
  4. Harvest the relationship. Inside the partner, attackers read email and shared drives for VPN instructions, service accounts, jump-host names, IP ranges and maintenance windows.
  5. Log into the victim with the real account. The vendor VPN or remote support tool authenticates them. MFA may not exist at all, or push approval is part of the vendor's daily routine.
  6. Check what the account may touch. The contract usually says "billing servers only". The network often says more. Attackers scan from the vendor zone to learn the truth.
  7. Move across the gap. A missing firewall rule, a flat subnet or an exposed jump host carries them from the vendor lane into the rest of the company.
  8. Do the mission. From there it is ordinary intrusion work: discovery, credential theft, data collection or ransomware — hidden inside trusted traffic.

3.3 Where it works best

🏢MSP-managed estates

One MSP toolset can reach hundreds of small companies. Breaking the MSP is breaking them all, as the Kaseya VSA incident of 2021 showed.

🧊Facility vendors

HVAC, refrigeration, elevator and security firms often keep remote access for years. Nobody remembers why it exists, so nobody removes it.

☁️Cloud and SaaS resellers

Delegated admin permissions in Microsoft 365 let a partner manage customer tenants. A stolen partner login is a master key to many tenants.

🏦Identity providers

Groups like LAPSUS$ and Sea Turtle attacked identity and DNS providers because one compromise redirects trust for many victims.

🏭Suppliers with site links

Factories and logistics firms sometimes run permanent site-to-site links to suppliers. Sandworm Team abused exactly such dedicated connections.

📬Finance-heavy chains

Where invoices flow, look-alike domains follow. A vendor mailbox compromise can turn into fake bank-change requests and fraud.

3.4 Why attackers like this technique

  • The account is real. It passes VPN, MFA and geo checks that stop fake accounts, because the checks are aimed at outsiders, not at partners.
  • One successful phishing mail against a small partner can open a large, well-defended victim. The effort-to-reward ratio is excellent.
  • Logs look like business. VPN sessions from the vendor at billing time match what the SOC expects, so alerts never fire.
  • Recovery is hard: you cannot simply block the partner without stopping operations, so attackers keep a stable channel for weeks.
  • Scale: one MSP or reseller compromise multiplies to many victims, which is why ransomware groups hunt providers.

3.5 What attackers need first

  • A list of the victim's partners and the services they reach (OSINT — public-source information — plus press releases and job ads).
  • Access to one partner identity: phished password, infostealer logs bought online, or a password reused from another breach.
  • Knowledge of the remote entry path: VPN portal URL, RMM tool, jump host or federated SSO link.
  • Patience and discipline to keep traffic volumes and hours inside the vendor's normal pattern.
  • Standard intrusion tooling afterwards: remote access software, credential dumpers, and living-off-the-land commands (normal system tools misused by attackers).

Ethics and law

Everything in this section is for learning and defense. Testing any system you do not own, or a partner's system without written permission, breaks computer misuse laws in most countries and can cost you your job or your freedom. Even "just checking" a vendor portal is illegal access. Get a signed scope, use lab machines and test accounts, and share findings with the blue team. Attack knowledge is only useful when it makes defenders stronger.

Think like the defender

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

4.1 What defenders can see

The attacker arrives with a real account, so the strong "bad login" signals are missing. What stays visible is behavior that does not fit the contract. A vendor account signs in at 3 a.m. instead of the billing window. It logs in from a new country. It touches more servers than ever before. A remote management tool appears on a server that never had one. A B2B guest identity (a partner user invited into your tenant) that normally reads two folders suddenly opens the Azure portal. None of these events is an attack on its own, but together they draw a clear line. Defenders win by joining these weak signals across log sources instead of waiting for one loud alarm.

4.3 Where to look

  • VPN concentrator and firewall logs: every partner logon with source IP, time and duration.
  • Domain controller Security logs: 4624/4625 for partner accounts, 4672 for the rights they get, 4732/4728 for group changes.
  • Sysmon / EDR: new remote management software, service installs (7045), unexpected network sessions from vendor hosts.
  • Entra ID / M365: guest sign-ins, delegated admin relationship changes, app consents, mailbox permission grants.
  • Mail gateway: look-alike domains and auth failures on threads that mention invoices or payments.
  • Cloud audit trails: AssumeRole, federation and API-key use from partner accounts in AWS/Azure/GCP.
  • Asset and contract data: a list of every third party with access, what they may touch, and when their access expires.

4.4 Why this matters to the SOC

A trusted-relationship breach hits twice. First, the direct damage: stolen card data, ransomware delivered through the MSP tool, or fraud paid out from a fake invoice. Second, the damage spreads through trust: customers, regulators and auditors ask why you let a partner reach your most important systems. The Kaseya VSA event of 2021 touched roughly 1,500 downstream businesses through one provider. For the SOC (the security team that watches alerts) this means vendor accounts deserve their own baselines, their own alert rules and their own review calendar. A vendor login is not just another user login; it is a contract boundary moving in your network.

4.5 Where it fits in the attack chain

Before this technique, attackers do Reconnaissance (T1595, T1592, T1598) and often Phishing (T1566) against the partner, or buy credentials that map to Valid Accounts (T1078). T1199 itself is the Initial Access moment: the first foothold inside the victim. After it comes Discovery (T1087, T1046), then Privilege Escalation and Lateral Movement (T1021 series), Collection (T1005, T1056), Exfiltration (T1041, T1567), and Impact (T1486 ransomware, T1530 data destruction). Note the chain trick: the same trusted channel often serves again later for C2 and exfiltration, because nobody dares to block the partner mid-contract.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

Where threat intel comes from

🌐OSINT sources

Vendor blogs (Microsoft, CrowdStrike, Secureworks), CISA advisories, abuse.ch feeds, AlienVault OTX, and Shodan for finding exposed partner portals and RMM servers. These tell you which MSP tools and VPN brands are under attack this month.

🎯ATT&CK-based intel

The MITRE ATT&CK page for T1199 lists real users of this technique: APT28, APT29, GOLD SOUTHFIELD, HAFNIUM, LAPSUS$, menuPass, POLONIUM, RedCurl, Sandworm Team, Sea Turtle, Threat Group-3390 and VOID MANTICORE, plus the SolarWinds Compromise campaign. Their pages link the original reports.

📡Commercial and sector feeds

ISAC membership (FS-ISAC, H-ISAC, retail ISACs) gives sector-specific partner abuse reports. Commercial feeds add partner-tenant indicators, such as known rogue reseller tenants or abused RMM infrastructure.

🏠Internal telemetry

Your own VPN logs, Entra ID guest lists, and contract register. The most valuable intel for T1199 is internal: the baseline of what each partner normally does, and the list of who currently holds access.

The intel cycle for this technique

  1. Define priorities. Rank partner types by how many victims one breach can reach: MSPs with RMM tools first, then federated identities, then facility vendors.
  2. Collect. Pull CISA/Microsoft advisories, ATT&CK group updates, ISAC notes, and internal partner-access inventory changes.
  3. Process. Tag each report with T1199 and the exact log source it implies (VPN, RMM, identity, cloud).
  4. Analyze. Match reported behavior against your partner baseline: does any vendor account already show these patterns?
  5. Produce. Turn findings into hunts and Sigma rules; brief the SOC on how the new actor works in 15 minutes, not 50 slides.
  6. Feedback. After each quarter, record which detections fired, which partners changed, and refresh priorities.

How to apply intel here

  • Pivot every new MSP/RMM or partner-compromise report into a hunt: do you run that tool, do you have that partner, and what did their accounts do this month?
  • Watch infrastructure named in reports: rogue partner tenants, look-alike domains, known abused RMM servers — blocklist and alert.
  • Join your sector ISAC and CISA advisories; partner abuse often hits one sector in waves (retail, healthcare, local government).
  • Tag detections with actor context: a vendor VPN alert annotated with "APT29-style delegated admin abuse" changes the SOC response speed.
  • Feed intel back into contracts: when a wave targets your ERP vendor type, ask that vendor for evidence of hardening before renewal.

Threat #1 — Vendor VPN / RDP account abuse

An attacker with stolen vendor credentials connects to the victim's SSL VPN from an unknown external IP, outside the contracted maintenance window. Threat #5 — Vendor service account lateral movement

The contract says the vendor touches two billing servers. Windows network logons (Event 4624, LogonType 3) tell you what really happens. A vendor account authenticating from its own NAT zone to the billing servers is normal. Least privilege decays silently. A helper account becomes local admin “just for one project” and stays for years. Event 4732 records every addition to a security-enabled local group, so vendor names appearing in privileged groups are visible — and auditable — the moment they happen.

5.5 Sigma Rules & Splunk SPL

One Sigma rule and one matching Splunk SPL query per threat (T1–T10). Every rule keeps the unique ID, tags the technique, and names its main false positive. Drop the UUIDs and tune the allow-lists to your own partner inventory before production. Sigma |startswith, |endswith and |contains modifiers map to wildcards in SPL.

Learn from a real incident

Real-World Case Study

This is a real, well-documented incident. In November 2013, attackers entered Target — then one of the largest US retailers — through Fazio Mechanical Services, a small refrigeration and HVAC contractor in Minnesota. Fazio had remote network access to Target for billing and invoicing. The attackers first took over Fazio, then used its credentials to walk into Target. They reached the payment card network, installed RAM-scraping malware on checkout registers, and stole roughly 40 million payment card numbers plus about 70 million customer records. The story became the textbook example of Trusted Relationship abuse, and every detail below comes from public reporting: the Bloomberg Businessweek reconstruction, court filings, and state attorney-general settlements.

Why is it famous? Because Target had strong security on paper — and even had working intrusion alerts fire during the intrusion. The failure was not one broken tool. It was a chain: a weak partner, an over-trusted connection, a segmentation gap, and an alert process that did not connect the dots. It cost the CIO her job in March 2014 and the CEO his in May 2014.

How the attack chain worked

Phish the contractor→ Steal vendor credentials→ Trusted Relationship (T1199): vendor portal login→ Citrix portal lateral reach→ POS network via segmentation gap→ BlackPOS RAM scraping→ Exfiltration→ Fraud

Notice the trick at the center: every stage after the phishing reused something Target had intentionally given to Fazio — the account, the portal, the network route. Attackers did not need a single exploit against Target's own systems.

How it was detected

The uncomfortable truth: detection technology worked. FireEye alerts fired on November 30, within days of the malware landing. Symantec endpoint tools raised flags too. The sensors detected the movement; the human system did not. Public reconstruction says alerts were reviewed and dismissed in the rush before Black Friday, and the escalation path to a real investigation took days. Weeks later, it was the victims' banks — seeing millions of fraudulent card charges traced to one retailer — that forced the issue with the Secret Service. Detection, in the end, came from outside. The lesson is not "buy more sensors". It is: partner-account anomalies need a named owner, a written escalation path, and rehearsed follow-through, or even a correct alert dies in a queue.

Containment

  1. Remove the POS malware and re-image every affected register; disable the compromised vendor credentials and VPN access immediately.
  2. Engage external incident response and forensics; preserve registers and logs as evidence.
  3. Notify the US Secret Service and card brands; brief law enforcement before public disclosure.
  4. Work with acquiring banks and issuers to block and reissue the exposed cards at scale.
  5. Freeze and review every third-party connection while the investigation maps the true path.

Recovery

  1. Accelerate chip-and-PIN card rollout across all US stores to blunt re-use of stolen magnetic-stripe data.
  2. Rebuild the POS environment with stronger segmentation between vendor access, store networks and the cardholder data environment.
  3. Overhaul monitoring: full-time alert review coverage, clearer escalation, and a new CISO role reporting with authority.
  4. Re-verify every vendor's security posture; set contractual security terms and access reviews for the future.

Who did what

  • SOC L1 analysts: received the FireEye and endpoint alerts; triaged them as low priority during Black Friday prep and did not escalate in time.
  • Incident response lead: once engaged, drove malware removal, evidence preservation and law-enforcement coordination under intense time pressure.
  • Threat intel / forensics teams: mapped the intrusion path from Fazio to POS, identified the malware family, and fed indicators to the industry.
  • Comms and legal: managed disclosure sequencing, card-brand negotiations, litigation and the state AG settlement.
  • Executives and board: owned the accountability outcome — CIO resignation in March 2014, CEO resignation in May 2014 — and funded the security rebuild.

Lessons learned

  • Vendor least privilege is real security: a billing vendor should never be able to touch a payment network. Enforce it in the network, not just the contract.
  • Segmentation between third-party access zones and crown jewels (POS/cardholder data) would have stopped the theft even after entry.
  • Alerts need an escalation path with owners and deadlines — an unescalated true positive equals a missed detection.
  • Third-party risk management must include the partner's own security state (Fazio had malware) and not just questionnaires.
  • Time-box and monitor vendor credentials; rotate them per contract, and kill them the day work ends.
  • Joint exercises with critical partners (purple team, tabletop) find trust gaps before attackers do.
Why this case is pure T1199

No Target password was phished, no Target firewall was broken, no Target exploit was used. Entry came through a relationship — an HVAC contractor's legitimate remote access — exactly what T1199 describes: breaching an organization that has access to the intended victim. Every Sigma rule in Section 5.5 exists because of this case: vendor VPN anomalies (T1), cross-segment logons (T5), privilege creep (T6), and stale partner accounts (T9) are the paths this intrusion would leave in today's logs.

Layered defenses that work

Mitigation & Defense

Defense in depth

One control never stops a trusted-relationship attack alone. The goal: make vendor access visible, limited, time-boxed and revocable, so even a compromised partner stays small and gets noticed.

Enterprise defenses

🔐MFA for every third party

Require phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication (FIDO2 keys or certificate-based) for all vendor VPN, RDP, federated SSO and delegated admin access. ATT&CK lists M1032 Multi-factor Authentication for this technique. No exceptions for "just the billing vendor".

🧱Segmentation for partner zones

Put every vendor connection in its own fenced network zone (VLAN/VRF) with explicit allow-lists to only the contracted systems. ATT&CK M1030 Network Segmentation. Target's POS network should have been unreachable from the HVAC lane — make that true in your estate.

🗄PAM vaulting and time-boxing

Store vendor credentials in a privileged access management (PAM) vault; check out per session, record every keystroke on jump hosts, auto-expire at contract end. Standing vendor passwords are an attacker's favorite raw material.

👤Account lifecycle (offboarding)

ATT&CK M1018 User Account Management: unique IDs per vendor person, automatic disable on contract end, quarterly access recertification signed by the business owner. Kills the stale-account threat (T9) before the SOC ever needs a rule for it.

📜Third-party risk program

Security questionnaires at onboarding, evidence-based reviews at renewal, contractual security terms (MFA, logging, breach notification), and risk tiers based on how much each partner can reach. This is PCI DSS 12.8.x and ISO A.5.19 made operational.

📡Monitor the trust lane

Ship every partner logon to the SIEM and run the 5.5 detections: vendor VPN anomalies, RMM installs, cross-segment 4624s, delegated admin changes. A partner alert should page like any other — with a named owner.

🎓Train the partners

ATT&CK M1017 User Training extends to your vendors: phishing-awareness content, secure-mail practices for invoice threads, and a 24/7 contact for "we think our staff clicked". Their inbox is your front door.

🧯Response readiness

Write the playbook before you need it: disable a partner in minutes, isolate vendor VLANs, and rehearse a "partner compromised" tabletop drill (a talk-through exercise, no live systems) twice a year with procurement and legal in the room.

Home user defenses

You are also someone else's third party — a freelancer, a family IT helper, a small supplier. Simple steps cut your risk of being the door:

  • Turn on MFA everywhere, starting with email and any client portal login.
  • Keep one separate account per client; never share passwords across them.
  • Use a password manager so one leak does not cascade.
  • Update the router, the laptop and every remote-access tool you run.
  • Do not save client VPN credentials in the browser; delete them when a job ends.
  • Think before you click on invoices and "banking detail change" mails — verify by phone.
  • Run antivirus/EDR and let it update automatically.
  • When a job ends, email the client and ask them to disable your access — and confirm it.

Best practices for day-to-day SOC work

  1. Build the partner inventory first. Every hunt and rule in this guide needs it: who has access, to what, since when, expiring when. No inventory, no baseline.
  2. Run hypothesis hunts monthly. Pick one threat from 5.3 (rotate T1–T10) and hunt it across all sources for the last 30 days, even with no alert firing.
  3. Purple-team the Sigma rules. In a lab, replay the exact log lines from 5.4 and confirm each rule in 5.5 fires; fix the ones that do not.
  4. Enrich, then page. Attach contract scope, maintenance calendar and last-90-days session history to partner alerts so the responder decides in seconds, not hours.
  5. Close the loop with procurement. Every confirmed third-party anomaly should update the vendor's risk tier, contract terms, or access scope.
  6. Measure coverage and MTTD. Track what percentage of partner-access sources ship logs, and how long it takes from first anomalous event to page. Improve one number per quarter.
  7. Document the boring things. Offboarding dates, NAT ranges, delegated admin tenants, jump-host owners — this metadata is what makes every future alert meaningful.
