    Overview

    This section explains the technique in plain words, why it matters, and where it sits in an attack. It also lists the three sub-techniques you will meet on the next pages.

    What the technique is

    Gather Victim Identity Information means that an attacker collects facts about the people of an organization. This includes who works there, what they do, their contact details, their login secrets, and what they say in public or semi-public chats. The attacker does this before any intrusion.

    Think of a burglar who first learns who lives in a house, when they leave for work, and which door sticks. The burglar has not entered yet. But the break-in is now much easier. Identity research works the same way for cyber attacks.

    MITRE ATT&CK splits this technique into three sub-techniques. Each one covers a different type of information: credentials, email addresses, and employee names.

    Why it matters

    Almost every targeted attack starts with information about a person. A phishing email that uses your real manager's name and your real project name is far more convincing than a generic one. Stolen passwords from an old breach often still work on other sites.

    Security teams spend a lot of effort on firewalls, endpoint tools, and patches. Those controls matter, but the first step of the attack often happens far outside their view. A useful defense therefore combines exposure management (publish less), awareness (train people), and indirect detection (watch for the signs that recon leaves behind).

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Reconnaissance is the first tactic in the ATT&CK Enterprise matrix. It happens on the PRE platform, which means the activity is mostly external, before the attacker has any access to victim systems. The information collected here feeds almost every later step.

    Step 1
    Reconnaissance (TA0043) — research the organization and its people. This technique lives here.
    Step 2
    Resource Development — buy domains, build malware, prepare infrastructure.
    Step 3
    Initial Access — phish the people you researched, or log in with their leaked credentials.
    Step 4
    Execution, Persistence, Movement — the classic intrusion, now much easier because the attacker knows the environment.

    Identity information is the fuel for later techniques such as T1566 Phishing, T1078 Valid Accounts, and T1656 Impersonation.

    Key fact: why detection is indirect

    The technique usually runs on other people's platforms: social networks, mail servers, leak forums. ATT&CK's own detection strategy (DET0841) is therefore indirect: watch for probing traffic, such as many authentication requests from one source, and study web metadata such as user-agent and referer fields. Your SIEM cannot see the gathering itself. You detect it through side effects: user reports, lookalike domains, leaked credentials, scraping bursts on your public web servers, and unusual inbound email waves. Every detection idea in Section 5 follows this idea.

    Good to know

    The strongest control is not a detection at all. Publishing less, enforcing phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication (MFA), and making it easy to report suspicious email remove most of the value of identity recon before any alert ever fires.

    The same story, written out

    Step 1 — Pick the target

    An attacker picks Aurelia Pay because it is growing fast and appears in fintech news. Target selection needs no tools and leaves no trace on the victim's systems. The SOC sees nothing, because nothing happened on company property yet.

    Step 2 — Harvest public information

    The attacker reads the company website, press releases, and professional social profiles. They collect employee names, job titles, and the public email format, for example first.last@aureliapay.example. If the public site is scraped fast and often, web server logs may show a scraping burst.

    Step 3 — Collect leaked credentials

    The attacker browses criminal forums and paste sites for old breach dumps that contain Aurelia Pay email addresses. Old passwords are valuable because people reuse them. A leak monitoring service is the defender's best chance to see this step.

    Step 4 — Build victim profiles

    The attacker joins names, emails, roles, and leaked passwords into one target list. IT, finance, and HR staff get priority, because their accounts can reset systems and move money. This list is the main product of T1589.

    Step 5 — Send targeted phishing email

    Using the profile data, the attacker sends believable emails: a fake payroll change request to HR, a fake invoice portal to finance. The mail gateway sees new messages arriving. This is the first point where the attack touches your own telemetry.

    Step 6 — An employee reports the email

    One employee notices something odd and presses the report button. The report lands in the security mailbox and becomes the single most useful artifact of the whole campaign. Reports tell you who was targeted and with what story.

    Step 7 — Intelligence confirms the pattern

    A threat intelligence feed reports a newly registered lookalike domain, and the leak feed confirms that company emails appear in a fresh dump. Now two independent signals point at the same campaign, and confidence rises.

    Step 8 — Defenders respond

    The SOC blocks the sender and the lookalike domain at the mail gateway, adds detections for impossible-travel logins, and starts targeted password resets for exposed users. Recon cannot be undone, but its next step — the intrusion — can be blunted.

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    This section explains how professional attackers think about identity research. The goal is defensive understanding: if you know why and how attackers collect identity data, you can remove what they need. All examples here are lab-only and use fictional targets.

    When and why attackers use it

    Identity recon is cheap, quiet, and pays for everything that follows. Criminal groups run it as a routine first phase, sometimes for weeks. A researched target list turns a mass campaign sent to random people into a precise one, and precise campaigns have much higher success rates.

    It is also low risk: reading a public website is legal in most places, and leak forums are outside the reach of corporate security teams. Attackers exploit exactly this blind spot.

    Where it applies and preconditions

    The technique applies to any organization with a public presence, which means effectively all of them. Preconditions on the attacker side are minimal: time, patience, and free public sources. The richer your public footprint — staff pages, press quotes, conference talks, job ads — the better their raw material.

    For the credential part, the precondition is that some of your users appear in breach data. For the employee-name part, the precondition is any public list of your staff, from a team page to a conference brochure.

    How it is performed

    Public website

    Team pages, "about us" pages, and support portals reveal names, roles, and email formats. PDF reports sometimes leak internal metadata, such as author names and file paths.

    Social and professional media

    Professional networks show org charts, new hires, tools used, and even screenshots of internal dashboards. Job ads leak the full technology stack before the first day of work.

    Breach and leak data

    Old database dumps contain email-password pairs. Criminal forums resell them in bulk. Attackers test which passwords still work, a step that later becomes visible as anomalous logins.

    Chat communities

    Open Slack or Discord communities, customer forums, and hobby servers give away internal tool names, ongoing problems, and helpful staff members who answer everything.

    Mail probing

    Attackers confirm which addresses really exist by sending tiny probe messages or using paid email-verification services. A verified list is worth far more than a guessed one.

    Data brokers

    Commercial services sell contact databases scraped from many sources. Attackers simply buy what defenders hope they would have to find by hand.

    Lab-only rule

    Everything on this page is educational. Only ever profile organizations that you own or are authorized to test, in a written engagement with clear rules. Never target real employees, never buy real stolen credentials, and never use recon output to attack real systems. If you want to practice, create a fictional company website in a lab and profile that.

    A safe lab exercise

    In a training lab, publish a small fictional company site: five employee profiles, one press release, and one job ad that names the tech stack. Then let a teammate play the attacker and answer three questions in twenty minutes: Who controls money? Who resets passwords? What is the email format? The teammate will almost always succeed, and the exercise shows exactly which pages leak the most. Fix those pages, run the exercise again, and measure the difference. This turns an abstract technique into a concrete, repeatable security drill.

    Common variations and attacker mistakes

    Variations

    • Passive only — read, never touch. Safest for the attacker, slowest to verify.
    • Active probing — send mail probes or scrape aggressively. Faster, but leaves logs.
    • Buying data — use brokers or dark-web markets to skip the work.
    • Interactive — chat with employees under a false identity, a step toward T1598 Phishing for Information.

    Mistakes attackers make

    • Scraping too fast and tripping rate limits, which logs their address.
    • Reusing the same lookalike-domain pattern across victims, so one detection covers many campaigns.
    • Sending phishing too soon after recon, which makes the timeline obvious in a joint investigation.
    • Forgetting that their own fake personas leave a history that researchers can link together.

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    Defenders rarely catch identity recon directly. They catch its shadows: a reported email, a lookalike domain, a leaked password, a scraping burst. This section shows what those shadows look like, where to look for them, and what to do in the first hour after one appears.

    How defenders can see it

    Four channels carry most of the signal. First, people: employees who report suspicious messages. Second, outside feeds: certificate transparency logs, leak trackers, and brand-monitoring services. Third, your own edges: mail gateways and public web servers, which record probing and scraping. Fourth, identity telemetry: failed logins and odd locations that appear when attackers finally use what they gathered.

    What artifacts it leaves

    • Phishing or probe emails sitting in mailboxes and gateway quarantines.
    • Newly registered lookalike domains, visible in certificate logs.
    • Employee names and emails inside third-party breach dumps.
    • Web server access logs full of directory-page requests from one source.
    • Messages from new external chat accounts asking unusual questions.

    Where to look

    • Mail gateway and mailboxes: quarantines, user reports, delivery logs.
    • Public web server logs: request bursts against team or people pages.
    • Certificate transparency feeds: new domains containing your brand.
    • Leak monitoring services: your domain appearing in dumps.
    • Identity provider logs: the moment recon turns into access attempts.
    • Chat platform audits: external members in channels they should not see.

    Why it matters and where it fits

    Recon signals are the earliest indicators you will ever get for a targeted attack. Acting on them means blocking the attack before the first login attempt, not during ransom negotiations. In the ATT&CK chain, these signals sit in Reconnaissance (TA0043), one full stage before Initial Access, so every minute you save here buys preparation time for the whole incident response process.

    First-hour triage flow

    1. Validate
    Is the signal real? Open the reported email in a sandbox, check the domain in certificate logs, confirm the dump is genuine.
    2. Scope
    Who else received it? Search the gateway for the same sender, subject, or link across all mailboxes.
    3. Contain
    Block the sender and the domain, pull the message from other mailboxes, rate-limit the scraping source.
    4. Assess exposure
    If credentials are in a dump, check which users reused those passwords. Force resets for them.
    5. Harden
    Add the lookalike pattern to your detections, brief the helpdesk about expected follow-up calls, and log the event for trend analysis.

    A triage like this takes under an hour when roles are clear. The checklist in Section 9 repeats it in a compact form.

    Warning

    Do not dismiss a single user report as noise. In most documented intrusions that started with phishing, at least one employee reported the lure before the intrusion. The report was there — it just was not triaged in time. Treat every report as free, perfect, human-generated telemetry.

    5.3 Threat ranking and categorization

    Below are the realistic identity-recon threats for a typical mid-size organization. Ranking combines likelihood (how often it happens) with impact (how bad it gets). Every detection you build should trace back to one of these rows, so the ranking also becomes your detection backlog.

    IDThreatPlatformSeverity
    TH-1Targeted credential phishing of employeesIdentity EmailHigh
    TH-2Breach-dump credential reuse and account takeoverIdentityHigh
    TH-3Employee-name harvesting feeding personalized attacksIdentity NetworkHigh
    TH-4Bulk email harvesting for BEC campaignsEmail NetworkMedium
    TH-5Public directory scraping feeding later attacksNetwork CloudMedium
    TH-6Executive profiling for impersonation fraudIdentityMedium
    TH-7Commodity mass phishing (background noise)EmailLow

    TH-1 — Targeted credential phishing High

    Description: attackers use harvested names, roles, and email formats to send convincing lures that capture passwords and MFA codes.

    Preconditions: a harvested target list; a fake login page or lookalike domain; MFA that users will blindly approve.

    Impact: account takeover, mail access, further recon from inside, ransomware entry.

    Justification: the most common identity-based entry point in public incident reporting; very likely, very damaging.

    TH-2 — Credential dump reuse High

    Description: employees appear in old breach dumps; attackers replay those passwords against VPN, mail, and SSO logins.

    Preconditions: a staff email in any third-party breach; password reuse; no unique per-service passwords.

    Impact: silent access with a valid password; often no phishing email to warn you.

    Justification: independent of user skill — a single old reused password is enough.

    TH-3 — Employee-name harvesting High

    Description: attackers collect staff names from team pages, professional networks, conference lists, and public chat communities, then use those names to derive email addresses and build believable personas.

    Preconditions: public staff lists; weak social-media guidance; the same names reused across platforms.

    Impact: personalized phishing and voice phishing that cites real colleagues and projects; derived address lists feed later campaigns.

    Justification: the documented first step of groups such as LAPSUS$ and Scattered Spider; real names make every later lure credible.

    TH-4 — Bulk email harvesting Medium

    Description: automated collection of every address on your public pages, feeds, and PDFs, later used for BEC and phishing waves.

    Preconditions: public directories or documents with mailto links.

    Impact: large address lists in criminal hands; higher phishing volume aimed at you.

    Justification: near-certain to happen, but individually less damaging than targeted attacks.

    TH-5 — Directory scraping Medium

    Description: scripted downloading of team pages and leadership bios to build an org chart, including who controls systems and money.

    Preconditions: staff pages published without rate limits or bot filtering.

    Impact: attackers learn who to impersonate and who to avoid; enables TH-1 and TH-6.

    Justification: it makes every other threat on this list stronger.

    TH-6 — Executive profiling Medium

    Description: detailed tracking of executives' public activity, travel, and announcements to time convincing fraud or impersonation.

    Preconditions: executives with public profiles; press coverage of deals and events.

    Impact: wire-fraud attempts with high credibility; reputational damage.

    Justification: lower frequency, but each success is expensive and public.

    TH-7 — Commodity mass phishing Low

    Every organization receives untargeted phishing every day. It uses no harvested data, so it barely qualifies as T1589 — but it trains your mail pipeline and your users. Severity is Low for identity risk, yet it still deserves automation: gateway filtering plus trend monitoring, with almost no analyst time.

    Realistic event examples

    These four event-shaped examples come from the sources above. Read the fields under each one to see exactly what a detection would use.

    Operation: ReportPhish is the Microsoft 365 audit action written when a user presses the report button. SenderAddress and Subject let the SOC scope the campaign to every other recipient. A count of these events per hour is the backbone of Sigma rule S1.

    The identity provider already did the hard work: riskEventTypes_v2 contains impossibleTravel, meaning two sign-ins were too far apart for the time between them. status.errorCode: 0 means success. Attackers use VPN exits, so treat any medium or high risk level plus success as suspicious, then verify with the user.

    Three directory pages in two seconds with no images, scripts, or CSS fetched is a scripted walk, not a human. The python-requests user agent is a hint, though attackers can fake browsers — so the rate per IP is the more reliable signal in Sigma rule S4.

    A verification service is asking your mail server "does this address exist?" and your server is answering "no". One line is noise; dozens from one IP in minutes is a harvested-list validation run. Count 550 5.1.1 rejections per source to catch it.

    S1 — Spike in user-reported phishing emails

    Mapping
    Both count report events per user in a one-hour window and alert above five. SPL adds the sender count for quick scoping.
    Assumptions
    index=o365 with the Splunk Add-on for Microsoft Cloud Services; operation names follow the M365 audit schema. Adjust index and threshold to your baseline (typical: 1–3 reports per user per day).
    False pos.
    Phishing simulation days; seasonal spam waves. Exclude your simulation sender domain.
    Tuning
    Track weekly baselines; alert on 3–5x baseline instead of a fixed number once you have data.
    Severity
    High — a spike means your users are under active targeting.

    S2 — Lookalike domain in certificate logs

    Mapping
    Both versions match brand plus suspicious keyword in the same stream of certificates.
    Assumptions
    Certificate transparency data lands in index=threat_intel sourcetype="ct:stream" with a DomainName field. Replace aurelia.?pay with your own brand regex, and define the brand_allowlist lookup for known partners.
    False pos.
    Partners, resellers, and regional subsidiaries that embed your brand legally.
    Tuning
    Grow the allowlist over time; add notification keywords such as -support and -help once noise is controlled.
    Severity
    Medium — a domain is preparation, not yet an attack; escalate when mail traffic to it appears.

    S3 — Successful risky sign-in

    Mapping
    Same logic: success plus elevated risk. SPL groups per user so analysts see the full story in one row.
    Assumptions
    index=identity sourcetype="azure:aad:signin" from the Microsoft cloud add-on. Field names such as RiskLevelAggregated depend on your ingestion; keep the names consistent with your other identity rules.
    False pos.
    Travel, VPN hobby use, mobile carriers. Enrich with a "user is on approved travel" lookup.
    Tuning
    Suppress known-benign users for 24 hours after verification; consider auto-revoking sessions on high-risk sign-ins if your policy allows.
    Severity
    High — this is often the first successful step after recon.

    S4 — Directory scraping burst

    Mapping
    Same page set, same window, same threshold of 40 requests.
    Assumptions
    Sigma uses webserver-category fields (cs-uri-stem, User-Agent, src_ip); Splunk uses CIM fields from the Apache technology add-on (uri_path, useragent, src). Map src to src_ip with an eval if your add-on differs.
    False pos.
    Job scrapers and recruiters. Ask HR which job boards they use and allowlist them.
    Tuning
    Rate-limit the pages at the CDN as a preventive twin of this rule; track the threshold against a week of real traffic.
    Severity
    Medium — recon in progress against your own infrastructure.

    S5 — Company domain in a credential dump feed

    Mapping
    Same single-match logic; SPL summarizes volume and first and last sighting.
    Assumptions
    Leak feeds vary by product (domain monitoring services write different sourcetypes). Point the query at your feed's index and rename fields such as Email, BreachName to match its schema.
    False pos.
    Old breaches resurfacing in aggregator posts. Keep a record of handled incidents and dedupe.
    Tuning
    Route to an automatic playbook: identify affected users, check recent auth anomalies, force reset for password reuse.
    Severity
    High — stolen credentials are the shortest path from recon to access.

    S6 — SMTP recipient probing

    Mapping
    Same message pattern and count threshold, grouped by source address.
    Assumptions
    Postfix syslog lands in index=email sourcetype=postfix; the rex extracts the connecting IP from the standard reject line. For Exchange or Proofpoint environments, adapt to their rejection events.
    False pos.
    Your own bulk mailings to stale lists. Tag known marketing senders.
    Tuning
    Combine with tarpitting or reject delays at the MX, which slows probes and makes the pattern even clearer.
    Severity
    Medium — confirms someone is actively validating a harvested list.

    Validation note

    Test every rule before production: replay a sample event from Section 5.4 against the query, confirm it fires, then confirm a known-good event does not. Record the test in your detection inventory with a date and an owner. A rule without a test is a hypothesis, not a detection.

    Real-World Case Study

    The story below is a composite: a fictional company, Aurelia Pay, with a timeline assembled from patterns that appear repeatedly in public incident reports of identity-based intrusions. It shows how small recon signals, taken seriously, could have changed the outcome.

    Context

    Aurelia Pay is a fictional payments company with 850 employees, growing quickly after a funding round. Security was a team of four: one SOC analyst on duty per shift, an email gateway, a cloud identity provider, and no leak monitoring service. Public staff pages listed every team with photos, names, and titles.

    Attack chain at a glance

    Stage 1
    Identity recon: staff pages, professional networks, leak forums (this technique)
    Stage 2
    Targeted SMS phishing of two IT helpdesk staff
    Stage 3
    Credential capture and a convincing MFA-reset call
    Stage 4
    VPN access, helpdesk tool abuse, password resets for executives
    Stage 5
    Cloud data theft and extortion attempt

    Timeline

    TimeEventVisible signal at the time
    Day 0–12Attacker scrapes the public team pages and professional profiles, builds a 40-person target list including helpdesk staff.Web server saw ~300 requests from one non-browser agent; nobody looked at those logs.
    Day 9Attacker registers a lookalike SMS-sender identity mimicking the IT service desk brand.No brand or sender monitoring existed.
    Day 13, 10:20SMS phishing hits the two helpdesk staff with a fake "employee account lockout" portal.Phones are outside corporate telemetry; nothing alerts.
    Day 13, 16:05One helpdesk tech submits credentials on the fake portal; the attacker calls, posing as the employee, and talks through an MFA re-enrollment.The helpdesk ticket later shows the request, but with no verification against policy.
    Day 13, 17:12Attacker signs in to the VPN from a residential proxy in another country.The identity provider flags a medium-risk sign-in; the alert sits in a backlog.
    Day 14Attacker uses helpdesk tooling to reset two executive accounts and exports cloud mail and files.Bulk export triggers a DLP warning, seen the next morning.

    Day 15–17Containment: sessions revoked, accounts reset, helpdesk tooling locked to an approved list, proxy IPs blocked.Attack access ends.
    Day 18–30Eradication and recovery: stolen data inventoried, regulator notified, staff pages reduced, leak monitoring purchased.Rebuild and lessons learned.

    The detection point that mattered

    Three signals existed before any damage: a scraping burst on Day 0–12, a lookalike identity on Day 9, and a risky sign-in on Day 13. Any single one, triaged in time, would have exposed the campaign. The investigation itself only started when a human noticed something wrong — two full days after the attacker was inside. This is the cost of leaving recon signals unwatched.

    Containment

    Sessions and tokens for affected accounts were revoked, all helpdesk passwords reset, and the attacker's proxy ranges blocked at the VPN. Chat and mail rules the attacker had created were removed and audited.

    Eradication

    The team confirmed no malware was present — this was a pure identity attack. They hardened the MFA policy to number matching, required manager confirmation for executive account changes, and restricted helpdesk resets with verification scripts.

    Recovery

    Access was restored from clean administration accounts, exported data was inventoried for the regulator, and a takedown request removed the phishing infrastructure. Public staff pages were reduced to names and roles only.

    Impact and consequences

    Direct impact stayed limited because extortion failed, but costs were real: 30 staff-days of response, a regulator notification, customer trust questions during a funding round, and a rushed purchase of brand and leak monitoring. The CFO's comment in the retrospective: the attackers knew our org chart better than our own HR portal did.

    Team response and communication

    The small team used a simple model that worked: one incident commander, one communicator, one scribe. They briefed the executive team twice a day in plain language, gave the helpdesk a script for user questions, and wrote a public statement only after facts were confirmed. Clear roles, not tools, kept the response calm.

    Lessons learned

    • Recon signals (scraping, lookalike identities, risky sign-ins) must have an owner and a triage path before the incident, not after.
    • Helpdesk identity verification is an identity control, equal in weight to MFA.
    • Public staff pages are attacker working material; publish the minimum.
    • Human reporting worked — the executive report started the investigation. Make reporting even easier.
    • Leak and brand monitoring convert invisible recon into visible alerts.
    • Small teams need playbooks more than dashboards: who decides, who speaks, who writes it down.

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop someone from reading a public web page. Mitigation therefore works in three layers: publish less, make stolen identities less useful, and watch the channels where recon touches you. ATT&CK lists no mitigations for T1589 itself — which is exactly why a layered plan matters.

    Home and small-office advice

    • Use a password manager so every site has a different password; one leak then cannot unlock the rest.
    • Turn on the strongest MFA your services offer — passkeys or an authenticator app, not SMS when possible.
    • Search your own email address on breach-notification services and change reused passwords.
    • Keep your social profiles private; think before you post your employer, role, and tools.
    • Be kind but cautious in open chat communities: never share internal details with strangers, however friendly.

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    Most blue teams do not fail because they lack tools. They fail on ownership, tuning, and follow-through. Each mistake below shows the symptom you will notice first, the root cause behind it, the fix, and the prevention that stops it from returning.

    1. Phishing reports land in an unmonitored mailbox

    SymptomReports sit for days; victims call the helpdesk instead.

    Root causeThe report button forwards to a shared mailbox with no owner and no service level.

    FixRoute reports into the SIEM as events, assign an analyst queue, and set a one-hour triage target.

    PreventionMeasure time-to-triage weekly; treat breaches of the target as incidents of their own.

    2. No visibility into credential leaks

    SymptomYou learn about dumped staff credentials from a journalist or vendor.

    Root causeLeak monitoring was considered a niche or "nice to have" service.

    FixSubscribe to a domain monitoring service and wire matches into rule S5.

    PreventionQuarterly exposure review that covers dumps, paste sites, and broker listings.

    3. Impossible-travel alerts tuned into silence

    SymptomRisk sign-in alerts are auto-closed as "user traveling".

    Root causeEarly noise was never tuned with context, so analysts stopped trusting the rule.

    FixAdd a travel calendar lookup, split severity by risk level, and require comments on closure.

    PreventionMonthly tuning review with closure-reason statistics.

    4. Over-blocking after the first scraping alert

    SymptomRecruiters, job boards, and search engines get blocked; marketing files complaints.

    Root causeAn edge rule was written in reaction mode without a traffic baseline.

    FixIntroduce an allowlist and per-client thresholds; revert the blanket block.

    PreventionChange control for CDN and WAF rules, with a test window before enforcement.

    5. Public pages leak the whole org chart

    SymptomThe attacker's target list mirrors your staff page exactly.

    Root causeMarketing owns the website; security never reviews what it publishes.

    FixReduce staff pages to names and roles; remove direct emails and team structures.

    PreventionAdd a security review step to the website release process.

    6. Sigma rules that were never tested

    SymptomA rule fires on everything, or worse, never fires at all.

    Root causeRules were written once and deployed without replaying real events.

    FixReplay the sample events from Section 5.4 against each rule; fix logic until both sides pass.

    PreventionKeep detections in version control with a test event per rule.

    7. Chat platforms missing from the log inventory

    SymptomWhen a chat incident happens, there is no evidence to review.

    Root causeChat audit logs were never enabled or never ingested into the SIEM.

    FixEnable platform audit logging and ingest it; backfill what the platform retains.

    PreventionQuarterly log-source review that walks every SaaS in use, not just servers.

    8. Detections without owners

    SymptomOld rules break silently after schema changes; nobody notices for months.

    Root causeDetections were treated as one-time projects, not maintained products.

    FixCreate a detection inventory with an owner, a review date, and a test status for each rule.

    PreventionOwnership review as part of the quarterly detection health check.

    9. Measuring only simulation success

    SymptomSimulation click rates look great while real phishing still succeeds.

    Root causeUsers learned to spot simulated lures, not real ones; the metric rewards gaming.

    FixTrack real-world report rates and time-to-triage as the primary program metrics.

    PreventionReport both simulation and real metrics to leadership together, always.

    10. Thresholds too high for small probes

    SymptomProbing waves of 10–14 requests pass unnoticed under a threshold of 40.

    Root causeA fixed threshold chosen once, never re-tested against low-and-slow behavior.

    FixUse adaptive baselines and add a secondary low-and-slow rule for trusted-browsers patterns.

    PreventionTest rules at low volumes; attackers read your public thresholds too.

    11. Helpdesk verification that exists only on paper

    SymptomReset requests from a confident caller are processed anyway.

    Root causeThe script is optional in the ticketing tool and audits never check it.

    FixMake the verification fields mandatory in the ticket workflow; block approval without them.

    PreventionMonthly spot audits of reset tickets with feedback to the desk.

    Habits that keep you ready

    Daily

    • Triage every phishing report within the target time.
    • Review risky sign-in alerts with fresh eyes.
    • Check overnight mail-gateway anomalies for new sender patterns.

    Weekly

    • Review scraping and probing trends against baseline.
    • Update allowlists from marketing, HR, and recruiting inputs.
    • Confirm the leak feed still reports zero unhandled matches.

    Monthly

    • Validate one third of detection rules against sample events.
    • Audit chat guests and external members.
    • Walk the public website as an attacker would, and file cleanup tickets.
