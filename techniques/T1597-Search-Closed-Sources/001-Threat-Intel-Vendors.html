1Overview

What this sub-technique covers, why it is attractive, and where it sits in the attack chain.

What is T1597.001?

Threat Intel Vendors is the sub-technique where an adversary buys information from commercial threat intelligence companies. The products include private research reports, searchable vendor portals with curated actor data, and — most sensitive of all — custom reports written by analysts to answer a paying customer's specific questions.

None of this requires hacking. A subscription, a payment method, and sometimes a screened buyer identity are enough. The attacker reads the same high-quality research your security team reads, but aims it at you.

Why it matters to defenders

Vendor reports compress months of expertise into a short document. For an attacker, that means knowing your sector's common weaknesses, the exploits that work against your stack, and the incidents that made your peers pay ransoms. It raises their success rate before the first packet is sent.

The defender's edge is that vendors sit in the middle. They see who searches for what. Many offer "monitored term" alerts when someone researches your brand, and most keep audit logs of searches and downloads. Those signals are invisible to your firewalls but available by contract — if you ask for them.

Where it fits in the attack chain

1 · Recon→ 2 · Weaponize→ 3 · Deliver→ 4 · Exploit→ 5 · Install→ 6 · C2→ 7 · Act

As a PRE technique, T1597.001 happens before initial access. Its output feeds the whole operation: sector context improves phishing lures (T1566), vendor-described vulnerabilities guide exploitation of public-facing apps (T1190), and actor tracking helps attackers mimic or avoid known patterns.

Note

Buying a threat intelligence report is legal. The abuse lies in the intent, not the purchase. This is why detection must lean on behavior (unusual search patterns) and contracts (telemetry rights), not on "blocklist the vendor".

Scope

One scope note from ATT&CK: if a threat actor searches vendor data for information about their own activities (to see what defenders know about them), that is a different technique — Search Threat Vendor Data. T1597.001 covers searching vendor data for information about victims. Keeping the two apart prevents wrong mappings in your detection inventory.

Buy a subscription

Through a front company, the operator subscribes to a commercial threat intel portal. Payment clears, buyer screening passes, and the account is ready. The target company sees nothing of this.

Browse the catalog

The operator studies the vendor's catalog: actor profiles, sector threat reports, malware libraries, and the option to order custom research.

STEP 3

Search for the target

They search the portal for the target's name and domain. Two private reports mention the company. The vendor's monitoring service quietly logs every search — this becomes the SOC's first echo.

STEP 4

Order a custom report

The operator pays for a custom research task: "describe the security posture and key staff of this logistics group, with a focus on M&A activity".

STEP 5

The analyst compiles

A vendor analyst assembles public and licensed data into a report. The analyst follows the vendor's rules — the report is legal to write and sell. Intent, not content, makes it dangerous.

STEP 6

Download and digest

The operator downloads the finished PDF: key executives, an acquisition timeline, and a summary of the security stack. The vendor logs the download event.

STEP 7

Plan the operation

Armed with the report, the operator drafts an invoice-fraud email timed to the M&A transition and shortlists the executives named in the document.

STEP 8

The SOC's only echo

The SOC receives one email: the vendor's monitored-term alert showing a spike in searches for the brand. Properly wired, that alert starts a hunt. Ignored, it is the only warning that ever existed.

3Red Team / Hacker Perspective

How operators use paid vendor research, shown for defensive and educational purposes only.

?

When and why attackers use it

Vendor reports answer questions an attacker would otherwise spend weeks answering badly: which ransomware groups hit this sector, what their success rates look like, which vulnerabilities get exploited, and what victims paid. Buying that picture is cheaper and quieter than discovering it with scans that trigger alarms.

Custom reports are the sharpest tool. A victim-specific document with executives, org changes, and security posture turns generic phishing into precise social engineering. It is also the most defensible-looking part of a pre-attack operation — research is not a crime.

≡

How it is performed

  • Portal subscription: standard commercial account, sometimes via reseller or shell company.
  • Catalog research: reading actor profiles and sector reports to model what works against the target's industry.
  • Target search: querying the victim's name, domain, and executives in the portal's private data.
  • Custom order: commissioning analyst research framed as due diligence, market study, or journalism.
  • Feed monitoring: subscribing to victim-watch services that report when a company appears in incidents.

OPSEC detail: careful buyers search subsidiaries and old brand names instead of the exact brand, precisely because monitored-term alerts exist.

Prerequisites and conditions

Budget

Subscriptions range from hundreds to tens of thousands of dollars. Custom reports are priced per question. Well-funded crews see this as a very small cost.

Clean-enough identity

Major vendors screen buyers. Operators use front companies, resellers, or stolen analyst credentials when screening blocks them.

A use plan

Raw reports only help when converted into operations: lure drafts, timing calendars, and target lists. Weak operators collect and never act.

Common variations and mistakes

Variations
  • Ordering custom reports under the cover of due diligence, M&A research, or press inquiries.
  • Subscribing to victim-notification feeds to catch breaches early and buy access from the first responders' competitors.
  • Buying compromised vendor accounts instead of subscriptions — cheaper and untraceable to the buyer.
  • Using free vendor trials in bulk to stay under screening thresholds.
Attacker mistakes defenders can profit from
  • Searching the exact brand and triggering monitored-term alerts.
  • Ordering custom reports with questions that name the victim too precisely, leaving a paper trail at the vendor.
  • Reusing the same payment rail or persona across vendors, letting investigators cluster campaigns.
  • Downloading entire report libraries, whose bulk-export logs stand out immediately.

4Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Your visibility lives at the vendor, not in your network. Here is how to see the echoes and act on them.

Reality check

A purchased report leaves zero logs on your side. ATT&CK's own detection guidance says much of this activity may have a very high occurrence and false-positive rate, may take place outside your visibility, and is best covered by watching related stages such as Initial Access. The SOC's realistic goals are: (1) receive vendor-side echoes, (2) treat them as early warnings, and (3) harden the specific things a report would reveal about you.

What artifacts it leaves (and where)

  • Monitored-term alerts: vendor notifications when searches for your brand, domain, or executives spike.
  • Portal audit logs: account-level records of searches, report views, and downloads — available on request or via API.
  • Custom-report paper trail: a written record of who asked what about you; vendors may notify you for sensitive orders.
  • Downstream echoes: phishing that uses report-quality details (correct M&A context, correct executive names) is itself evidence that paid research happened.

Why it matters for the chain

T1597.001 is the context-gathering phase. When a phishing email arrives quoting a real acquisition timeline, the problem is no longer "user clicked a link" — the problem is "someone bought a profile of us". Teams that capture vendor echoes can brief leadership with a simple story: the reconnaissance is observable, and here is what we changed because of it.

It also reframes the vendor relationship. You are a customer and a data subject at the same time. Contracts should include: keyword alerts, audit-log export, and notification duties for sensitive research orders.

Short triage flow (run it as a playbook)

  1. Signal in
    Vendor keyword alert arrives

    Record the term searched, volume, window, and any account metadata the vendor provides. Assign to the intel owner — not a group mailbox.

  2. Minute 0–15
    Contextualize

    Is a sector event live (funding, M&A, layoff, earnings)? Known campaigns against your industry this month? Context decides urgency.

  3. Minute 15–60
    Score the exposure

    What would a report about us reveal today? Check: executive exposure, M&A status, public attack surface, recent incidents. Fix the worst finding this week.

  4. Hour 1–24
    Hunt downstream

    Search email and IdP logs for lures referencing live sector events. Brief high-risk staff named in possible reports with a short, human warning.

  5. Day 1–7
    Report and adjust

    One-page summary to leadership: what was searched, what we did, what we changed. Tune the vendor alert thresholds with the new baseline.

5.2Threat intelligence

This sub-technique is a threat intelligence problem end to end. The sources below, plus honest confidence labels, make the difference between noise and signal.

V

Your intel vendors (first party)

The vendors you already pay are your best sources here: monitored-term alerts, portal audit exports, and researcher reports on who targets your sector. Collect via API or scheduled exports into the SIEM. Apply by pairing every alert with a disposition note.

P

Peer reporting and ISACs

Sector ISACs share when members see profiling or pre-attack signals. Collect through member feeds and calls. Apply by comparing your keyword alerts to the sector baseline — a spike that peers also see is probably campaign-scale.

A

MITRE ATT&CK

Use ATT&CK to classify what the research enables: T1597.001 feeding T1566 (phishing) or T1190 (public apps). Navigator layers showing PRE coverage make leadership conversations concrete.

O

OSINT checks on yourself

Quarterly self-assessment: search your own brand in trial portals, order a synthetic "custom report" against your lab tenant, and read what a buyer would learn. Collect into a findings log. Apply by closing the worst exposures.

I

Internal telemetry

Your SEG, IdP, and help-desk data show whether report-grade details are already being used against you (correct exec names, correct timelines in lures). Collect by tagging lures with "report-quality context" flags. Apply by raising response priority when they appear.

C

Confidence labeling

High = vendor telemetry plus corroboration. Medium = single-source telemetry or credible peer report. Low = plausible inference. Every intel product you circulate must carry one of these labels — decisions depend on it.

5.4Logs and patterns

For each threat: the log source, the fields that matter, normal versus suspicious behavior, and the detection logic. Event shapes use realistic field names you can map to your schema.

TH-01 · Custom report ordered about your organization High Third-party intel

Log source: vendor notification service for sensitive research orders (contractual). Fields: order_id, subject_terms, account_type, account_age_days, billing_country.

Normal: research orders from known customers, universities, and journalists about public topics. Suspicious: an order naming your executives or internal systems from a very young account, or research questions that read like reconnaissance. Detection logic: alert when subject_terms matches your brand AND account_age_days is low OR the order includes security-posture questions.

TH-02 · Keyword-alert spike for your brand Medium Network

Log source: vendor monitored-term alert feed. Fields: query_term, query_count, query_window, source_account_type.

Normal: baseline of a few hits per day from analysts and media. Suspicious: 24-hour counts far above the weekly median, especially from new accounts. Detection logic: sum of query_count per term per day above 3× the trailing median.

TH-03 · Report-grade details in live phishing lures High People

Log source: email security gateway (SEG) detections, enriched by your triage notes. Fields: mail_subject, sender_domain, context_flag, recipient_role.

Normal: generic phishing with public details. Suspicious: lures referencing non-public timelines, correct org structure, or internal project names — the signature of bought research. Detection logic: tag any lure whose content matches "report-quality context" and raise severity when it targets finance or executives.

TH-04 · Bulk research across many victims from one account Medium Cloud / SaaS

Log source: vendor portal audit feed (abuse view shared by the vendor, or your own tenant's audit slice). Fields: account_id, action, searched_terms, distinct_targets.

Normal: an analyst researching one sector per project. Suspicious: one account searching dozens of unrelated company names in a day, or mass report downloads. Detection logic: count of distinct_targets per account per day above threshold, or download volume far above account baseline.

TH-05 · Stolen analyst credentials on your vendor account High Identity

Log source: vendor SSO/audit feed for your tenant plus your IdP sign-in logs. Fields: user, auth_method, mfa_used, source_ip, geo.

Normal: two named analysts, office or VPN IPs, MFA always used. Suspicious: missing MFA, new geography, new device, or session activity while the analyst is offline. Detection logic: mfa_used: false OR impossible travel on the vendor application, regardless of what the identity provider saw.

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

One Sigma rule and one matching Splunk query per threat. The vendor log sources are custom categories — map them to your ingestion as noted. Tune every threshold to your baselines.

SIG-01 · Sensitive research order naming your organization High

Mapping note: SPL keeps the Sigma intent exactly: brand match AND at least one reconnaissance-shaped question AND an account younger than a year (a year lets established researchers through and flags fresh accounts). False positives: genuine M&A due diligence; keep an allowlist of known counterparties.

SIG-02 · Keyword-alert spike for monitored brand terms Medium

Mapping note: the firing threshold matches Sigma exactly (sum(query_count) > 30 per 24 hours). The dynamic_baseline field is a ready-made upgrade: after two weeks of data, swap the where line for where hits > dynamic_baseline. False positives: news-driven spikes — add an "is sector event live?" annotation field to triage notes.

SIG-03 · Report-quality context in phishing lures High

Mapping note: context_flag is a triage-applied enrichment, not a raw gateway field — document how analysts set it. Tuning: require 2+ lures before escalating to a campaign investigation.

SIG-04 · Bulk multi-target research from one vendor account Medium

Mapping note: the audit feed sends one daily summary per account, so Sigma and the SPL both take the day's maximum distinct_targets and fire above 40 — identical logic. The SPL keeps download volume as triage context; make it a second arm or a companion rule (downloads > 25) if your vendor's abuse team uses that measure. Tuning: agree thresholds with the vendor's own abuse team — they know the real baselines.

SIG-05 · Non-MFA or impossible-travel session on vendor portal High

Mapping note: the 198.51.100. range is a reserved example range — replace it with your real VPN egress range. Tuning: enforce SSO with MFA at the vendor side so mfa_used=false becomes structurally impossible, then keep the rule as a drift alarm.

Validation

Replay the JSON examples above through your ingestion path and confirm each rule fires; then replay one "normal" event per rule and confirm silence. Store both runs as validation evidence for auditors (see 5.1).

6Real-World Case Study

An illustrative composite based on common BEC and invoice-fraud patterns. Names and details are fictional; the mechanism is what defenders actually encounter.

Composite

Custom-report purchases almost never appear in public incident reports, because the victim never sees them. This composite shows how report-grade research turns into a payment-fraud operation — and where defenders can still catch it.

Victim

Northwind Logistics (fictional), 800 staff, mid-close acquisition of a smaller competitor, finance team of twelve.

Adversary

A BEC crew that buys victim research instead of scanning, and strikes during corporate transitions.

Objective

Redirect a supplier payment during the acquisition window using a spoofed executive thread.

Timeline

  1. Day −30
    The purchase

    Through a nine-day-old portal account, the crew orders a custom report: "security posture, finance staff list, and M&A timeline for Northwind Logistics". The vendor logs the order. Northwind has no notification clause with this vendor, so nobody on the victim side knows.

  2. Day −29 to −3
    The report and rehearsals

    The PDF arrives: correct executives, the acquisition codename, payment-process notes. The crew registers the lookalike domain northwind-closing.example.net and drafts lures that quote the real approver chain.

  3. Day −12
    An echo nobody reads

    The vendor's monitored-term service emails Northwind's finance address: brand searches spiked 14×. It is forwarded to a shared mailbox and forgotten. No SLA, no owner.

  4. Day 0 · 10:05
    The strike

    Three lure emails land, timed to a real payment run: "update the beneficiary account for the Northfield closing". Two are opened; one reply comes from the CFO's impersonated thread.

  5. Day 0 · 14:20
    Detection point — the catch

    The treasury system's new-payee rule flags the change: new bank account, new country, 90 minutes before a payment run. Payment verification calls the supplier on a known number and the fraud is confirmed before money moves.

  6. Day 0 · 15:00
    Containment

    Payment frozen, all staff receive a live warning with the exact lure subject line, lookalike domain submitted for takedown, and the SEG is loaded with the sender domain pattern.

  7. Day 1–4
    Eradication and investigation

    Mailbox rules (none found — no compromise occurred) and sender infrastructure are swept. The vendor, now contacted formally, provides the audit export: one young account, 61 targets searched in a week. Other victims are notified through the ISAC.

  8. Day 5–21
    Recovery and hardening

    Payment verification made mandatory for account changes; executive-brand monitoring moved into the SIEM with an owner and SLA; vendor contracts updated with telemetry and notification clauses at renewal.

Attack chain summary

  • T1597.001 — custom report bought on a young portal account
  • T1583.001 — lookalike domain registered (mitigated by CT watching later)
  • T1566 — phishing: external lure emails quoting the real approver chain
  • T1656 — impersonation of executives in lures
  • T1657 — financial theft attempt (payment redirection), stopped

Tools in play

Attacker side: vendor portal subscription with custom research order (the profile), a freshly registered lookalike domain with TLS, and standard BEC lure infrastructure.

Defender side: treasury payment-verification workflow (the catch), SEG quarantine rules, vendor audit export, and CT/domain monitoring for the lookalike.

Impact and consequences

  • No funds lost — caught by payment verification within 90 minutes
  • ≈ 60 staff-hours of response; one supplier relationship briefly strained
  • Two days of distraction for the finance team during a live acquisition
  • Valuable positive: the vendor's audit export helped three peer victims

Team response and communication

Treasury, security, and finance leadership joined one bridge within 30 minutes. The CFO's office communicated directly with the real supplier to confirm no account change was real. The SOC wrote the internal summary the same evening: no mailbox compromise, lure blocked, process gap identified. Legal contacted the vendor for logs and the registrar for takedown. A short, factual note went to all staff the next morning, naming the technique — which made the follow-up training land.

Lessons learned

  • The earliest echo (Day −12) was free and died in a shared mailbox. Vendor keyword alerts need an owner and an SLA like any other alert class.
  • Payment verification (callback on a known number) was the control that actually stopped the loss — worth funding everywhere.
  • The vendor relationship worked both ways: once engaged formally, their audit data helped identify peer victims. Contract for it up front.
  • Lure quality is a detection signal. "Report-quality context" should raise severity automatically.

7Mitigation and Defense

You cannot stop someone from reading a report about you. You can make the report's contents less useful and its echoes visible. This matches MITRE's official mitigation position (M1056 Pre-compromise): the activity happens outside enterprise defenses, so efforts focus on minimizing the amount and sensitivity of data available to external parties.

Home and small-office advice

V

Verify payment changes

Small businesses are prime BEC targets. Call the supplier on the number you already have before any bank-detail change. Thirty seconds on the phone beats a lawsuit.

S

Separate your inboxes

Keep banking and business email away from newsletter and social accounts. Leaked data from low-value sites fuels better profiles of you.

T

Think before you post

Public org charts and "our team" pages are the raw material of the reports sold about companies. Publish roles, not full contact chains.

Response checklist

  • Confirm the vendor echo is genuine; record term, volume, window, and account metadata
  • Check for a live sector event (funding, M&A, layoffs) that could explain benign spikes
  • Ask the vendor for the account view: age, country, other terms searched
  • Hunt email and IdP logs for lures that use report-grade details
  • Brief named executives and finance staff with the specific pattern seen
  • Verify payment-process controls held (callbacks, cooling periods)
  • Share sanitized findings with your ISAC and the vendor's abuse team
  • Write the one-page summary: signal, context, action, change made
