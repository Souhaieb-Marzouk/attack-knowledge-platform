1Overview

What this sub-technique covers, why technical history is dangerous, and where it sits in the attack chain.

What is T1597.002?

Purchase Technical Data means an adversary buys technical records about a target from commercial platforms: passive DNS (a record of which hostnames pointed at which IP addresses, and when), WHOIS history (who registered a domain, and when it changed hands), certificate transparency data, subdomain lists, and IP reputation history.

The magic ingredient is history. Your current DNS points at what exists today. Your historic DNS shows what existed in 2014 — including the test portal, the old VPN, and the staging server somebody forgot to decommission. Attackers buy your past because your present is usually defended better.

Why it matters to defenders

This technique needs no exploit and makes no noise. A five-dollar API query can return an asset map your own CMDB does not contain. The classic result is "the attacker knew about our infrastructure before our IT team did."

The good news: every query the attacker can run, you can run too — against yourself. Attack-surface management is essentially this technique pointed inward, continuously. Teams that do both (outside-in inventory + vendor telemetry) close the gap before it becomes an entry point.

Where it fits in the attack chain

1 · Recon→ 2 · Weaponize→ 3 · Deliver→ 4 · Exploit→ 5 · Install→ 6 · C2→ 7 · Act

As a PRE technique, T1597.002 happens before initial access. Its output selects the door: a forgotten VPN portal (T1133), an unpatched legacy web app (T1190), or the naming pattern used for a convincing lookalike domain (T1583.001).

3Red Team / Hacker Perspective

How operators turn purchased technical records into a chosen entry point, presented for defense and education only.

?

When and why attackers use it

Scanning a target's IP range (T1595) is slow, expensive in stealth terms, and may hit a honeypot. Purchased data answers the same question instantly and silently: what existed, what changed, and what was abandoned. For well-prepared crews, it is the default first step before any active technique.

It also degrades your defense-in-depth. A target who thinks "they can't know our internal names" is wrong: certificate records leak SANs and CNs for years, including names that were never meant to be public. Assumptions built on obscurity collapse quietly.

≡

How it is performed

  • WHOIS history: registrant emails, old brand names, and ownership changes that reveal acquisitions and rebrands.
  • Passive DNS: every hostname-to-IP mapping observed over years, exposing the full shape of the estate.
  • Certificate data: SANs, CNs, issuance dates, and internal naming conventions from transparency logs.
  • Subdomain datasets: curated lists of hostnames found by crawling and resolvers, including long-dead ones.
  • IP reputation history: what else lived on the IPs your assets share — neighbors, abuse history, hosting patterns.

Operational note: careful buyers spread queries over days and rotate API keys, precisely because some platforms alert the domains being queried in bulk.

Prerequisites and conditions

API budget

Data platforms price by query or subscription. A full mapping of a mid-size company costs tens to hundreds of dollars — trivial for any funded actor.

Correlation skill

Raw records are not a map. The skill is joining them: WHOIS dates + passive DNS + certificates → "this forgotten host still resolves and serves a login page."

Patience with OPSEC

Query patterns are logged. Professional buyers throttle queries and avoid single-session bulk pulls that trip vendor abuse rules.

Common variations and mistakes

Variations
  • Buying subsidiary and acquisition domains after WHOIS history reveals them — young subsidiaries are rarely hardened.
  • Using IP reputation history to find shared hosting where a neighbor compromise yields route-level access.
  • Harvesting internal naming conventions from certificate records to build perfect lookalike domains.
  • Combining with open technical databases (T1596) to confirm purchased findings before acting.
Attacker mistakes defenders can profit from
  • Bulk queries in one session, which trigger vendor abuse alerts to the queried brand.
  • Trusting stale maps: the quiet door was patched or decommissioned last month — wasted effort and, sometimes, a probe you can see.
  • Correlating on old registrant emails that now belong to the defender's monitoring inbox.
  • Building lookalikes on naming patterns the defender has already watched for since their last CT-log review.

4Blue Team / SOC Perspective

The query happens on someone else's platform. Your defense is to run it first, watch the echoes, and kill the raw material.

Reality check

You will not see the purchase in your logs — ever. ATT&CK's detection guidance for the T1597 family says much of this activity may take place outside the visibility of the target organization, may carry a very high false-positive rate, and is best covered by watching related stages such as Initial Access. What you can do is eliminate the advantage: if your own outside-in view is fresher than the attacker's purchased map, they gain nothing. Two echoes (vendor alerts, CT watchers) still give you a heads-up.

What artifacts it leaves (and where)

  • Vendor API audit alerts: several platforms notify (or can be contracted to notify) when a domain is queried at unusual volume.
  • Certificate transparency: lookalike certificates issued after internal naming patterns are harvested appear in CT logs — your watcher can catch them.
  • Probe echoes: the attacker eventually checks whether a forgotten asset is alive — one request to a dead service is a signal worth logging.
  • Registration echoes: lookalike domain registrations built on your naming patterns appear in newly-registered-domain feeds.

Why it matters for the chain

T1597.002 selects the door for T1190 and T1133. If your asset inventory is complete and your DNS is clean, the technique returns nothing useful. If it is not, this is usually where breaches quietly start — and your first real log line may be the successful login weeks later.

It is also the cheapest technique to defend in the whole ATT&CK catalog. The data is the same data; the only question is who queries it first, and who acts on the difference.

Short triage flow (run it as a playbook)

  1. Signal in
    Vendor or CT alert arrives

    Bulk-query notice about your domains, or a new certificate matching your naming pattern. Assign to the ASM owner — not to nobody.

  2. Minute 0–15
    Verify

    Confirm the certificate/domain is not yours. Confirm the query volume is real, not your own tooling (allowlist first, always).

  3. Minute 15–60
    Run the mirror

    Execute the attacker's queries on yourself: WHOIS history, passive DNS, certs. Diff against the CMDB. List every "alive but unknown" asset.

  4. Hour 1–24
    Act on the diff

    Decommission what is dead (and purge its DNS), patch or MFA what is alive and forgotten, and block/monitor the lookalike.

  5. Day 1–7
    Close the loop

    Update the CMDB, log the finding, and set a recurring mirror query so the next buyer finds a boring map.

5.2Threat intelligence

For technical-data abuse, intelligence means knowing what your domains say about you and which actors monetize that knowledge.

D

Data platforms (defensive use)

Schedule the attacker's queries — WHOIS history, passive DNS, certs — for every domain you own. Collect weekly via API. Apply by diffing against the CMDB; every unmatched live asset is a ticket.

C

Certificate transparency feeds

CT logs are public but aggregation platforms make them searchable. Collect via watcher services or your own log monitoring. Apply by alerting on brand + auth keywords and on internal naming patterns.

N

Newly-registered-domain feeds

NRD feeds show lookalikes built on harvested naming conventions. Collect daily. Apply by matching against your brand strings and host-name patterns, then blocking pre-delivery.

V

Vendor abuse and audit alerts

Some platforms alert brands about bulk queries and can share audit summaries. Collect contractually. Apply as an early indicator with low severity but real context value.

I

ISAC and peer reporting

Peers share which asset types (old VPNs, staging portals) are being weaponized in your sector this quarter. Apply by prioritizing your own cleanup queue to match.

A

ATT&CK + confidence labels

Map findings to T1597.002 → T1190/T1133/T1583.001 chains. Label every intel item High/Medium/Low and say why. A labeled guess beats an unlabeled certainty in an incident bridge.

5.4Logs and patterns

For each threat: the log source, the fields that matter, normal versus suspicious behavior, and the detection logic. Event shapes use realistic field names you can map to your schema.

TH-01 · Forgotten subdomains found in passive DNS data High Network

Log source: your own ASM tool running scheduled mirror queries, or its change feed into the SIEM. Fields: hostname, state, last_seen_pdns, cmdb_match.

Normal: every resolving hostname exists in the CMDB with an owner. Suspicious: a hostname that resolves now but has no inventory entry, especially with VPN/admin/staging tokens in the name. Detection logic: alert when state: resolving AND cmdb_match: false.

TH-02 · Internal naming leaked through certificate data High Network

Log source: your CT ingestion/watcher reviewing your issued certificates. Fields: common_name, san_entries, issuer, contains_internal_pattern.

Normal: public-facing certificates with public names. Suspicious: certificates whose SANs include internal-style names (hq-vpn01, core-fs02) or non-public TLDs (.internal, .corp). Detection logic: flag issuance events where any SAN matches the internal-naming regex.

TH-03 · Lookalike domain built on harvested naming patterns High Network

Log source: newly-registered-domain (NRD) feed + CT watcher. Fields: domain, match_type, registered_at, cert_issued.

Normal: unrelated registrations containing your brand by coincidence (suppliers, regional resellers). Suspicious: brand + auth tokens (login, sso, vpn) or hyphenated lookalikes registered days after a data-platform spike. Detection logic: brand match AND auth token AND age under 30 days.

TH-04 · Bulk technical-data queries about your domains Medium Network

Log source: data platform brand-alert / API audit feed (contractual). Fields: query_term, endpoint, query_count, client_country.

Normal: researchers, your own tooling (allowlisted), compliance checks. Suspicious: one client hitting multiple endpoint types for your domains in a short window. Detection logic: per-client counts above threshold across 2+ endpoint types within an hour.

TH-05 · Abandoned asset becomes takeover-prone Critical Cloud

Log source: ASM tool probing known dangling records and unclaimed hosting. Fields: hostname, dangling_record, provider_response, takeover_risk.

Normal: zero dangling records for your domains. Suspicious: any CNAME pointing to unclaimed cloud storage or hosting, or NXDOMAIN answers on records still served by nameservers. Detection logic: alert on takeover_risk: high regardless of other fields — this class is always urgent.

5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

One Sigma rule and one matching Splunk query per threat. The ASM and vendor log sources are custom categories — map them to your ingestion as noted. Tune every threshold to your baselines.

SIG-01 · Live asset missing from the inventory High

Mapping note: the name_tokens check mirrors the Sigma intent — old admin/VPN-shaped names are the ones buyers hunt; the multivalue list is flattened with mvjoin before match() so the regex works reliably. False positives: inventory sync lag — allow a 48-hour TTL for brand-new assets before paging.

SIG-02 · Internal naming pattern published in a certificate High

Mapping note: the contains_internal_pattern flag is computed by your CT pipeline with a regex like (hq|core|fs|vpn|adm)[a-z0-9-]*\.(internal|corp|local) — document it. Sigma and the SPL share one condition (flag is true), so they fire together. Fix: this is a hygiene alert — the right response is to stop issuing internal names in public certificates. To allowlist a sanctioned migration, add a NOT clause with the sanctioned certificate names on both sides.

SIG-03 · Fresh lookalike domain on brand + auth tokens High

Mapping note: SPL ranks results with certificates first — a registered lookalike with a live cert is the act-now case. Tuning: the 30-day window matches typical phishing-infrastructure lifetimes.

SIG-04 · Bulk multi-endpoint queries about your domains Medium

Mapping note: Sigma and the SPL share one threshold: the sum of query_count per API key per hour above 80 — identical firing logic. The 2+-endpoint check remains as triage context in the note field; promote it to a hard condition in a companion rule if noise requires. Tuning: raise the threshold to 3× your observed maximum after allowlisting your own tools.

SIG-05 · Dangling DNS record with takeover risk Critical

Mapping note: dedupe by hostname — probes repeat hourly and the Sigma/SPL counts reflect that by design; group at triage. Response: remove the record first, investigate second. A takeover risk is a live vulnerability, not a hygiene item.

Validation

Replay the JSON examples above through your ingestion path and confirm each rule fires; then replay one "normal" event per rule and confirm silence. For SIG-01, plant a deliberate test hostname (asmtest.example-corp.com) and prove the diff finds it.

6Real-World Case Study

An illustrative composite based on the forgotten-asset pattern that access brokers and ransomware crews exploit. Names and details are fictional; the mechanism is real and common.

Composite

Purchased technical data never appears in public incident timelines as its own step, because it leaves no victim-side trace. This composite shows the moment where it plausibly did — and the mirror query that would have beaten the attacker to it.

Victim

Helios Energy (fictional), 2,100 staff, two data centers, a 2019 cloud migration that left debris behind.

Adversary

An access broker mapping edge assets for resale to a ransomware affiliate.

Objective

Find one quiet, credible foothold and sell it.

Timeline

  1. Day −25
    The purchase

    The broker buys WHOIS history, passive DNS, and certificate records for all Helios domains: under $200 of API credits. The platform logs the queries; Helios has no alert arrangement.

  2. Day −25 to −20
    Correlation

    Passive DNS shows stage.helios-energy.example resolving to the same IP as today's mail gateway. Certificate history confirms a 2025 renewal. The CMDB, as the broker will later prove, says nothing.

  3. Day −20
    The quiet check

    One HTTPS request confirms the staging portal serves a login page for a deprecated SSO product — unpatched since the migration. The broker has a product.

  4. Day −20 to −12
    Foothold built and listed

    Exploiting the stale SSO service, the broker plants a small proxy, harvests two service accounts, and lists "verified access, energy sector" on an access market.

  5. Day −12
    Detection point — the mirror

    Helios runs its first monthly ASM mirror query after a vendor visit. The diff flags stage.helios-energy.example: resolving, unknown, token "stage". The team decommissions it within the hour and reviews the host: the proxy is found.

  6. Day −12 to −8
    Containment and eradication

    Record deleted, host isolated and rebuilt, both service accounts rotated, SSO logs reviewed for the broker's sessions, and the market listing reported through the ISAC.

  7. Day −8 to −1
    Verification

    Full passive-DNS re-mirror shows a clean diff. Two more dangling records found and removed. The listing's buyer (if any existed before takedown) finds a dead door.

  8. Day −1 to Day 30
    Recovery of process

    DNS purge added to the decommissioning checklist, mirror query scheduled weekly instead of monthly, vendor alert clauses added at renewal, and a leadership report quantifies the near-miss: no breach, one caught foothold.

Attack chain summary

  • T1597.002 — passive DNS + cert data purchased to find the staging portal
  • T1190 — exploitation of the unpatched, forgotten SSO service
  • T1078 — valid service accounts harvested for access resale
  • T1090 — proxy planted on the forgotten host
  • Averted: affiliate's T1133/T1486 chain never started

Tools in play

Attacker side: technical-data platform API credits, simple correlation scripts, a single HTTPS liveness probe, and an access-market listing.

Defender side: ASM mirror queries and dangling-record probes, CMDB diff tooling, sinkhole/logging on retired IP ranges, and the ISAC channel for listing takedown.

Impact and consequences

  • No confirmed buyer intrusion; one broker foothold eradicated
  • ≈ 120 staff-hours across ASM rollout, rebuild, and review
  • Positive regulatory story: documented pre-breach discovery
  • Cultural shift: "decommission = remove the record" became policy

Team response and communication

The ASM owner triaged the diff as P1 because of the "stage" token, and the security engineering lead joined within minutes — the runbook said unknown-live-asset equals possible foothold. Leadership got a three-line update the same day: what was found, what was removed, what changes permanently. The vendor relationship was reopened the same week to add query alerts, and the ISAC shared the indicators so two peer utilities could check their own staging hosts.

Lessons learned

  • The mirror query found in one hour what five external pentests had missed — because it ran against the same data the attacker buys.
  • DNS purge discipline is the cheapest control in the entire technique chain. It costs a checklist line.
  • Vendor alert clauses are easier to negotiate before renewal season pressure, not during.
  • "Unknown live asset" must be a P1 by default: the attacker's map is probably fresher than yours was.

7Mitigation and Defense

The attacker's power comes from the gap between your past and your inventory. Shrink the gap and the purchased data buys them nothing. This matches MITRE's official mitigation position for the T1597 family (M1056 Pre-compromise): the activity happens outside enterprise defenses, so efforts focus on minimizing the amount and sensitivity of data available to external parties.

Home and small-office advice

R

Retire cleanly

Shut down an old router, NAS, or blog? Delete its port-forward and DNS name the same day. Forgotten home services are scanned for free.

U

Update what stays

Small offices often keep one ancient camera or printer portal "temporarily". If it must live, patch it and put it behind MFA.

D

Check your domain's past

Free WHOIS and DNS history lookups show what the world remembers about your domain. If an old name surprises you, clean it up.

Response checklist

  • Confirm the signal is genuine and allowlisted tooling is excluded
  • Run the mirror query on yourself: WHOIS history, passive DNS, certificates
  • Diff results against the CMDB; list every unknown-live or takeover-prone asset
  • Purge or secure each finding; rotate credentials on anything that served logins
  • Review logs on retired IP ranges for liveness probes or footholds
  • Block and report lookalike domains to the registrar and your feed provider
  • Update the CMDB and the exposure-history entry in the risk register
  • Write the one-page summary: finding, action, process change
