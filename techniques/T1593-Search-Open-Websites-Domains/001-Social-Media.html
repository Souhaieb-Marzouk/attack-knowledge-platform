Overview

A plain-language summary of the technique, why it matters, and what this page will teach you.

What is this technique?

T1593.001 is a sub-technique of T1593 Search Open Websites/Domains. It covers adversaries searching social media — platforms such as LinkedIn, X/Twitter, Facebook and Instagram — for information about a victim. They look for staff names and job titles, skills and tools mentioned in profiles, contact details, office locations, photos, and the relationships between people.

This is the people-focused lane of open-website research. Where search engines reveal systems and files, social media reveals the humans — and humans remain the easiest way into any organization.

Why it matters to defenders

Everything collected here makes the next attack personal. A message that uses your real name and your real manager's name is very hard to reject. Add a project from your profile and a plausible job offer, and most people reply. Documented espionage campaigns used this pattern at scale: "Operation Dream Job" and "Operation In(ter)ception" (2020) were built almost entirely on social media research. Fake recruiters then converted that research into access.

You cannot log what happens on a social platform. Your controls sit on the supply side. Reduce what staff expose, teach staff how the trick works, and give them a one-click way to report a suspicious recruiter.

Animated Simulation

A step-by-step story of the documented "fake recruiter" pattern: how an attacker turns public profiles into a targeting list, and what the security team sees while it happens.

How to use this

The simulation starts paused. Press Play, or click the number chips to jump. Hover any box to see what it is. The written story below repeats the same steps in words.

The written story

  1. Step 1 — Build a fake recruiter profile. The attacker creates a professional profile with a photo that is not theirs, a polished work history and some copied posts. It looks real enough to pass a quick check.
  2. Step 2 — Search for the target's staff. Using a company filter on the platform, they list employees of the fictional target org and sort by role: engineers first, then IT and finance.
  3. Step 3 — Map the org chart. Job titles, tenure and skills in profiles reveal who leads what, who recently changed roles, and who might feel overlooked — useful pressure points.
  4. Step 4 — Harvest contact details. Some profiles list personal emails or links to other accounts. The attacker records contact routes that skip the company's mail gateway.
  5. Step 5 — Send connection requests. They connect with a small set of chosen staff. Accepting feels harmless: recruiters contact engineers every day.
  6. Step 6 — Deliver the "opportunity". Each accepting contact gets a friendly message and a PDF job description. The document is the delivery vehicle for the next stage of the attack.
  7. Step 7 — The SOC view. None of this touched company systems. The only early signals are human: someone notices the profile is odd, or the recruiter seems too interested in internal details.
  8. Step 8 — The outcome. Staff who engaged are moved into a phishing pipeline (T1566/T1598). The company's best chance was step 7: a trained, fast report that warns the others.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

Why social media is the attacker's favorite source on people, how fake identities work, and what each platform gives away — described for defenders, with defensive lab exercises only.

When and why attackers use it

Social media is the only place a company publishes its own attack map. It shows who works there and who reports to whom. It also shows the tools each person uses and what they worry about. Attackers prefer it because it is cheap, legal to read, and stays invisible to the victim's defenses. Typical uses:

  • Build a target list for spearphishing, ranked by privilege and by how reachable the person seems.
  • Write a believable story: a recruiter who knows your tech stack, or a vendor who knows your reorganization.
  • Choose the moment: job changes, projects close to a deadline and office moves are public on purpose.
  • Pick the contact route: a personal email on a profile bypasses the corporate mail gateway entirely.

What each platform gives away

Platform typeRich source ofRisk for the victim

General social appsLocations, office photos, badge photos, family detailsPretexting (inventing a false role to trick someone) material, physical recon hints

How fake identities are built (high level)

Documented campaigns reuse the same pattern. A persona starts with a photo that is not the operator's (stolen or generated). The work history fits the target sector. Some weeks of copied or reposted content make the profile look established. The persona then connects with targets and starts a slow, polite conversation. Recruitment is a perfect cover: it gives a reason to talk for weeks and a reason to send a "job description" document. Defenders should note what makes personas detectable: recycled photos, thin post history, endorsements from other new accounts, and unusual curiosity about internal tools.

Ethics and legality — lab only

This page describes techniques so defenders can recognize them. Do not create fake personas to test real staff without written authorization and strict rules — even authorized simulations must never harvest credentials or deliver malware. Exercises against your own staff belong to a controlled awareness program with HR approval.

Defensive lab exercise: audit your own staff exposure

Do this with HR and legal approval; it touches staff data.

checklist — profile hygiene audit (your own org)
1. Open each platform in a private window (not logged in).
2. Search your company name. List who is visible and what each
   profile shows: title, tools, photo, contact details.
3. Flag: personal emails on profiles, badge/office photos,
   screenshots with internal names, "current project" details.
4. Check job ads for tool versions and internal system names.
5. Score each finding (what could a lure use?) and assign an owner.
6. Re-run quarterly; track the trend, not a one-time result.

Prerequisites and conditions

  • Staff present on platforms — effectively always true for any hiring company.
  • Public profile visibility by default, or staff who accept unknown contacts.
  • Patience: personas need a believable history, so campaigns prepare weeks in advance.

Common variations and attacker mistakes

  • Variation: several personas cross-endorsing each other to look established.
  • Variation: impersonating a real recruiter from a known agency, with a lookalike domain for follow-up mail.
  • Variation: monitoring targets quietly (follows, likes) for months to time the approach.
  • Mistake: moving too fast — a fresh profile with 30 connections asking for a CV (curriculum vitae — a resume) on day one gets reported.
  • Mistake: reusing personas across campaigns. Defenders can cluster the activity, so the attacker must rotate them (change them).

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

The research itself is invisible to your logs. Your sensors are human reports, platform actions, and the moment the lure finally touches your mail or endpoints.

Hard truth

Profile views, searches and connection requests happen entirely on the platform. There is no log line for them in your SIEM (security information and event management). Treat staff reports as your primary detection and everything else as confirmation.

What defenders CAN use

  • Staff reports: "a recruiter asked odd questions" is your earliest and best signal. Make reporting one click.
  • Platform impersonation reports: most platforms remove fake profiles that use your brand or staff photos.
  • Mail gateway: when the lure moves to email, subject and attachment patterns become matchable.
  • Identity signals: risky sign-ins (new device, new country) shortly after an employee reports a recruiter contact.
  • Leaked-credential feeds: if a targeted person's personal email appears in a dump, raise their monitoring.

What it leaves behind

  • Platform-side artifacts only: profiles, requests, messages — held by the platform, not you.
  • Screenshots and message text from staff reports — preserve them for patterns across targets.
  • Later, the lure itself: a PDF or DOC quoting real names and tools (evidence the research happened).
  • Follow-on domains for the "company careers page": catchable by brand monitoring.

Where to look

SourceWhat you might findValue
Awareness / report channelFake recruiter contacts, odd questions, suspicious profilesCritical
Mail gateway logsLure emails referencing public details; unknown "HR" sendersHigh
Identity provider risk logsRisky sign-ins soon after a reported contactHigh
Endpoint / proxyDocument detonations (opening a file in a safe test box), lookalike "job portal" domainsHigh
Platform reportsImpersonation of staff or the company brandMedium

Short triage flow for a staff report

  1. Thank and preserve. Get screenshots or forwarded messages; never shame the reporter.
  2. Verify the profile. Photo source, history depth, mutual connections — record why it is fake.
  3. Report and block on the platform, then check which other staff accepted or replied.
  4. Warn the exact people contacted, with a short "this is what it looks like" note.
  5. Hunt for the lure: search the mail gateway for the sender domain, subject words and attachment names.
  6. Watch identities for 72 hours: risky sign-ins, new MFA (multi-factor authentication) devices, password resets for contacted staff.
Why this matters

A single early report shortens the attacker's window from weeks to days. Campaigns like the 2020 recruiter-lure espionage operations succeeded partly because targets did not know the pattern. Training changes that at near-zero cost.

Detection Engineering Deep Dive

The practical core of this page: regulations, threat intelligence, ranked threats, log patterns, and ready-to-adapt detections. Because the research is platform-side, every detection watches the downstream signs: the lure, the identity use, and the exposure that made it work.

Official ATT&CK detection strategy

ATT&CK lists analytic AN1944 under detection strategy DET0812 — Detection of Social Media. The official text says this activity often has a high occurrence and a high false-positive rate, and it usually happens outside the visibility of the target organization, which makes detection difficult. ATT&CK advises focusing detection on related attack stages, such as Initial Access. The detections below follow that advice: they watch the lure reaching your staff and the follow-on stages.

Note

How to apply it

  • Add reported personas and lure indicators to a watchlist; correlate new reports against it.
  • Share sanitized patterns (subject lines, domains, attachment names) with your ISAC and receive theirs.
  • Brief HR and recruiting teams first — they live on these platforms and spot anomalies earliest.

Logs and Patterns

For each threat: which logs carry the signal, which fields matter, what normal looks like, and what is worth an alert. Field names differ by vendor; adjust them to your environment.

Threat 1 — Fake-recruiter lure email delivered

Log sources
Mail gateway / O365 message trace; staff report channel; endpoint EDR (endpoint detection and response)
Key fields
SenderAddress, RecipientAddress, Subject, AttachmentCount, FromIP
Normal
HR mail and known applicant-tracking domains like workday.com, greenhouse.io and lever.co
Suspicious
An unsolicited recruiter message with an attachment from a first-contact sender domain
Detection logic
Alert when a first-contact external sender pairs career words with an attachment; correlate with staff reports; gateway detonation adds a second check

Threat 2 — Staff resolve a lookalike career or brand domain

Log sources
DNS resolver logs (recursive resolver or DNS security service)
Key fields
query (requested domain), client_ip, query_type, answers
Normal
Staff resolve real job boards, known recruiter agencies and your own domains
Suspicious
Queries for domains that combine your brand with career or hiring words, especially on young domains
Detection logic
Alert on brand + career-token domains, excluding your own domain and known job boards

Threat 3 — Credentials submitted to a fake career portal

Log sources
Proxy logs (for example Zeek HTTP); the portal visit alone is not enough — watch what is sent to it
Key fields
method (POST), host, uri, user, client_ip
Normal
POSTs to your own careers site and to known SaaS login pages
Suspicious
A POST to a login, signin, apply or auth path on a brand + career lookalike domain
Detection logic
Alert on credential POSTs to lookalike career portals; treat each match as a compromise and reset the account

Threat 4 — Risky account activity after reported contact

Log sources
Identity protection sign-in logs (e.g. Azure AD); MFA and password-reset logs
Key fields
riskLevelName, deviceDetail, location, userPrincipalName
Normal
Sign-ins from managed devices in expected regions
Suspicious
Medium or high risk sign-ins that are not remediated, for a user who reported a recruiter contact within 72 hours
Detection logic
Correlate report tickets with risky sign-ins in a time window around the report

Threat 5 — Lure campaign at scale

Log sources
Mail-gateway volume logs; look for one sender domain or one subject template across many staff
Key fields
SenderAddress (domain), Subject (template), RecipientAddress count, time window
Normal
Legitimate mass HR mail (benefits, all-hands) from known internal domains
Suspicious
The same sender domain or an identical subject template reaching 3 or more staff within 24 hours, especially from a fresh domain
Detection logic
Group messages by sender domain and subject template; alert when one template touches several staff in one day

Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

Five detections, one per threat. Each Sigma rule is valid YAML; each has a matching Splunk SPL query. Adapt indexes and field names to your environment and test in a measure-only window first.

Detection 1 — Recruiter-lure email pattern High

Covers threat 1 — fake-recruiter lure email delivered.

Sigma — recruiter lure email (T1593.001)
title: Recruiter Lure Email Pattern
id: 4d8c2b6f-91a3-4e27-8c50-6f1b9d3a7e24
status: test
description: >
  Detects external messages that look like unsolicited recruiter outreach
  with an attachment, a pattern documented in espionage campaigns that
  begin with T1593.001 research and continue with T1566.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1593/001/
  - https://www.welivesecurity.com/2020/06/17/operation-interception-aerospace-military-companies-cyberespionage/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-01-15
logsource:
  product: office365
  service: exchange
detection:
  selection_subject:
    Subject|contains:
      - 'opportunity'
      - 'interview'
      - 'position'
      - 'job offer'
  selection_attach:
    AttachmentCount|gte: 1
  filter_known_hr:
    SenderAddress|endswith:
      - '@workday.com'
      - '@greenhouse.io'
      - '@lever.co'
  condition: selection_subject and selection_attach and not filter_known_hr
fields:
  - SenderAddress
  - RecipientAddress
  - Subject
  - AttachmentNames
falsepositives:
  - Genuine external recruiters contacting staff who applied publicly.
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1593.001
  - attack.t1566.001
  - attack.tactic.reconnaissance
  - attack.ta0043
Splunk SPL — recruiter lure email
index=mail sourcetype=ms:o365:management Workload=Exchange
    Subject IN ("*opportunity*", "*interview*", "*position*", "*job offer*")
    AttachmentCount>=1
| search NOT SenderAddress IN ("*@workday.com", "*@greenhouse.io", "*@lever.co")
| stats count, values(AttachmentNames) AS attachments, latest(_time) AS last_seen
    by SenderAddress, RecipientAddress
| sort - count
  • Mapping note: assumes the O365 Management Activity API is onboarded with sourcetype ms:o365:management; Subject and AttachmentCount come from that schema. If you use another gateway, map to its message-trace fields.
  • False positives: legitimate agency recruiters; maintain an allowlist and require the attachment to trigger.
  • Tuning: alert on first-time sender domains only; auto-suppress domains verified by HR.

Detection 2 — Staff resolve a lookalike career or brand domain High

Covers threat 2 — staff contact with lookalike portals.

Sigma — staff DNS query for lookalike career domain (T1593.001)
title: Staff DNS Query for Lookalike Career or Brand Domain
id: 8a1f4c72-3b95-4e60-b7d2-91c4a6f08e33
status: test
description: >
  Detects DNS queries from staff devices for domains that combine the
  company brand with career or hiring words. Attackers register such
  lookalike domains after social media research and host fake career
  portals on them during T1593.001 campaigns.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1593/001/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1656/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-01-15
logsource:
  category: dns
detection:
  selection_brand:
    QueryName|re: '(?i)(acme).*(career|careers|job|jobs|recruit|talent|hire|hiring)'
  filter_own:
    QueryName|endswith: '.acme.example'
  filter_known:
    QueryName|endswith:
      - 'linkedin.com'
      - 'indeed.com'
      - 'glassdoor.com'
  condition: selection_brand and not filter_own and not filter_known
fields:
  - QueryName
  - ClientIP
falsepositives:
  - Genuine job boards that contain the brand string in their domain name.
level: high
tags:
  - attack.t1593.001
  - attack.t1656
  - attack.tactic.reconnaissance
  - attack.ta0043
Splunk SPL — lookalike domain resolution
index=proxy sourcetype=zeek:dns
| regex query="(?i)acme.*(career|careers|job|jobs|recruit|talent|hire|hiring)"
| where NOT like(query, "%.acme.example")
| where NOT like(query, "%linkedin.com") AND NOT like(query, "%indeed.com") AND NOT like(query, "%glassdoor.com")
| stats count as lookups, values(client_ip) as staff by query
| sort - lookups
  • Field assumptions: query is the requested domain (your feed may call it QueryName or dns.query.name); client_ip is the asking device; index and sourcetype vary — zeek:dns is a common choice. Covers threat 2.
  • False positives: genuine job boards that contain the brand string in their domain name; keep an allowlist.
  • Tuning: add your known legitimate job boards to the filter list and review new matches weekly.

status: test
description: >

author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-01-15

  product: office365
  service: exchange
detection:
  selection_subject:
    Subject|contains:
      - 'opportunity'
      - 'interview'
      - 'position'
      - 'job offer'
  timeframe: 24h
  condition: selection_subject | count(RecipientAddress) by SenderDomain > 3
fields:
  - SenderAddress
  - RecipientAddress
  - Subject
falsepositives:
  - A real recruiter campaign from a legitimate applicant-tracking system; reuse the known-domain filter from Detection 1 here.
level: medium
tags:
  - attack.t1593.001
  - attack.tactic.reconnaissance
  - attack.ta0043
Splunk SPL — lure campaign volume
index=mail sourcetype=ms:o365:management
| where match(Subject, "(?i)(opportunity|interview|position|job offer)")
| eval SenderDomain=mvindex(split(SenderAddress, "@"), -1)
| bin _time span=24h
| stats dc(RecipientAddress) AS staff, values(Subject) AS subjects by SenderDomain, _time
| where staff >= 3
| sort - staff
  • Mapping note: the Sigma aggregation counts distinct recipients per sender domain in 24h; the SPL does the same with dc() after a 24h bin. The query assumes one event per delivered message with sender, recipient and subject fields — common for gateway and message-trace exports; the O365 management feed may name them differently, so map SenderAddress/RecipientAddress/Subject to your schema.
  • False positives: a legitimate recruiter campaign from a real applicant-tracking domain; the known-domain filter from Detection 1 removes the common ones.
  • Tuning: start with staff >= 3 and raise it if your industry gets routine recruiting mail; add a filter for your own ATS domains.

Real-World Case Study

"Operation In(ter)ception" (2020), documented by ESET, with the closely related "Operation Dream Job" (ClearSky). The best-documented example of T1593.001-style research converted into an espionage campaign. Details are simplified from public reporting for teaching.

Context

Between January and June 2020, security researchers observed a large-scale campaign targeting employees of aerospace, military and defense-related companies. ESET reported European aerospace and military companies; ClearSky reported Middle East targets under the Dream Job name. The attackers ran fake recruiter identities on professional social networks. Targets — engineers, salespeople and HR staff at firms linked to defense projects — were approached with genuine-looking job opportunities. The campaign is publicly linked by ESET to the Lazarus group; ClearSky documented the same playbook as "Operation Dream Job". It shows the complete arc of this technique: research on people, a tailored approach, and a document that turns trust into access.

Timeline

  • January 2020First wave: fake recruiter profiles begin approaching staff at defense and aerospace firms with job offers.
  • Early 2020Targets receive a PDF describing a plausible role. Those who reply are sent a "job specification" document with unusual macro or template behavior.
  • One morning09:42 — A staff member reports the message to the SOC mailbox.
  • Same morning10:15 — The mail gateway retro-search finds three more recipients; every copy is purged.
  • Spring 2020The documents fetch additional content from attacker-controlled servers (remote template injection), then load first-stage malware on some machines.
  • June 2020ESET publishes "Operation In(ter)ception" describing the campaign, personas, infrastructure and payloads. ClearSky’s July 2020 ‘Operation Dream Job’ reporting (a month after ESET’s) connects the pattern to Lazarus.
  • After publicationDomains and accounts are taken down; defenders sector-wide add the lure patterns to their mail and awareness programs.
  • LaterVariants of the same recruiter-lure pattern keep appearing against new sectors — the playbook outlives any single takedown.

Attack chain

  1. Research (this technique): map staff at target firms from professional networks; choose engineers and managers; note skills and projects for personalization.
  2. Persona: operate convincing fake recruiter profiles with believable history.
  3. Contact: connection requests and friendly messages; a PDF job description builds comfort.
  4. Delivery: a "job specification" document is sent, often password-protected or macro-enabled to slip basic scanning.
  5. Execution: remote template injection fetches a payload; a first-stage implant runs.
  6. Follow-through: espionage tooling and long-term access where execution succeeded.

Detection point

The first reliable technical detection is the document step: the mail or the opened document fetching content from an unfamiliar domain (Word reaching out is never normal). The earliest possible detection is human: a recruiter profile that asks too much. In this campaign, the research and contact phases produced no victim-side logs at all — exactly as this page predicts.

Containment

Quarantine the lure messages and domains; isolate endpoints that opened the documents; block the payload domains at proxy and DNS level; warn all staff contacted by the same personas.

Eradication

Hunt for the implant's persistence (scheduled tasks, run keys, services); remove it; revoke and rotate any credentials typed during the engagement; re-image affected machines.

Recovery

Return cleaned machines to service with extra monitoring; keep the persona domains on blocklists; hold a review with HR and recruiting staff who saw the approach.

Impact and consequences

  • Espionage risk: implants on machines belonging to defense-sector staff put project data at risk.
  • Credential exposure: targets who engaged were likely asked for information that eased later intrusions.
  • Response cost: sector-wide hunts for the documented indicators; weeks of mailbox triage and endpoint forensics.
  • Trust damage: targets learned that a normal recruiter message can be an attack — a lasting change in how staff treat strangers online.

Team response and communication

Victim organizations that detected the document step followed a clean sequence: isolate, purge the mail, rotate credentials, warn contacted staff, and — importantly — tell staff plainly what happened without blaming anyone who engaged. Organizations that had awareness training recognized the pattern faster. The public reporting itself became the sector's best training material.

Lessons learned
  1. The research phase is invisible; train the sensors you have. Staff are the only detector during steps 1–5.
  2. Normal is the camouflage. Recruiter contact is routine in engineering; the defense is knowing the tell-tale signs, not suspicion of everyone.
  3. Documents must justify their network activity. A Word file fetching from an odd domain is a first-class detection.
  4. Password-protected or macro "job specs" are a red flag — teach HR and recruiting to forward, not open.
  5. MFA reduces the value of stolen passwords. Stolen credentials from a convinced target should not equal access.

Mitigation and Defense

ATT&CK lists the Pre-compromise mitigation (M1056): you cannot stop the research, so reduce what it finds and what it can convert. Controls below are ordered by effect. M1056 Pre-compromise is the official ATT&CK mitigation for this technique: minimize the amount and sensitivity of data available to external parties.

Home and small office

  • Verify recruiters out-of-band: a real recruiter will not mind you checking the agency's official site or calling the company switchboard.
  • Never open job documents on a work machine before the offer is verified; open PDFs in a viewer that does not run scripts, and never enable macros.
  • Trim your profiles: no personal email, no badge or office photos, no "current project" specifics.
  • Separate accounts: keep a personal email for platforms that is different from your work identity.
  • Report it: report the profile on the platform and tell your security team even if you are unsure — especially then.

Daily habits

  • Zero-age the report queue: every staff report gets a first response the same day.
  • Check gateway hits against the persona watchlist.
  • Glance at risky sign-ins for users with open report tickets.

Weekly habits

  • Review new lookalike domains and platform impersonation reports.
  • Tune the lure-pattern rules; retire rules with no hits and no value.
  • Spot-check five public staff profiles as an outsider would see them.

Monthly & quarterly habits

  • Monthly: report-volume and outcome metrics to leadership.
  • Quarterly: profile hygiene audit; refresh lure scenarios from TI.
  • Quarterly: practice the fake-recruiter runbook with HR and legal.
