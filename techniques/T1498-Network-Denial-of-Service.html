Overview

Network Denial of Service is an attack on availability. The attacker does not steal data. Instead, the attacker sends so much unwanted network traffic that the target becomes slow or stops working.

What the technique is

A denial of service (DoS) attack makes a service unavailable. A distributed denial of service (DDoS) uses many machines at the same time, usually a botnet — a large group of infected devices that the attacker controls. The traffic can be volumetric (raw bandwidth), protocol-based (half-open connections), or application-based (many real-looking requests). All three share one goal: use up a resource the victim needs.

Why it matters

When a shop, hospital system, bank portal, or game server goes offline, the business stops earning and customers lose trust. Recovery costs money, and staff lose hours. Some groups also use DDoS as a distraction while they try a quieter intrusion, or as extortion ("pay us or we take you offline again").

Where it fits in the attack chain

T1498 belongs to the Impact tactic in MITRE ATT&CK. It usually appears in one of three places: as the final destructive step after an intrusion, as the main goal itself (for example, a rented botnet attacking a competitor), or as a cover for another, quieter attack. Some attackers do not need access to the victim at all — they only need enough machines and bandwidth to send the flood.

    Written version of the simulation

    Every animated step is described here in text. This is the accessible version of the story, and it is the same story the diagram tells.

    1. Step 1 — The attacker gives the order. The bot herder sends a command to thousands of infected devices. Defender: usually silent; rare outbound patterns from infected hosts can be an early clue.
    2. Step 2 — Quick reconnaissance. A few bots probe the target's public services to see which ports answer. Defender: short scans to many ports from few sources; rate-limit and log them.
    3. Step 3 — The flood starts. The bots send a huge, mixed stream of packets at the target edge. Defender: link load rises fast; deny counts on the firewall spike.
    4. Step 4 — The link saturates. The internet link and edge queues fill up. Even valid packets are dropped. Defender: interface counters, NetFlow volume, and connection-table alerts fire.
    5. Step 5 — Real users are locked out. Normal customers time out and see errors. Defender: synthetic checks from outside fail first; support tickets rise.
    6. Step 6 — The SOC detects the attack. Flow analytics and firewall alerts confirm a volumetric DDoS. Defender: open the DDoS runbook; call the ISP and CDN contacts.
    7. Step 7 — Upstream scrubbing starts. The ISP or CDN routes traffic through a scrubbing center and forwards only clean traffic. Defender: watch clean-traffic graphs and confirm the service recovers.
    8. Step 8 — Recovery and review. Traffic returns to baseline. The team writes the timeline and improves thresholds. Defender: keep packets, flow records, and ISP reports for the post-incident review.

    Red team and hacker perspective

    This section explains how attackers think and prepare. It is written for defenders who need to understand how attackers work. Every command example is for a lab you own — never test these techniques against systems you do not have written permission to touch.

    When and why an attacker uses it

    Network DoS is cheap, loud, and fast. Criminal groups use it for extortion; competitors and hacktivists use it to damage reputation; state-linked groups use it to disrupt services or to force the victim's defenders to look at noise while something quieter happens elsewhere. Because rented booter services (paid DDoS-for-hire sites) cost only a few dollars, almost anyone can order an attack without owning a single bot.

    How it is performed

    The attacker needs three things: enough senders (a botnet, a rented stresser, or a cloud burst), a target that is reachable (an IP address, a domain, or an origin server hidden behind a CDN), and a flood type the target cannot absorb. Volumetric floods fill the pipe, protocol floods fill state tables, and application floods fill worker threads and databases with expensive requests.

    Prerequisites and conditions

    • Firepower: a botnet, a rented stresser, or many cheap cloud instances.
    • Reachability: the true IP of the target, or a way to bypass CDN protection.
    • Timing: attacks often follow public events, payment deadlines, or rival product launches.
    • Knowledge: knowing whether the target's DNS, game server, or API is the weakest link.

    Legal and ethical line: launching a DoS attack against systems you do not own or do not have written authorization to test is a crime in most countries, even "just to see if it works". Use only isolated lab networks and your own hardware or virtual machines.

    Lab-only teaching examples

    The commands below are safe only inside an isolated lab, for example a virtual network with two machines: one attack box and one test server. They generate load so you can watch how sensors react.

    Bash — lab only
    # LAB ONLY: run against a test server you own, on an isolated network.
# Gentle TCP connection load to exercise SYN-rate detections:
hping3 -S -p 443 --rate 500 --rand-source 10.20.0.50

# Baseline bandwidth with iperf3 (run the server side first):
iperf3 -s                    # on the test server
iperf3 -c 10.20.0.50 -u -b 50M    # client: UDP load, 50 Mbit/s

# Local HTTP load to exercise web-rate detections:
ab -n 2000 -c 200 http://10.20.0.50/prices

    Even the commands above can crash weak lab equipment. Start with low rates, watch the target, and stop as soon as the effect is visible. The goal is to generate sensor events, not to break things.

    Common variations

    • Volumetric: raw UDP or ICMP floods measured in Gbit/s (see T1498.001).
    • Reflection: third-party servers reply to the victim with amplified answers (T1498.002).
    • Protocol: SYN floods that fill half-open connection tables.
    • Application: floods of valid-looking HTTP requests — cheap for the attacker, expensive for the server.
    • Multi-vector: several flood types at once, so the victim cannot block one pattern.

    Mistakes attackers make

    • Flooding from one or two sources — easy to block at the edge.
    • Using botnet nodes that are also on the victim's customer list, so C2 traffic shows up first.
    • Announcing the attack on social media before starting, which gives defenders warning time.
    • Reusing the same booter account, which law enforcement can trace across campaigns.

    Blue team and SOC perspective

    Defenders cannot stop packets at the attack source, but they can see the attack early, react fast, and keep the service available. This section shows what to watch, what the attack leaves behind, and how to triage the first minutes.

    How defenders can see it

    Three vantage points work best: upstream (ISP or CDN dashboards show the attack before it lands), edge (firewall and router counters show deny spikes and queue drops), and inside (server metrics show connection backlogs and error rates). A view from only one place hides half the story.

    What artifacts it leaves

    • NetFlow records with extreme packet-per-second counts to one destination.
    • Firewall deny logs from thousands of unique source addresses.
    • Router interface counters near line rate, with output drops.
    • CDN or WAF logs showing request floods with odd user agents or paths.
    • Synthetic monitoring failures from outside regions while internal checks pass.

    Why it matters to the SOC

    A DDoS is not a malware event: there is no host to isolate. The response is network engineering plus communication. The SOC that only knows "restart the server" will fail. The SOC with a tested DDoS runbook, ISP contacts, and clear thresholds keeps the outage to minutes instead of hours.

    Fits into the attack chain — defender view

    Treat a DDoS as both an incident and a signal. Ask two questions at once: is this the whole attack, or is it cover for something quieter such as a phishing wave or an exploit attempt against a distracted team? Check other alert queues during any DDoS, and verify that authentication and VPN logs show no unusual success rates while the noise runs.

    First-minutes triage flow

    1. Confirm it is real. Compare current traffic with the 30-day baseline for the same hour and weekday. A launch-day spike is not always an attack.
    2. Classify the flood. Which port and protocol? How many sources? Volumetric, protocol, or application layer? This decides who you call.
    3. Check blast radius. Is one service down or the whole edge? Are other alert queues (IDS, auth, WAF) busy with a second event?
    4. Engage mitigation. Enable scrubbing, rate limits, or geo/ASN filters per the runbook. Never invent the plan during the attack.
    5. Communicate. Tell leadership, support, and customers what is happening and what to expect. Update every 30 minutes until stable.
    6. Preserve evidence. Save flow records, firewall logs, and PCAPs before rotation. You will need them for the report and for the ISP.

    5.2Threat intelligence

    Good DDoS intelligence tells you who attacks whom, with which flood types, and at what scale. Feed that knowledge into detections, exposure checks, and stakeholder briefings.

    Sources and collection

    • MITRE ATT&CK: T1498 and its sub-techniques give a shared language for reports and detections.
    • Vendor reporting: quarterly DDoS trend reports from Cloudflare, Akamai, Radware, and NETSCOUT describe sizes, vectors, and targets.
    • OSINT: CERT advisories, Shadowserver reports about your exposed or abused services, and BGP or DNS telemetry communities.
    • Commercial feeds: botnet C2 lists and reflector lists help you find infected machines inside your network and open services on it.
    • ISACs: sector information sharing communities warn about active campaigns against peers.
    • Internal telemetry: your own NetFlow baselines and honeypots show what an attack looks like on your specific edge.

    Analysis and confidence

    Tag every intel item with a confidence level and an action. High confidence: flood families such as Mirai and its variants, and public incident reports such as the 2016 Dyn DNS attack. Medium: attributions to named groups, which vendors often infer from infrastructure reuse. Low: motive claims posted on social media by attack groups. A claim is not proof until another source supports it.

    ATT&CK procedure examples
    APT28 (G0007) — DDoS against the World Anti-Doping Agency in 2016; Lucifer (S0532) — TCP, UDP, and HTTP DoS capability; NKAbuse (S1107) — multi-protocol network DoS after install
    Botnet families
    Mirai and its variants remain the reference case for consumer-device floods
    Hacktivist waves
    collective-driven multi-target DDoS campaigns against government, health, and retail sites — claims are public, proof varies
    Extortion (RDDoS)
    groups send a demand note, prove power with a short burst, then threaten a longer attack
    Campaign signals
    extortion emails before the flood; multi-vector waves; peak sizes above 1 Tbit/s
    Typical action
    enrich detections with reflector lists; verify your own assets are not open amplifiers

    Confidence discipline: the ATT&CK procedure examples above are high confidence, because MITRE cites public reporting for each one. Attribution of hacktivist waves to named sponsors is usually medium or low confidence, because evidence often rests on infrastructure reuse. A claim posted on social media is not proof until a second source supports it.

    Application routine: every month, check Shadowserver and similar scanners for your external IP ranges. Finding your own misused DNS or NTP servers before attackers do removes you from the weapon pool and from future abuse reports.

    The table ranks the realistic ways this technique hits an organization. Severity reflects impact and likelihood combined. The platform column shows where the threat lives, and all ATT&CK platforms for this technique are covered: network, cloud, Windows, Linux, macOS, and identity services.

    #ThreatSeverityPlatformDescription and preconditionsImpact and justification
    1 Multi-vector volumetric DDoS from a rented botnet Critical Network Cloud Mixed floods (UDP, SYN, DNS) from tens of thousands of bots. Needs only a rented stresser and the target's public address. Total service outage and saturated links. Highest likelihood and impact combination for internet-facing organizations; mitigation needs upstream capacity.
    2 DNS reflection amplification Critical Network Open resolvers reply to a spoofed victim address with large answers. Needs spoofable senders and open resolvers (see T1498.002). Extreme amplification from few attacker machines; can saturate even 100 Gbit/s edges without any botnet.
    3 TCP SYN protocol flood High Network Half-open connections fill server and firewall state tables (see T1498.001). Needs spoofed sources or a mid-size botnet. New connections fail while established ones survive; hits stateful devices hardest.
    4 Cloud edge saturation with cost shock High Cloud Flood traffic lands on cloud load balancers and public IPs; auto-scaling adds instances against a flood. Needs a cloud-hosted public service. Service degradation plus a sudden large cloud bill; autoscaling alone cannot beat a volumetric flood.
    5 Ransom DDoS (RDDoS) campaign High Network Group sends a demand note, proves power with a short burst, then threatens a longer attack. Needs a credible stresser platform. Business pressure and payment dilemmas; historical campaigns hit finance, travel, and e-commerce sectors.
    6 Flood exhausts Windows server connection tables Medium Windows SYN or UDP floods target Windows servers running IIS, RRAS, or other public services; the nonpaged pool and half-open queue fill. Needs a reachable Windows service. The Windows service stops accepting new connections before the link is full; event 5157 block storms and SYN-RECV growth are the visible traces.
    7 Flood exhausts Linux host network stack Medium Linux SYN backlog and conntrack tables fill on Linux servers and appliances. Needs a reachable Linux service and enough flood volume. Kernel logs the "possible SYN flooding" message, ListenDrops climb, new connections stall; containers share the same host stack and fail together.
    8 Auth-portal flood causes lockouts and login downtime Medium Identity Floods against VPN concentrators or SSO login pages keep authentication services busy or unreachable. Needs a public login endpoint. Staff cannot log in during the attack, which blocks all other response work; recovery is usually quick after filtering.
    9 macOS-hosted internal services overwhelmed Low macOS Build servers, file shares, or dev tools on macOS are flooded from inside the network. Needs an internal attacker or infected host. Rare and low impact: most macOS systems sit behind the edge and never face the raw flood; include it so asset coverage is complete.
    10 Legitimate flash crowd mistaken for attack Low Network Cloud A marketing event or news spike looks like a flood in raw graphs. Preconditions: weak baselines and missing business calendar. Wrong mitigations block real customers. Low harm if baselines and communication exist; include it to avoid false positives.

    Scope boundary — T1498 versus T1499. A flood of valid-looking HTTP requests that exhausts application workers is an endpoint DoS in ATT&CK: MITRE maps application exhaustion floods to T1499.003 and service exhaustion floods to T1499.002. T1498 covers attacks that exhaust the network path — bandwidth, state tables, and edge devices. In practice both can arrive together, so monitor the WAF path as well, but tag detections with the technique ID that matches the resource being exhausted.

    5.4Logs and patterns

    Each card below matches a threat from 5.3. It lists the log sources, the fields that matter, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and one realistic event example you can test your parser against.

    Threat 1 — Multi-vector flood: firewall deny spike

    Sources: perimeter firewall traffic logs, router ACL logs. Key fields: action, srcip, dstip, dstport, proto, sentbyte, rcvdbyte, count of events. Normal: a steady few hundred denies per minute from scanners and expired sessions. Suspicious: thousands of denies per minute, thousands of unique sources, and inbound byte counts far above the same-hour baseline.

    JSON — firewall traffic log (FortiGate style)
    {"date":"2026-10-22","time":"09:41:17","devid":"FGT-edge-01","vd":"root",
 "action":"deny","proto":"17","srcip":"203.0.113.44","srcport":"40012",
 "dstip":"198.51.100.10","dstport":"443","service":"HTTPS",
 "sentbyte":1420,"rcvdbyte":0,"duration":0,"crlevel":"medium","craction":"65536"}

    Detection logic: count deny events in a five-minute window; alert when the count is ten times the 30-day baseline for that hour. The zero reply bytes with nonzero sent bytes are typical for one-way flood traffic.

    Threat 2 — Reflection flood seen from the victim edge

    Sources: ISP NetFlow exports, firewall logs, router interface counters. Key fields: src_ip diversity, src_port (53, 123, 11211, 1900, 19), proto, packets, bytes. Normal: DNS replies from your known resolvers only; few other UDP services. Suspicious: inbound UDP from thousands of different source IPs on reflector ports, with reply bytes far larger than any request you sent.

    CSV — NetFlow v5 record
    2026-10-22 09:41:20,192.0.2.77,198.51.100.10,53,44120,17,0,1428,1,PKTS=1
2026-10-22 09:41:20,192.0.2.78,198.51.100.10,53,44121,17,0,1428,1,PKTS=1
2026-10-22 09:41:20,192.0.2.79,198.51.100.10,123,44122,17,0,468,1,PKTS=1
# fields: time, src_ip, dst_ip, src_port, dst_port, proto, tos, bytes, packets, flags

    Detection logic: count inbound flows per destination port per minute and count unique sources. Many sources plus reflector ports plus high bytes-per-flow equals reflection. Full detection details are in the T1498.002 guide.

    Threat 3 — SYN flood: flow records and state tables

    Sources: Zeek conn.log, firewall session logs, load-balancer SYN proxy counters. Key fields: proto, conn_state, id.orig_h, id.resp_h, id.resp_p, half-open count. Normal: most TCP flows reach complete handshakes (conn_state SF) within seconds. Suspicious: thousands of flows stuck in S0 or REJ to one port in one minute, and the device's half-open table climbing to its maximum.

    TSV — Zeek conn.log (tab separated)
    #fields ts      uid     id.orig_h       id.orig_p  id.resp_h     id.resp_p  proto  service  duration  orig_bytes  resp_bytes  conn_state
1761122481.302    CAbcde1  203.0.113.77   51204      198.51.100.10  443       tcp    -        0.000000  0           0           S0
1761122481.310    CAbcde2  203.0.113.78   51205      198.51.100.10  443       tcp    -        0.000000  0           0           S0
1761122481.318    CAbcde3  203.0.113.79   51206      198.51.100.10  443       tcp    -        0.000000  0           0           REJ

    Detection logic: count conn_state S0 and REJ per destination per minute. Above two thousand in one minute to a business port is a flood signal, not a scan. Scans spread across many ports; floods concentrate on one.

    Threat 4 — Cloud edge saturation: load balancer and CDN metrics

    Sources: load-balancer access logs, CDN edge metrics, cloud monitoring alarms. Key fields: elb_status_code, healthy host count, processed bytes, requests per second, p99 latency. Normal: balancer-generated 5xx share below 0.5 percent; healthy host count stable; p99 latency flat. Suspicious: a wave of 502 to 504 responses, healthy host count falling, and bytes-in rising much faster than requests-in.

    JSON — cloud load-balancer alarm events
    {"ts":"2026-10-22T09:41:30Z","metric":"HealthyHostCount","lb":"prod-web-alb",
 "target_group":"tg-web-01","value":1,"previous":4,"state":"ALARM",
 "reason":"targets failed health checks"}
{"ts":"2026-10-22T09:41:30Z","metric":"HTTPCode_ELB_5XX_Count","lb":"prod-web-alb",
 "value":11842,"period_seconds":60,"note":"5xx made by the balancer, not the app"}

    Detection logic: alert when balancer-generated 5xx responses pass their baseline and the healthy host count falls in the same minute. Compare bytes-in with requests-in: floods raise bytes far faster than request counts, while real campaigns do the opposite.

    Threats 6 to 8 — Host and identity signals during the flood

    Sources: Windows security event log, Linux kernel and firewall logs, VPN or SSO gateway logs, plus synthetic monitoring. Normal: block events spread across many ports and stay near baseline; logins succeed within a second. Suspicious: block storms aimed at one port, kernel SYN-flood messages, and login timeouts that start and stop with the network event.

    JSON — Windows event 5157 (blocked connection), Windows servers
    {"EventTime":"2026-10-22 09:41:12","Channel":"Security","EventID":5157,
 "Application":"System","SourceAddress":"203.0.113.88","SourcePort":"51233",
 "DestAddress":"198.51.100.10","DestPort":"443","Protocol":"6",
 "Direction":"Inbound","Action":"Blocked"}
# tells: thousands of 5157 blocks to one port per minute during the flood window
    Text — Linux kernel and firewall log lines
    2026-10-22 09:41:10 web01 kernel: [UFW BLOCK] IN=eth0 SRC=203.0.113.51 DST=198.51.100.10 PROTO=TCP SPT=51200 DPT=443
2026-10-22 09:41:10 web01 kernel: TCP: request_sock_TCP: Possible SYN flooding on port 443. Sending cookies.  Check SNMP counters.
# tells: kernel SYN-flood message plus a block storm to one port; also watch ListenDrops in /proc/net/netstat.
# macOS note: macOS systems rarely face the raw flood. Watch them from synthetic checks instead —
# for example, a build server or file share that becomes unreachable during an internal flood.
    JSON — VPN or SSO gateway log, identity services
    {"ts":"2026-10-22T09:41:25Z","gateway":"vpn-edge-01","user":"-","src_ip":"203.0.113.204",
 "result":"timeout","reason":"portal overloaded","attempts_in_window":18477}
# tells: login attempts time out at the portal itself — staff cannot sign in while the flood runs

    Detection logic: for hosts, alert when block events to one destination port pass twenty times baseline, or when the kernel logs SYN-flood messages. For identity, alert when failed or timed-out logins on the gateway pass their five-minute baseline. Correlate both with the edge rules so the SOC sees one incident, not five alerts.

    Splunk SPL
    index=netfw sourcetype=fortigate_traffic action IN ("deny","block","drop")
| bin _time span=5m
| stats count AS denied_sessions, dc(src_ip) AS unique_sources,
        sum(eval(coalesce(src_bytes,0)+coalesce(dest_bytes,0))) AS bytes_seen by _time
| where denied_sessions > 5000
| eval est_gbit = round(bytes_seen * 8 / 1000000000, 2)
| sort - denied_sessions

    Mapping note: assumes the FortiGate App for Splunk with Common Information Model aliases. The add-on stores raw names srcip, dstip, sentbyte, rcvdbyte; either alias them to src_ip, dst_ip, src_bytes, dest_bytes, or replace the field names in the query. False positives: patch-server floods on Tuesday nights; a data-center migration. Tuning: raise the threshold to ten times your measured 30-day hourly baseline, and exclude trusted internal subnets from the source count. Severity: high.

    Rule 2 — Inbound UDP flow spike to one host

    Sigma — YAML
    title: High Volume of Inbound UDP Flows to a Single Host
id: 2f8b6d1c-4a7e-49b3-9d2f-6c8a1e3f5b70
status: stable
description: |
  Detects a large number of UDP flow records toward one destination in a
  five-minute window. Volumetric UDP floods and many reflection attacks

  service: conn

  timeframe: 5m

fields:
  - id.orig_h
  - id.resp_h
  - id.resp_p
  - proto

  - NTP pools or game servers with real high player counts

    index=netids sourcetype=zeek:conn proto=udp
| bin _time span=5m

    Mapping note: uses raw Zeek field names as kept by the Splunk TA for Zeek. id.resp_h is the destination host; id.orig_h is the sender.     Rule 3 — Edge interface saturation (NetFlow / IPFIX)

    Sigma — YAML
    title: Edge Interface Saturates With One-Way Inbound Flow Volume
id: f2a4d7c9-5b1e-4c8f-9a6d-3e7b2c4f1a90
status: stable
description: |
  Detects near-line-rate inbound volume concentrated on one destination,
  measured with flow records (NetFlow, IPFIX, or Zeek conn). When inbound
  bytes to one public host stay far above normal while replies stay small,
  the network path itself is the target — the core condition of T1498.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1498/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1498/001/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-22
logsource:
  category: network
  service: netflow
detection:
  selection:
    proto:
      - 'udp'
      - 'tcp'
      - 'icmp'
  timeframe: 1m
  condition: selection | sum(bytes) by dst_ip > 2000000000
fields:
  - dst_ip
  - src_ip
  - dst_port
  - proto
  - bytes
  - packets
falsepositives:
  - Nightly backups and bulk file transfers to one data center
  - Software distribution or OS image pulls behind one cache
level: high
tags:
  - attack.impact
  - attack.t1498
  - attack.t1498.001
  - attack.t1498.002
    Splunk SPL
    index=netflow sourcetype=netflow:records (proto=udp OR proto=tcp OR proto=icmp)
| bin _time span=1m
| stats sum(bytes) AS inbound_bytes, sum(packets) AS packets_in,
        dc(src_ip) AS unique_sources, values(dst_port) AS target_ports by dst_ip, _time
| where inbound_bytes > 2000000000
| eval est_gbit = round(inbound_bytes * 8 / 1000000000, 2)
| sort - est_gbit

    Mapping note: two billion bytes per minute is about 267 Mbit/s sustained — replace it with 60 percent of your measured 30-day same-hour peak, per destination. False positives: backup windows and release file distribution — exclude their schedules and destinations. Tuning: if your flow device does not log bytes, compute it from packets times average packet size. Severity: high.

    Rule 4 — Internal server used as a flood reflector

    Sigma — YAML
    title: Internal DNS Server Answering Large Queries for External Clients
id: 1a6c8f3d-2b9e-4c7a-a4d5-3f6b9e2c1d40
status: stable
description: |
  Detects an internal DNS server that answers large query types (ANY or
  TXT) at a high rate. This is the reflector side of amplification attacks:
  your server is being used as a weapon against someone else, which creates
  abuse reports and legal exposure even though your own service is up.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1498/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1498/002/
  - https://www.cisa.gov/uscert/ncas/alerts/TA14-017A
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-10-22
logsource:
  product: zeek
  service: dns
detection:
  selection:
    qtype_name:
      - 'ANY'
      - '*'
      - 'TXT'
  timeframe: 5m
  condition: selection | count() by id.resp_h > 500
fields:
  - id.orig_h
  - id.resp_h
  - query
  - qtype_name
  - rcode_name
falsepositives:
  - Public recursive resolvers that intentionally serve the internet
  - Bulk DNS lookups from security scanners inside your network
level: high
tags:
  - attack.impact
  - attack.t1498
  - attack.t1498.002
    Splunk SPL
    index=netids sourcetype=zeek:dns qtype_name IN ("ANY","TXT","*")
| bin _time span=5m
| stats count AS large_queries, dc(id.orig_h) AS distinct_askers,
        values(id.resp_h) AS dns_servers by id.resp_h, _time
| where large_queries > 500
| sort - large_queries

    Mapping note: Zeek represents the ANY query type as *, so the rule checks all three spellings. The count is grouped per DNS server (id.resp_h), and distinct_askers shows how many different clients are involved. False positives: a deliberate public resolver, which should be a documented exception with strict rate limits. Tuning: for authoritative-only servers, any ANY query from outside deserves a look; lower the threshold to 50. Severity: high.

    Real-world case study

    On 21 October 2016, the DNS provider Dyn was hit by one of the largest DDoS attacks seen up to that time. Because Dyn resolved names for many major websites, the outage reached far beyond one company. Times below are approximate, as reported publicly.

    Context

    Dyn operated authoritative DNS for platforms such as Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, Spotify, and GitHub. Weeks earlier, the Mirai botnet source code had been published, and Mirai had already attacked security blogger Brian Krebs and the hosting company OVH. Mirai scanned the internet for cameras, routers, and recorders that still used factory passwords, then enrolled them in a botnet of roughly one hundred thousand devices.

    Time (ET, reported)What happenedSource of record
    ~07:00First flood wave hits Dyn DNS infrastructure, mainly US East Coast; users cannot resolve major sites.Dyn public updates
    ~09:30Service partially restored; attackers adjust flood types.Dyn public updates
    ~11:52Second wave arrives with global impact; resolution fails again for many providers.Dyn public updates
    ~13:20Filtering and rate limiting reduce impact; teams work with ISPs and vendors to block botnet traffic.Dyn public updates
    ~15:11Third wave; partially mitigated by the day's new filters.Dyn public updates
    AfterwardService stable; investigation confirms the Mirai botnet and millions of malicious DNS queries.Dyn incident report

    Attack chain

    1. IoT devices with default passwords were infected with Mirai across many countries.
    2. The bot herder pointed the botnet at Dyn's DNS anycast addresses.
    3. Bots flooded DNS ports with several protocols, including malformed queries.
    4. Resolver infrastructure slowed; cache misses cascaded to dependent websites.

    Detection point

    The detection was operational telemetry: abnormal DNS query volume from millions of unique source addresses, latency growth, and failing health checks. Dyn's monitoring saw the pattern quickly; the challenge was capacity and filtering, not visibility. This is typical for T1498: you usually detect the flood itself, but the real work is absorbing it.

    Containment and eradication

    Dyn rate-limited and filtered malicious query patterns, and worked with ISPs and technology partners to block botnet traffic upstream. There was nothing to "eradicate" inside Dyn's own systems because the malicious code lived on IoT devices worldwide; the cleanup happened at network boundaries and on other people's devices over following months.

    Recovery

    Service returned in stages as filters tightened; by early afternoon the platform was stable. Because DNS answers are cached, many users recovered before the attack fully ended. Dyn published a detailed incident report, which became a reference for DNS resilience planning.

    Impact and consequences

    • Hours of degraded access to dozens of major consumer platforms across Europe and North America.
    • No data breach — the damage was availability and trust, which is the signature of T1498.
    • Industry-wide push for IoT password rules and for multi-provider DNS designs.
    • Increased sales for scrubbing and CDN vendors as boards asked "could this happen to us?".

    Team response and communication

    Dyn posted status updates during the attack and a full report afterward. The visible, regular communication kept customers from guessing. For your own playbooks, copy this pattern: short updates every 30 minutes, one named incident commander, and one public status channel.

    Lessons learned

    • DNS is a single point of failure for everything else — use at least two independent DNS providers.
    • Botnets built from default-password IoT devices remain effective; patch and change credentials everywhere.
    • Upstream coordination (ISPs, anycast, scrubbing) decides recovery speed, not on-premises tools.
    • Publish post-incident reports; they help the whole community harden against the next attack.

    Mitigation and defense

    You cannot stop the packets from arriving, but you can decide where they are dropped. The controls below are ordered by network layer, and the priority table at the end tells you what to do first. The core control is the one MITRE lists for this technique: M1037 Filter Network Traffic — intercept the incoming flood upstream, at the ISP, CDN, or a DDoS mitigation service, and forward only clean traffic.

    Home and small-office advice

    • Update router and camera firmware; change every factory password.
    • Turn off router administration from the internet (WAN) side.
    • Put smart devices on a guest Wi-Fi network, separate from laptops and phones.
    • Do not run public services (DNS, game servers) from a home connection.
    • If your business is small, use a hosted CDN or your hosting provider's DDoS protection instead of defending alone.

    Daily habits

    • Check the DDoS dashboard and edge deny trend at shift start.
    • Confirm synthetic monitoring from outside regions is green.
    • Read any ISP or abuse reports received overnight.

    Weekly habits

    • Review deny-rate and flow-volume trends against the 30-day baseline.
    • Check that new public services entered the asset inventory.
    • Scan for newly exposed UDP services on your ranges.

    Monthly habits

    • Re-tune one alert threshold from fresh data.

    Cheat sheet and quick reference

    Everything important from this guide in one place. Print this section for the SOC wall.

    Key facts

    Technique
    T1498 — Network Denial of Service (Impact)
    Sub-techniques
    T1498.001 Direct Network Flood, T1498.002 Reflection Amplification
    Goal
    Make a network service unavailable to real users
    Attacker needs
    Botnet, booter rental, or open reflectors — no victim access
    Victim evidence
    Flow spikes, deny storms, half-open tables, saturated edge interfaces
    Best detections
    Deny-count spike, UDP flow spike, edge byte saturation, reflector abuse, cloud 5xx wave
    Fastest mitigation
    Upstream scrubbing plus edge rate limits

    Top detections

    • Firewall deny spike: more than 5000 denied sessions in 5 minutes (Rule 1).
    • UDP flow spike: more than 8000 inbound UDP flows to one host in 5 minutes (Rule 2).
    • Edge saturation: inbound flow bytes to one destination far above baseline (Rule 3).
    • Reflector abuse: internal DNS answering ANY or TXT at high rate (Rule 4).
    • Cloud signal: load-balancer 5xx wave with falling healthy host count (Rule 5).
    • Identity signal: VPN or SSO login failures and timeouts in a storm (Rule 6).
    • Human signal: support tickets and synthetic checks fail while bandwidth graphs explode.

    Response checklist

    • Confirm against the same-hour baseline; rule out a marketing spike.
    • Classify: protocol, port, unique sources, estimated volume.
    • Open the DDoS runbook; name the incident commander.
    • Call the ISP or CDN; request scrubbing activation.
    • Apply pre-approved edge filters; never invent blocks live.
    • Watch other alert queues for a second, quieter attack.
    • Save flow records, firewall logs, and PCAPs before rotation.
    • Send customer updates every 30 minutes until stable.
    • Close with a blameless review and assigned follow-ups.
