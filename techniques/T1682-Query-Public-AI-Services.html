Query Public AI Services T1682

Attackers can ask public AI chatbots and assistants questions about your organization. This page shows how the technique works, what it looks like, and how to detect and defend against it. Everything is written for beginners in security operations.

Overview

This section explains the technique in plain words, why it matters to you, and what this guide will teach you.

What is T1682?

T1682, called Query Public AI Services, describes attackers who use public artificial intelligence (AI) tools as a research engine. A public AI service is any AI tool that anyone can reach, such as a large language model (LLM) chatbot on the web. An LLM is a program trained on huge amounts of text. It can answer questions, summarize text, and combine facts from many places.

Instead of reading your website page by page, an attacker can ask an AI service to summarize your organization. The AI can pull together your staff names, your office locations, the software you use, and your business partners. The attacker does this from their own laptop, with a normal account, on someone else's platform. There is no malware and no direct touch on your systems.

Why it matters

Research is the fuel of every attack that follows. A phishing email that uses the right names, the right projects, and the right tone is far more convincing. Public AI tools make that research fast and cheap. Your public information and your staff's AI habits become part of your attack surface, which is the set of all points an attacker could try to use.

Where it fits in the attack chain

MITRE ATT&CK is a shared list of attacker behaviors. This technique sits in the Reconnaissance tactic (TA0043), the first stage of the chain. The results often feed Phishing (T1566), Phishing for Information (T1598), Establish Accounts (T1585), and social engineering, which means tricking people instead of breaking software. ATT&CK also notes two further uses: gathering embarrassing or private details about people for extortion or coercion, and feeding resource development work such as Generate Content or Establish Accounts (T1585), where attackers set up fake personas and accounts. ATT&CK also points to its Artificial Intelligence coverage for how attackers obtain access to AI tools and services in the first place.

Written version of the simulation (same 8 steps)

  1. Setup. The attacker opens a public AI chatbot with a normal account. Nothing touches the victim yet. Defender view: nothing to see yet.
  2. AI research. The attacker asks the AI to summarize the victim: what it sells, where it works, who leads it. The AI service may crawl or recall public victim data. Defender view: the victim's web logs may show AI crawler user agents such as GPTBot or ClaudeBot.
  3. Target list. From the AI summary, the attacker builds a short list of people and technologies to attack. Defender view: still indirect; the recon happened outside your estate.
  4. Chatbot probing. The attacker opens the victim's own public support chatbot and asks leading questions: internal system names, staff roles, remote access steps. Defender view: the chatbot backend logs every prompt, with source IP and timestamp.
  5. WAF notices. The victim's web application firewall (WAF), a filter in front of the website, sees an odd burst of long prompts from one address. Defender view: WAF rule hits and rate-limit events appear.
  6. SOC triage. A SOC analyst, a defender who reviews alerts, reads the chatbot logs and finds repeated probing questions. Defender view: confirmed recon; block the source and tune rules.
  7. Insider leak. In parallel, one employee pastes an internal document into the public AI service to "get a quick summary". The corporate proxy and data loss prevention (DLP) see the upload. Defender view: DLP alert on sensitive data leaving to an AI domain.
  8. Response. Defenders block the probing source, coach the employee, and publish AI usage rules. The attacker keeps the gathered public data, but the leak stops. Defender view: risk reduced; monitoring continues.

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

This section explains how attackers think when they use this technique. Knowing the "why" and "how" helps you predict where to look. All examples here are safe and for lab learning only.

When and why attackers use it

AI services are free or cheap, need no hacking skill, and leave no trace on the target. An attacker uses them to save hours of manual reading. One good prompt can return an org chart, a technology list, and a set of public email patterns. This is why even low-skill actors can now do deep research.

Where it applies

It works best when the victim has a rich public footprint: a detailed website, news stories, job ads, conference talks, and public documents. It applies to any organization type. The attacker never needs a foothold, so it can start months before any visible attack.

What an attacker needs (prerequisites)

  • A normal account on a public AI service, sometimes even none.
  • Public information about the victim: names, domains, staff pages, filings.
  • Optional: a corporate AI subscription that makes research look like normal work.
  • Optional: patience to verify AI answers, which are sometimes wrong.

How it is performed (conceptual steps)

  1. Pick one or more public AI services and create an everyday account.
  2. Ask broad questions first: "Summarize this organization from public sources."
  3. Ask narrow questions next: roles, org charts, technology stacks, partners, contact formats.
  4. Ask the AI to draft personas and pretexts — a pretext is a made-up story that makes an attacker seem trustworthy — for example a help-desk script that uses real names and projects.
  5. Test the victim's own public chatbot with safe-looking questions to see what it reveals.
  6. Keep the outputs in notes and feed them into later techniques, such as phishing.
lab-only-prompt.txt — safe practice prompt for your own org (lab only)
# Lab exercise: run this against YOUR OWN organization to see your exposure.
# This is a normal chatbot prompt. It performs no attack and touches no systems.

"You are helping a security team measure public exposure.
Using only public information, summarize our organization:
1. What we sell and where we operate.
2. Public leadership names and roles.
3. Technologies we mention in job ads.
4. How our email addresses look.
Then list which items would help a social-engineering attack."
⚠
Danger — ethics and law

Running structured research against an organization you do not own, even with an AI chatbot, can break laws and policies. Only test organizations that gave you written permission, such as your own employer or a lab domain. This page teaches defense, not offense.

Common variations

  • Multi-service research: compare answers from several AI tools to fix errors.
  • Agentic browsing: let an AI agent visit public pages and compile a dossier.
  • Chatbot scraping: probe the victim's own assistant to leak internal hints.
  • Pretext drafting: generate emails that match the victim's tone and projects.

Mistakes attackers make

  • Trusting wrong AI answers and sending mail to the wrong people.
  • Probing a victim chatbot so fast that WAF rules fire.
  • Using a work AI account that ties research to a real identity.
  • Pasting their own sensitive notes into the AI service by accident.

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

Defenders cannot see the attacker's screen. But this technique leaves small traces on systems you already own. This section shows where to look and how to react.

How defenders can see it

You see this technique only at its edges. The AI queries themselves happen on someone else's platform. What you can see: AI crawlers touching your website, probes of your own chatbot, staff using AI services through your proxy, and sign-ins to AI apps in your SSO logs. Think of it as watching the shoreline, not the open sea. MITRE says the same in its official guidance (detection strategy DET0919): most of this activity is outside the target's visibility, so defenders should also watch related stages, such as the phishing that follows initial access attempts.

Why it matters to you

Recon is a leading indicator. A probing burst against your chatbot today often becomes a phishing wave next month. Seeing recon early buys time: you can brief staff, fix exposure, and tune detections before the real attack starts.

Where to look (log inventory)

  • Web/CDN logs: user agents, paths, referrers, status codes for AI crawlers.
  • Chatbot platform logs: prompt text, session ID, source IP, timestamp.
  • WAF logs: rule hits, rate limiting, blocked clients.
  • Proxy/DNS logs: which users reach which AI services, and how much data leaves.
  • SSO logs: which identities use AI applications, and when usage starts.
  • DLP alerts: sensitive patterns in uploads to AI endpoints.

Short triage flow

  1. Confirm the source: one IP or many? Internal user or outside address?
  2. Read the prompts or paths. Are they asking about internal systems, staff, or access?
  3. Check scope: is the same source touching other public endpoints?
  4. Block or rate-limit the source at the WAF or edge.
  5. If a staff member is leaking data, coach them and check what was pasted.
  6. Record the event and raise phishing awareness, since recon often precedes phishing.

Known actors and campaigns

ATT&CK lists three procedure examples for T1682. Campaign C0064 (the AI-Assisted Breach of Mexico's Government Infrastructure) used Anthropic's Claude Code and OpenAI's GPT-4.1 during operations. APT42 used LLMs to search for official email addresses to build target lists and to research potential business partners. Kimsuky used LLMs to identify think tanks, government organizations, and experts, then targeted them with spearphishing. Beyond ATT&CK, Microsoft Threat Intelligence's 2024 report Staying ahead of threat actors in the age of AI and the Google Threat Intelligence Group's AI Threat Tracker (2026) both document state-linked actors testing AI services for research tasks. Treat these as patterns, not a full list.

How to apply intel here

  1. Collect: save crawler user-agent lists, AI domains, and vendor reports.
  2. Analyze: match what you collect against your chatbot and proxy logs.
  3. Apply: tune the Sigma rules below, brief staff, and record confidence per finding.

5.4

Logs and Patterns

Each threat below maps to log sources you likely already have. For every threat you get: the sources, the fields that matter, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and the detection idea. Event examples follow.

Threat 1 — Employee data leak into public AI

Sources: proxy logs, DLP alerts, DNS logs. Fields: user, src_ip, url_domain, method, bytes_out, time.

Normal: short chats, small uploads, business hours. Suspicious: large POST uploads to AI domains, uploads outside hours, known-sensitive patterns.

Detection logic: alert when bytes_out to an AI domain passes a threshold, or when DLP matches a sensitive pattern in an upload.

Threat 2 — Identity abuse of enterprise AI

Sources: SSO/Entra sign-in logs, AI tenant audit logs, proxy logs.

Fields: user, app, ip, device, result, time.

Normal: known users on known devices. Suspicious: new AI app usage, sign-ins from new countries or anonymous proxies, sudden heavy use by a user who never used AI.

Detection logic: first-use baseline per identity; alert on new AI app or new source for a user.

Threat 3 — Victim chatbot probing

Sources: chatbot backend logs, WAF logs, web access logs.

Fields: src_ip, uri_path, method, bytes_in, status, user_agent, prompt text if stored.

Normal: short support questions, few per session. Suspicious: many long prompts per session, questions about internals, repeated retries from one IP.

Detection logic: rate-based rule on prompt count and prompt length per source IP.

Threat 4 — AI-assisted public recon

Sources: web/CDN logs, robots.txt fetch telemetry.

Fields: user_agent, uri_path, status, bytes, time.

Normal: known crawler agents follow robots.txt at set rates. Suspicious: unknown agents, ignored robots.txt, crawling of staff or policy pages.

Detection logic: user-agent allowlist plus rate checks; alert on new AI crawlers or odd paths.

Threat 5 — AI-drafted social engineering

Sources: email gateway logs, phishing report button data.

Fields: sender, subject, url domains, reply-to, report count.

Normal: low report volume. Suspicious: waves that name real staff, projects, or internal tools.

Detection logic: correlation: after recon alerts, watch for phishing that uses the same names or projects.

Threat 6 — AI crawler ingestion

Sources: web/CDN logs, robots.txt fetch telemetry.

Fields: user_agent, uri_path, status.

Normal: polite crawl rates from declared AI crawlers. Suspicious: crawling of pages you did not expect to be public, or agents you never allowlisted.

Detection logic: weekly review of new crawler agents against your robots policy.

Realistic event examples

proxy.log — employee upload to a public AI service (Threat 1)
2026-10-12 09:41:22 user=j.doe@corp.example src=10.20.4.17 method=POST
url=https://chatgpt.com/backend-api/conversation url_domain=chatgpt.com
bytes_out=184320 bytes_in=2210 category=ai-services dlp_status=alert
dlp_rule="Customer PII pattern" agent="Mozilla/5.0 (Windows NT 10.0)"

How to read it: user and src say who sent data. method=POST with bytes_out=184320 means about 180 KB left the network in one request, which is far more than a normal chat message. dlp_status=alert shows the DLP matched a sensitive pattern. This single line proves the leak path works.

nginx access log — probing of the victim's own chatbot (Threat 3)
203.0.113.44 - - [12/Oct/2026:10:02:11 +0000] "POST /api/chat HTTP/1.1" 200 5121
"-" "Mozilla/5.0 (X11; Linux x86_64)" rt=2.41 body_bytes=8944
203.0.113.44 - - [12/Oct/2026:10:02:45 +0000] "POST /api/chat HTTP/1.1" 200 5360
"-" "Mozilla/5.0 (X11; Linux x86_64)" rt=2.88 body_bytes=9102
203.0.113.44 - - [12/Oct/2026:10:03:20 +0000] "POST /api/chat HTTP/1.1" 429 231
"-" "Mozilla/5.0 (X11; Linux x86_64)" rt=0.31 body_bytes=8977

How to read it: the same src_ip sends large POST bodies (body_bytes around 9 KB, much larger than normal questions) every 30–40 seconds. The 429 status on the third line means the server started rate limiting. A few of these lines are normal support traffic; dozens from one address is probing.

Entra ID sign-in event — first use of an AI app (Threat 2)

How to read it: errorCode 0 means success. The user never used this AI app before, the sign-in came from a new country at night, and conditionalAccessStatus "notApplied" means no conditional access policy covered the app. Each field points at a control you can fix.

Rule pair 1 — Large upload to a public AI service (Threat 1)

sigma/ai_2026_01_large_upload_public_ai.yml
title: Large Upload to Public AI Service
id: b3f1c9e2-8a4d-4e2b-9c1d-5a6b7c8d9e01
status: stable
description: |
  Detects a large outbound POST to a known public AI service domain.
  Large uploads to chatbots often mean a user pasted documents or code.
  Maps to ATT&CK T1682 as the leak path that feeds AI-assisted targeting.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1682/
  - https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-14
logsource:
  category: proxy
  product: zscaler
detection:
  selection_domain:
    url_domain|endswith:
      - chatgpt.com
      - openai.com
      - claude.ai
      - gemini.google.com
      - copilot.microsoft.com
      - perplexity.ai
  selection_upload:
    method: POST
    bytes_out|gte: 100000
  condition: selection_domain and selection_upload
fields:
  - user
  - src_ip
  - url
  - bytes_out
  - dlp_status
falsepositives:
  - Staff uploading large legitimate work files that contain no secrets
  - Automated backups mistakenly routed to an AI service integration
level: high
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1682
  - attack.t1567.002
splunk — matching SPL (assumes Zscaler NSS web feed)
index=proxy sourcetype=zscalernss:web method=POST bytes_out>=100000
    url_domain IN ("chatgpt.com","openai.com","claude.ai","gemini.google.com","copilot.microsoft.com","perplexity.ai")
| stats count AS large_uploads max(bytes_out) AS biggest_upload values(url) AS urls
        max(_time) AS last_upload BY user src_ip
| sort - large_uploads

Field assumptions: the feed provides user, src_ip, url_domain, method, and bytes_out. If your proxy exports cs_bytes instead, map it to bytes_out in a field alias.

False positives and tuning: marketing teams may paste large copy drafts. Raise the threshold to 250 KB for a first rollout, or require a DLP match in the same event before paging. Keep the rule at High severity when DLP also fired.

splunk — matching SPL (Azure Monitor sign-in feed)
index=cloud sourcetype=azure:monitor:signin Status=success
    (AppDisplayName IN ("*AI*","*Assistant*","*Copilot*","*OpenAI*","*Anthropic*"))
| streamstats min(_time) AS first_seen count AS uses BY UserPrincipalName AppDisplayName
| where uses<=1
| table _time UserPrincipalName AppDisplayName IPAddress Location
| rename UserPrincipalName AS user, AppDisplayName AS app

Field assumptions: the sign-in feed normalizes Status, AppDisplayName, UserPrincipalName, IPAddress, and Location as top-level fields. Adjust if your add-on nests them under properties.*.

False positives and tuning: new-hire onboarding creates bursts. Limit to privileged groups first (help desk, executives, IT), or add a lookup of approved app-user pairs and alert only on pairs absent from the list for 7 days.

| stats count AS prompts BY _time src_ip uri_path
| where prompts > 40
| sort - prompts

Field assumptions: access_combined is the standard Apache/Nginx combined format. Your parsing app must expose uri_path, body_bytes, and src_ip. If body size is not parsed in your feed, drop the body-size filter and lower the threshold, because short messages then count too.

False positives and tuning: campus and office networks share one public IP, so count distinct source IPs behind the NAT if your vendor gives client IP via header. Exclude your own load-testing CIDR list. Tune prompts > 40 after one week of baseline data.

Rule pair 6 — Undeclared AI crawler user agent (Threat 6)

sigma/ai_2026_06_undeclared_ai_crawler.yml
title: Undeclared AI Crawler User Agent on Public Site
id: a8e6b2d5-df7c-4d6e-ab9a-0fa6b6c6d066
status: test
description: |
  Fires when a crawler-style user agent that is not on the declared
  allowlist fetches many pages. Unknown collectors may feed third-party
  AI systems that you cannot audit or request deletion from easily.
references:
  - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1682/
author: Detection Engineering Team
date: 2026-09-14
logsource:
  category: webserver
  product: nginx
detection:
  selection_agents:
    http_user_agent|contains:
      - bot
      - spider
      - crawler
      - scraper
  filter_declared:
    http_user_agent|contains:
      - Googlebot
      - bingbot
      - GPTBot
      - ClaudeBot
      - PerplexityBot
      - CCBot
  filter_assets:
    cs_uri_stem|endswith:
      - .css
      - .js
      - .png
      - .jpg
  timeframe: 1h
  condition: selection_agents and not filter_declared and not filter_assets | count(distinct cs_uri_stem) by http_user_agent > 20
fields:
  - http_user_agent
  - src_ip
  - cs_uri_stem
falsepositives:
  - Search engines not yet added to the allowlist
  - Monitoring services you forgot to declare
level: low
tags:
  - attack.reconnaissance
  - attack.t1682
splunk — matching SPL
index=web sourcetype=access_combined
    (user_agent IN ("*bot*","*spider*","*crawler*","*scraper*"))
    NOT (user_agent IN ("*Googlebot*","*bingbot*","*GPTBot*","*ClaudeBot*","*PerplexityBot*","*CCBot*"))
    NOT (uri_path IN ("*.css","*.js","*.png","*.jpg"))
| bin _time span=1h
| stats dc(uri_path) AS unique_paths count AS requests BY _time http_user_agent src_ip
| where unique_paths > 20
| sort - unique_paths

Field assumptions: standard combined access feed; the allowlist mirrors your robots.txt.

False positives and tuning: keep the allowlist in a lookup table shared with the team that owns robots.txt, or the two lists will drift. Academic crawlers are common; add them only after a policy decision, not by default. If low-volume agents matter to you, add a minimum request count on top of the distinct-path check.

Real-World Case Study

A composite case walks the full path from first AI query to incident response: timeline, detection point, containment, recovery, impact, and lessons.

✎
About this case study

This is a composite case. It combines public reporting on AI-assisted targeting with realistic details. Names and numbers are invented. The behavior pattern matches what ATT&CK and vendors have described for T1682.

Context

Organization: "Northwind Logistics", a 900-person freight company (fictional). It runs a public support chatbot, a detailed website with a leadership page, and allows staff to use public AI assistants without policy. Threat actor: a financially motivated group planning invoice fraud through executive impersonation.

Timeline

  • Day 0 — Public research. The actor asks a public AI assistant to summarize Northwind: leadership names, the ERP system mentioned in job ads, and the help-desk email format.
  • Day 2 — Chatbot probing. From one IP address, the actor sends 214 prompts to the public chatbot in 70 minutes, asking about VPN portals, invoice approval steps, and staff names.
  • Day 2 — First signal. The WAF logs a rate-limit event for the chatbot endpoint. The SOC has no rule for it, so the event is closed as noise.
  • Day 5 — Insider leak. A finance employee pastes a 60-page supplier contract into a public AI assistant for a "quick summary". The proxy records a 9 MB day of uploads to AI domains; no DLP is in place.
  • Day 9 — Pretext built. The actor drafts emails that quote the real CFO, the real ERP name, and real approval steps gathered from the research.
  • Day 12 — Phishing wave. Twelve finance staff receive a "supplier bank change" request that mirrors the real process. Two reply; one starts a payment change.
  • Day 13 — Detection. A finance manager calls the supposed supplier by phone, follows the verification rule, and the fraud fails. The SOC finally connects the chatbot probing from Day 2 with the phishing wave.
  • Day 14–21 — Response. The SOC blocks the probing ranges, enables DLP for AI domains, publishes an AI usage policy, and adds the two rules from section 5.5 that fit its stack.

Attack chain

  1. Query public AI services for organization research (T1682).
  2. Probe the victim's own chatbot for internal details.
  3. Collect extra data from an employee's AI upload (unintended insider help).
  4. Draft targeted pretexts with AI assistance.
  5. Send impersonation phishing for invoice fraud (T1566 pattern).

Detection point

The technique itself was visible twice: the chatbot probing burst on Day 2 and the proxy upload spike on Day 5. Both were missed for different reasons: no rule existed for the first, and no DLP existed for the second. Either signal, if triaged, would have exposed the campaign two weeks before the phishing wave.

Containment

Blocked the probing IP range at the edge, rate-limited the chatbot endpoint, and paused the finance payment-change workflow until re-verified with phone callbacks.

Eradication

Removed the phishing mails from all mailboxes, revoked the two affected sessions, and added the sender patterns to the mail gateway. No malware was involved, so there was nothing to re-image or rebuild.

Recovery

Reopened the payment workflow with dual approval, deployed DLP rules for AI domains, and retrained finance staff with the real examples from the attempt.

Impact and consequences

No money was lost. Real costs appeared anyway: about 60 staff-hours of response work, a delayed supplier payment, and a board question about AI governance. The largest cost was trust: leadership learned that public surfaces and staff habits were part of the attack path.

Team response and communication

The SOC lead sent a plain-language summary to all staff on Day 15: what happened, what data was involved, and what changes to expect. Finance and IT held a joint review. The chatbot vendor received a request for better prompt logging, which arrived in the next release.

Lessons learned

  • Rate-limit events on public endpoints deserve a rule, not a shrug; recon looks like noise first.
  • Chatbot logs must capture prompts, source IP, and session IDs, or probing stays invisible.
  • DLP for AI domains is now as basic as email attachment controls.
  • An AI usage policy must exist before staff habits create the leak.
  • Recon and phishing belong to one story; correlate them in reviews.
  • Out-of-band verification (a phone call) stopped the fraud; keep funding it.

Mitigation and Defense

Controls you can deploy, ordered by effort and impact, for enterprise teams and for home and small offices.

MITRE lists one mitigation for this technique: M1056 Pre-compromise — you cannot stop recon from happening, but you can shrink what it finds and how it helps. The controls below turn that idea into actions.

Policy and governance

  • Publish an AI usage policy: approved tools, allowed data, forbidden data.
  • Add AI scenarios to the risk register and to supplier reviews.
  • Name an owner for public-surface exposure.

Identity and access

  • Conditional access for AI apps; block anonymous-proxy sign-ins.
  • Least privilege on chatbot admin panels.
  • Quarterly review of who can use enterprise AI tenants.

Endpoint

  • Browser extensions that warn on AI paste events.
  • Keep endpoint logging on, so copy-paste flows are visible.
  • Standard builds prevent personal AI desktop agents.

Network and edge

  • Rate-limit chatbot and login endpoints per source IP.
  • Categorize AI domains in the proxy; log them fully.
  • WAF rules for prompt-size and prompt-rate anomalies.

Data protection (DLP)

  • DLP patterns for PII, source code, and contracts on uploads.
  • Watermark sensitive exports so leaks are traceable.
  • Block or warn on large uploads to AI domains.

Logging, monitoring, response

  • Centralize proxy, SSO, web, WAF, and chatbot logs.
  • Deploy the section 5.5 rules that match your stack.
  • Add "public-surface probing" to the incident playbook.

Home and small office

  • Never paste client data, contracts, or code into public chatbots; summarize by hand or use an approved business tool.
  • Turn on automatic updates and use the router's built-in filtering if available.
  • For a family business website: keep the staff page short, remove email patterns like first.last@ from public pages, and use a contact form instead.
  • If your site uses a chatbot plugin, update it monthly and read its privacy settings.
