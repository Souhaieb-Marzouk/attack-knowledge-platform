MITRE ATT&CK · RECONNAISSANCE TECHNIQUE

T1596 Search Open Technical Databases

Before an attack, intruders read. This technique covers how they use free public databases — Domain Name System (DNS) records, WHOIS records, certificates, content delivery network (CDN) data, and scan databases — to map an organization from the outside. This guide explains how it works and how defenders can shrink what those databases reveal.

  • Tactic: Reconnaissance
  • Platform: PRE (Pre-attack)
  • Sub-techniques: 5
  • Created: October 2020
  • ATT&CK page ↗

What it is

Recon by reading. Attackers search free public technical databases to learn about a target before any direct contact.

What attackers learn

Domains, subdomains, IP addresses, hosting history, certificates, staff contacts, and exposed services.

Why detection is hard

The queries hit third-party databases, not your systems. Most of the research leaves no trace in your logs.

Defensive start

Manage your external footprint, and watch the few visible traces: DNS noise, crawler agents, public certificates.

1

Overview

T1596 describes research, not intrusion. The intruder sits somewhere else, opens free public services, and reads facts about your organization. Nothing touches your network during this step. This section explains the idea in plain words and shows why it still matters to defenders.

What it is

Search Open Technical Databases means an attacker gathers technical facts about a target from free public services: DNS records, WHOIS registration data, digital certificates, CDN data, and internet scan databases. Think of it as reading your organization's public paper trail. It is patient work, and it is quiet.

Why it matters

These databases answer questions that once needed risky, noisy scanning: Which subdomains exist? Who runs the mail server? Which services face the internet? What software versions do they show? The answers become the attacker's map. A thin map makes every later attack step harder.

Where it fits in the attack chain

ATT&CK places this technique in the Reconnaissance tactic, marked PRE — it happens before the victim is involved at all. Findings feed later choices: who gets the phishing email, which exposed appliance gets probed, which subdomain gets abused.

What you will learn

This guide covers five things. It explains how each of the five sub-techniques works. It lists the rare traces that reach your logs. It ranks the real threats and gives five complete detection rules in Sigma and Splunk Search Processing Language (SPL). Finally, it offers a mitigation plan that starts with shrinking what the databases can see.

The good news

You control most of what these databases know. Every stale DNS record you delete, every certificate SAN you clean, and every unpatched banner you hide makes the attacker's map thinner. This is one of the few techniques where cleanup is detection.

FamilyThe five sub-techniques

T1596.001 DNS/Passive DNS

Passive DNS services store DNS answers seen across the internet and answer questions from that stored history. Attackers get subdomain lists and old IP addresses without ever querying the victim's own name servers. Old records often outlive the hosts they pointed to.

ATT&CK page ↗

T1596.002 WHOIS

WHOIS and RDAP publish domain registration data: registrar, dates, name servers, and contact details unless redacted. Pivoting on a registrant email or name server links supposedly separate domains into one family, including vendor and acquisition domains.

ATT&CK page ↗

T1596.003 Digital Certificates

Certificate transparency (CT) logs are public lists of every Transport Layer Security (TLS) certificate issued for public domains. They expose hostnames — including forgotten test boxes and internal-looking names — through the certificate's SAN (Subject Alternative Name) field.

ATT&CK page ↗

T1596.004 CDNs

Content delivery network (CDN) data shows which edge network fronts a website. Combined with DNS history, it can hint at the origin server hiding behind the CDN — the direct address that skips the CDN's protections.

ATT&CK page ↗

T1596.005 Scan Databases

Internet scan databases such as Shodan or Censys index exposed services: open ports, software banners, devices, and login pages. One search by company name or IP range hands over an exposure report the victim may not have made yet.

ATT&CK page ↗

How they combine

Each source answers a different question: names (DNS), ownership (WHOIS), hostnames (certificates), infrastructure (CDN), and exposures (scan databases). Together they become a target dossier — you will watch this happen in Section 2.

Text versionThe story, step by step

  1. Step 1 — Choose the target. The attacker selects Northwind Freight Ltd. and sets a goal: find a quiet way in without touching the company's systems. They open an empty dossier and plan to fill it using only free public sources.
  2. Step 2 — Passive DNS search. The attacker asks a passive DNS service for every name it has seen under northwind-freight.example. The service answers from its own stored records: live subdomains and old IP addresses, including hosts that moved long ago. Defender view: none — passive queries never touch the victim's servers.
  3. Step 3 — WHOIS lookups. WHOIS queries return registration records. The main domain and two partner domains share a registrant email, so the attacker links them into one family — and collects two staff addresses that were not redacted. Defender view: the query is invisible; privacy redaction shrinks what the record teaches.
  4. Step 4 — Certificate transparency search. On a CT search site the attacker finds a certificate issued eight months ago for staging.northwind-freight.example — a forgotten test host that appears in no document and no asset list. Defender view: monitor certificate transparency logs for your domains.
  5. Step 5 — CDN data review. CDN and hosting data show the website sits behind a large content delivery network. One DNS record and one response header hint at the origin server's real address behind that shield. Defender view: restrict origin servers to accept CDN traffic only.
  6. Step 6 — Scan database search. In internet scan databases the attacker finds three indexed exposures. They include an open RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol) port on a branch office, an old VPN appliance banner from 2023, and a camera login page with default-credential hints. Defender view: track your external footprint weekly with an external attack surface management (EASM) tool.
  7. Step 7 — Assemble the dossier. The attacker merges everything into one page: subdomains and old IPs, staff emails, the forgotten staging host, an origin hint, and three weak services. The next phase now has a shopping list — and the victim logged none of it. Defender view: still quiet — your edge logs show zero packets from the attacker.
  8. Step 8 — SOC retrospective. The SOC reviews the month. The database queries left no trace at all. What did leave traces: a small spike of NXDOMAIN answers in DNS logs and a few scanner user agents on the website — exactly the signals worth alerting on. Defender view: alert on NXDOMAIN spikes and known crawler user agents; keep DNS and edge logs for at least 90 days.
3

Red Team Perspective

This section explains how security professionals — red teams and penetration testers working under written authorization — use open technical databases. The goal is defensive understanding: if you know how the picture is assembled, you know what to remove from it.

Why attackers love this technique

  • Free. Every source has a no-cost tier or a plain web page.
  • Quiet. No packets reach the victim, so no alarm can fire.
  • Safe. Reading public data breaks no laws in most places and risks no attribution.
  • Early. It happens in the PRE phase, long before any endpoint or SIEM (security information and event management) is involved.

Where it applies

  • Target selection and scoping — is this organization worth the effort?
  • Phishing preparation — real names, emails, and project names for believable pretexts.
  • Perimeter review — which services are already exposed before a single scan runs.
  • Infrastructure choices — learn the victim's mail setup before spoofing or hosting lookalikes.

Prerequisites

  • Written authorization that names the target and the rules of engagement.
  • A lab or approved workspace, kept separate from production systems.
  • Basic tooling: a WHOIS client, dig, curl, and a browser.
  • Agreed handling rules for personal data found in public records.

Typical workflow

  1. Step 1 · Define the scope

    Pick the organization and the goal. Write down what the dossier must answer before the next phase can start.

  2. Step 2 · Enumerate the domain family

    WHOIS pivots, certificate transparency searches, and passive DNS build the list of names that belong to the target.

  3. Step 3 · Enrich with history

    DNS history services show old IP addresses, hosting moves, and forgotten infrastructure that still resolves.

  4. Step 4 · Hunt exposures in scan data

    Scan databases are searched by company name, IP range, and domain. Old banners and default pages stand out.

  5. Step 5 · Pivot and expand

    Registrant emails, name servers, and certificate issuers link partner, vendor, and acquisition domains to the same family.

  6. Step 6 · Hand off

    The dossier moves to the next phase: phishing targets, a shortlist of exposed services, or an active-scanning plan (T1595).

Common variations

UI vs API

Web search pages for one-off lookups; scripted API calls for bulk research. The information is the same — only the speed differs.

Broad vs targeted

Some researchers pull every name for a whole company; others chase one lead, such as the mail setup, before a spoofing attempt.

Pivot chains

A registrant email links five domains; a certificate SAN adds a sixth; a name server adds a seventh. Chains grow without touching the victim.

Mistakes attackers make — and defenders should watch for
  • Loud enumeration. Brute-forcing subdomain names through the victim's own resolver produces NXDOMAIN storms — the single most visible recon trace (see rule R2).
  • Identifiable user agents. Scanner and crawler software often announces itself, and those strings land in web logs (rule R3).
  • Trusting stale data. Public databases lag reality. Old records point to hosts that no longer exist, which wastes attacker time and can expose their interest.
  • Accidental direct contact. Resolving names through the victim's authoritative servers is a small but real touch — logged, and unlike passive lookups, attributable.

Blue Team Perspective

Here is the uncomfortable truth: most of this technique is invisible to you. The queries go to third-party services, and their logs belong to someone else. But not everything is invisible — sloppy recon leaves traces — and everything the attacker learns shows up later as an attack you can prepare for.

Artifacts this technique can leave

In DNS logs

Bursts of queries for names that do not exist (NXDOMAIN answers), unusual record type requests such as AXFR (zone transfers), and query spikes from single external sources.

In web and edge logs

Requests announcing research tools: user agents like Shodan, CensysInspect, zgrab, or masscan, plus repetitive probing of login and admin paths.

In public data you can watch

New certificates for your domains in CT logs, changed name servers or contacts in WHOIS, and your own assets appearing in scan database results — all visible proactively.

What never appears

Passive DNS lookups, WHOIS queries, CT searches, and scan database reviews performed against third-party services. Expect silence from these and do not build detections that promise otherwise.

Where to look

Authoritative DNS logs
Query logs on the name servers that answer for your domains — the only DNS place where outsider interest is recorded
Web and edge logs
CDN, load balancer, and origin web server logs with full user agent strings, kept at least 90 days hot
CT monitor feeds
A certificate transparency monitoring service subscribed to your domain names; alerts on every new issuance
EASM reports
External attack surface management scans or manual reviews that show what scan databases see about you this week
Threat intel mentions
Reports or feeds noting your industry is being profiled — context that raises the priority of recon signals

Where this fits in the attack chain

T1596 is an early Reconnaissance step. Its findings feed later attack steps. T1595 Active Scanning probes what the databases revealed. T1598 Phishing for Information targets the people it identified. Later tactics use the exposed services it indexed. When you triage a recon signal, assume the next step is already planned.

Triage flow for a recon signal

When one of the visible traces fires, walk this path. It keeps the response calm and stops alert fatigue from setting in.

  1. Validate the source IP

    Is it your own vulnerability scanner, a known research crawler, or an unknown address? Check asset and scanner inventories first.

  2. Check the query type

    NXDOMAIN guessing, AXFR attempts, and banner probing mean different things. Record what was actually asked.

  3. Correlate with your exposure data

    Does the source's interest match something your EASM report flagged this week? If yes, treat the exposure as the incident.

  4. Classify the activity

    Misconfigured crawler, academic researcher, bug bounty scope, or deliberate recon? Write the judgment down.

  5. Enrich with threat intelligence

    Is the source known for scanning many organizations? Recent reporting about your sector raises the stakes.

  6. Decide and record the response

    Fix the exposure, block the source, file the report, or close as benign — and log the decision so the next analyst inherits the context.

Why this matters even when you see nothing

Absence of evidence is the normal state here. The value of monitoring recon signals is not catching every researcher. It is catching the careless ones. More importantly, it shows which of your exposures is interesting enough to attract attention. Every recon signal points at a thing worth fixing.

A simple confidence framework

High confidence

Multiple independent sources agree, or the fact is directly observable — a certificate exists, your DNS log shows the query. Act on it.

Medium confidence

One trusted source, no second confirmation yet. Corroborate before acting broadly; use it to raise priority, not to declare an incident.

Low confidence

Unverified, single anonymous, or inconsistent. Keep it in a watchlist file and revisit when more data arrives.

Known usage and public campaigns

Patterns seen in public reporting

Open database recon is not theoretical. These patterns appear again and again in public incident reporting. Each item carries a confidence label. A CVE is a Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures identifier — the public ID given to a published software flaw.

  • Mass scanning within days of disclosure. Scan databases made newly disclosed flaws searchable almost at once. Examples: Citrix ADC CVE-2019-19781 (January 2020) and Pulse Secure CVE-2019-11510 (2019–2020). Fortinet SSL-VPN CVE-2018-13379 (2021) and Log4Shell CVE-2021-44228 (December 2021) followed the same pattern. High confidence
  • Default-credential IoT abuse. The Mirai botnet (2016) showed mass attacks on devices that still used factory passwords. High confidence
  • Subdomain takeover. Researchers and bug-bounty programs have documented this class since about 2016. High confidence
  • Phishing with free TLS certificates. Anti-abuse reporting from certificate authorities shows that a large share of phishing sites use HTTPS. High confidence for the trend Medium for exact shares
  • Expired-domain hijacking. Public incident reports repeatedly describe attackers taking over expired domains and intercepting mail. Medium-High confidence

This guide does not name attack groups. Group and campaign mappings change over time. For the current list, check the mappings on the official T1596 page and cite what it shows.

Applying intelligence to this technique

  • Baseline monthly: run the attacker's searches against your own organization and save the results — this is your reference picture.
  • Diff, don't stare: value comes from changes — a new subdomain, a new certificate, a newly indexed service — not from the static list.
  • Feed the detections: when reporting names tools or data sources being abused (for example a passive DNS platform), raise the priority of the matching signal rules in 5.5.
  • Close the loop: every recon signal that points at a real exposure becomes a finding with an owner and a fix date.

Sample events and what makes them suspicious

Zeek DNS log — zone transfer attempt (threat 2)

  • id.orig_h — the asking IP (203.0.113.0/24 is documentation space, used here as a stand-in for an external address). id.resp_h — your DNS server.
  • qtype_name: "AXFR" — a request for a full zone transfer. Normal: essentially never from outside; planned replication uses known secondary servers. Suspicious: any AXFR/IXFR from a source outside your name server list.
  • rcode_name — the answer. NOTAUTH or REFUSED means the server said no; the attempt still maps the attacker's interest. NOERROR with populated answers means the zone walked out the door — escalate immediately.
  • Detection logic: alert when qtype_name is AXFR/IXFR and the source is not an internal or approved replication address.

Zeek DNS log — NXDOMAIN enumeration (threat 1 recon noise)

  • Normal: occasional NXDOMAIN answers from real users — typos, expired links, mail servers checking odd sender domains.
  • Suspicious: one external source generating hundreds of unique NXDOMAIN names in minutes, with name shapes that follow patterns (vpn-…, staging-…, mail2-…). That is a wordlist walking your namespace.
  • Detection logic: count NXDOMAIN answers per external source over a five-minute window; alert when both volume and unique-name count are high (rule R2).

Web access log — scanner user agents (threats 3 and 6)

  • Fields in combined format: client IP, identity, user, time, request line, status, bytes, referrer, user agent (the final quoted string).
  • Normal: research crawls are part of internet life. Expect a steady background of known research agents.
  • Suspicious: a new agent species, hits on sensitive paths (/vpn/login.php), or volume spikes. The value is correlation: a Shodan hit in May tells you your May exposure was indexed.
  • Detection logic: match known scanner agents, keep severity informational, and review weekly against your EASM diff (rule R3).

Sysmon process creation — recon CLI tools (threats 8 and 7)

  • Image — the program that ran. CommandLine — what it was asked to do. User and Computer — who and where.
  • Normal: security staff and some IT automation run these tools regularly. That is why the rule allowlists security-team accounts.
  • Suspicious: the same tools on a sales laptop, a server, or an account without a security role — inside recon often follows a foothold, and it looks exactly like this.
  • Detection logic: alert on process creation where the image name ends in a recon tool binary and the user is not on the approved list (rule R4).

CT monitor feed — new certificate (threats 5 and 8)

  • subject and san — the names the certificate covers. issuer — the certificate authority. not_before — validity start.
  • Normal: renewals of known names, planned launches, certificates created by SaaS providers hosting something for your domain.
  • Suspicious: a name nobody recognizes, odd naming conventions, or issuers you have never used. Remember: attackers can also get certificates for your domains to host lookalikes — the monitor sees both directions.
  • Detection logic: surface every issuance for monitored domains; keep severity low and let an allowlist of known names do the tuning (rule R5).

5.5Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

Sigma is an open rule format for describing detections in plain YAML; any SIEM that supports Sigma can translate the same rule. Splunk SPL is the query language used below as the concrete example. Each rule comes with a matching SPL query, a mapping note, known false positives, and tuning guidance. Severity is stated in the rule and repeated in the summary table in Section 9.

SPL conventions used here

index=network and index=web are example index names — replace them with the names your environment uses. sourcetype=zeek:dns:json is the sourcetype the Corelight/Splunk add-on produces. If you index raw Zeek JSON instead, the fields keep their original names, such as id.orig_h. You may then need | rename id.orig_h AS src_ip first. index=main sourcetype="XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" is the common path for Sysmon XML data. Every environment-specific value is repeated in the mapping note under each rule.

R1 — DNS Zone Transfer Attempt from External Source High

Any AXFR or IXFR request from outside your networks is worth a look. Successful transfers are rare misconfigurations with a big payout for the attacker.

R1 mapping note, false positives, tuning

Mapping and fields: the Corelight/Splunk add-on renames Zeek's id.orig_h to src_ip and id.resp_h to dest_ip. On raw JSON, insert | rename id.orig_h AS src_ip, id.resp_h AS dest_ip before the filter. False positives: approved replication from secondaries you forgot to allowlist; a lab subnet that should be internal. Tuning: replace the RFC 1918 ranges with your real DNS replication partners. Also consider a second, near-duplicate rule for your external-facing name server logs. Such attempts are visible there even without a network sensor.

R2 — High NXDOMAIN Enumeration from Single External Source Medium

Subdomain wordlists leave a loud signature in the one place they cannot avoid: the DNS server that has to answer. This rule catches the volume, not the content.

R2 mapping note, false positives, tuning

Mapping and fields: same rename assumptions as R1. The Sigma aggregation (| count() by id.orig_h > 200 over a five-minute timeframe) becomes the bin plus stats pair in SPL. Splunk has no direct Sigma-timeframe keyword, so the window is explicit. The extra unique_names check keeps a source that repeats one failing lookup from firing. False positives: busy mail gateways, security scanners, and research crawlers. Tuning: raise the threshold until the rule is quiet on a normal week. Then lower it slowly. Add allowlists for known mail servers and scanners. Expect a useful value around 200–500 per five minutes for a mid-size organization.

R3 — Known Recon Scanner User Agents on Public Web Applications Low

Research crawlers announce themselves. On its own a hit is usually harmless; the real value is correlating it with what your exposure management already found.

R3 mapping note, false positives, tuning

Mapping and fields: sourcetype=access_combined ships default extractions for clientip, useragent, uri_path, and status. If your platform uses http_user_agent or cs(User-Agent), point the regex at that field. False positives: your own scans, uptime monitors, and bug bounty traffic. Tuning: keep this rule informational. Send it to a weekly review, not to paging. Extend the agent list as new research tools appear. Add a companion rule that alerts only when a scanner hits an unknown host. That combination often marks a forgotten exposure.

R4 — Reconnaissance CLI Tools Execution Medium

Inside the network, the same research tools say something different. On a security workstation they are work; on a sales laptop they may be an intruder mapping your environment after a foothold.

R4 mapping note, false positives, tuning

Mapping and fields: Sysmon Event ID 1 is process creation; Image, CommandLine, User, and Computer are standard fields once the Sysmon add-on parses the XML. The four backslashes in the SPL regex produce one literal path separator — Splunk strips escaping twice (once in the string, once in regex). False positives: security and IT staff; keep an explicit allowlist of accounts and hosts. Tuning: extend the binary list with the tools your own teams use. Leave out curl — it is too noisy. Consider matching CommandLine keywords such as -wordlist for higher fidelity. Review the allowlist quarterly.

R5 — New TLS Certificate Issued for Monitored Domain Low

Certificate transparency gives defenders a free tripwire: every certificate for your domains is public within hours. The feed is only useful if someone reads it.

R5 mapping note, false positives, tuning

Mapping and fields: this rule assumes your CT monitor writes JSON events into Splunk. The events carry type=certificate_issued. Many monitor services can forward to a SIEM. The exact sourcetype is environment-specific. Here ct:monitor:json is a label you control. The lookup owned_domains is a CSV you maintain with a name column listing every approved subdomain; the rule then shows only unknown names. False positives: renewals, launch campaigns, SaaS-issued certificates. Tuning: keep the allowlist fresh and alert on a digest schedule. Pay attention to the issuer. A certificate authority your organization never uses is a stronger signal than the name alone. Validation: issue a test certificate for a sandbox subdomain and confirm the alert path end to end.

Real-World Case Study

The story below is a fictional composite written for training — it merges patterns that appear again and again in real incident reporting into one teaching narrative. Company names, people, dates, and numbers are invented.

Case file — Meridian Freight Services, May 2025

Meridian Freight Services is a fictional mid-size logistics company: three data centers, a remote workforce, an old VPN appliance nobody loved, and a two-person SOC that mostly fights phishing. In May 2025 it became the target of a ransomware attempt that started — as many do — with nothing but free public databases.

The attack chain in one view

  1. Read (T1596)

    Free databases produced: a forgotten subdomain, an old VPN appliance, organizational context for phishing.

  2. Phish (T1598-style lead-in)

    WHOIS-derived context made the emails believable enough to capture one credential.

  3. Log in

    The stolen credential worked on the very appliance the recon had flagged — no MFA, old firmware.

  4. Stage

    The staging host — found through a certificate SAN — offered a quiet place to prepare. Ransomware was not yet deployed.

  5. Caught at D+1

    Correlation of external recon signals with a real exposure stopped the chain before encryption.

The detection point that mattered

No single alert caught this. The save came from correlation. An EASM diff said “this appliance is exposed”. An NXDOMAIN alert said “someone is interested in your namespace”. An unforwarded appliance log would have said “someone logged in”. The lesson is not a new tool. It is a habit of putting outside-view findings next to inside signals, quickly and by a named person.

Containment

  • Appliance account disabled; device pulled from the internet.
  • Staging host isolated at the switch; nothing wiped yet.
  • Scanning source IP blocked at the edge.
  • Phishing victim's sessions revoked and password rotated.

Eradication

  • Appliance patched, then replaced; MFA extended to all remote access.
  • Staging host rebuilt; persistence checks clean.
  • All credentials used or visible near the incident rotated.
  • Dangling DNS records removed; CT monitor subscribed.

Recovery

  • Remote access restored through the new gateway in stages.
  • Enhanced monitoring on identity and VPN logs for 30 days.
  • Backup restore test run to prove ransomware readiness.
  • Staff briefed on the phishing lure they can expect to see again.

Impact and consequences

Operational disruption
About two days — remote staff lost VPN access while the appliance was offline and replaced
Data stolen
None — the chain was stopped before data collection or encryption
Direct cost
Roughly USD 40,000 (fictional): overtime, emergency patching, appliance replacement
Regulatory impact
No personal data breach, so no notification duty — but the near-miss entered the risk register

Team response and communication

The SOC analyst who correlated the signals became incident manager for the response. Updates followed a fixed rhythm: first message to the CISO within one hour (facts only), then every four hours during containment, then a written 24-hour summary with open risks. Staff received one clear message about the phishing lure. Vendors — the appliance maker and the registrar — were contacted in parallel for patch guidance and record cleanup. The comms rule that worked: say what is known, what is unknown, and when the next update comes.

Lessons learned

  • An EASM program with a weekly cadence finds exposures before attackers do — the D+1 report was the hinge of the whole case.
  • DNS hygiene is security work: dangling records and forgotten subdomains gave the attacker a map and a door.
  • MFA must cover every remote access path, including “temporary” appliances.
  • CT monitoring would have flagged the staging subdomain years earlier — a free control left unused.
  • A patch SLA for internet-facing systems (30 days maximum) belongs in policy, not in good intentions.
  • Recon-signal alerts only work when someone owns the triage flow — role, not tool.

Mitigation and Defense

You cannot stop someone from reading public data. You can shrink what is public, and you can watch the thin trail that remains. The controls below are grouped by domain; the table at the end prioritizes them by effort and payoff.

Policy

  • Name an owner for every public-facing asset.
  • Add an external exposure review to the risk register cadence.
  • Run a vulnerability disclosure intake so strangers can report findings safely.

Identity

  • Require MFA on every remote access path, without exceptions.
  • Separate admin accounts from daily accounts.
  • Protect the registrar account itself with strong MFA — it controls your namespace.

Endpoint

  • Deploy EDR (endpoint detection and response) on all hosts, including servers and jump boxes.
  • Restrict recon CLI tools to security roles by policy, not only by detection.
  • Keep test and staging hosts in the same patch loop as production.

Network

  • Disable zone transfers to untrusted parties (restrict AXFR/IXFR).
  • Apply egress rules so a compromised host cannot quietly probe others.
  • Rate-limit DNS responses to blunt enumeration noise.

Cloud

  • Allowlist CDN ranges at the origin; drop direct-to-origin traffic.
  • Remove dangling DNS records and de-provisioned cloud resources together.
  • Review CDN security features: bot rules, edge WAF, TLS policy.

Logging

  • Enable query logging on authoritative name servers.
  • Send DNS, edge, and origin logs to the SIEM; keep 90 days hot, one year cold.
  • Forward appliance logs (VPN, firewall) — an unread log is a missing log.

Monitoring

  • Deploy the five rules from Section 5.5 at appropriate severities.
  • Subscribe a CT monitor to all owned domains.
  • Review an EASM diff weekly — the single highest-value habit in this guide.

Backup

  • Keep tested restores for public sites and identity stores.
  • Hold one offline copy outside the domain admin path.
  • Time the restore drill; ransomware readiness is measured in hours.

Response

  • Write a recon-signal playbook: who triages, when to escalate, what to fix first.
  • Link every recon signal to the exposure it points at.
  • Rehearse the Meridian-style correlation once a quarter in a tabletop.
Home and small office
  • Turn your router's remote management off — scan databases index open admin pages within days.
  • Install firmware updates for the router and any camera or NAS; their banners are public.
  • For personal domains: enable registrar lock and WHOIS privacy, and use a unique password with MFA on the registrar account.
  • Run your own weekly check: search your home IP in a scan database and see what strangers see.

Daily

Weekly

  • Review NXDOMAIN and scanner-agent trends, not individual hits.

Response checklist

  • Confirm the signal source is not your own scanner or an approved crawler.
  • If an AXFR attempt: test a zone transfer yourself from outside; fix the server if it succeeds.
  • If an NXDOMAIN burst: record source, timespan, and name patterns; check whether the volume matches a known scanner.
  • Diff this week's EASM output against last week's before deciding anything.
  • Verify every newly indexed service has an owner and a patch date.
  • Empty the CT monitor queue; question every unknown name.
  • Patch or retire anything on the internet that still shows an old banner.
  • Rotate credentials if a recon pattern maps to your phishing-exposed staff.
  • Record the case in the recon playbook log with the decision made.
  • Brief the next shift and set a follow-up date for the exposure that was found.
