MITRE ATT&CK · RECONNAISSANCE

Phishing for Information T1598

Phishing for information is a family of tricks in which an attacker sends messages that make an employee hand over secrets. No malware is needed; the goal is the answer itself. This parent guide introduces all four sub-techniques and shows how a beginner-friendly SOC (security operations center) can detect each one. Throughout, it contrasts T1598 with T1566 (Phishing), the Initial Access version of the same tricks.

Tactic · Reconnaissance Platforms · PRE (pre-attack; channels are email, chat, web, and voice) Data sources · Application logs (email, SaaS), Network traffic, Telephony logs Sub-techniques · 4

What it is

Email, chat, form, and phone tricks that make people share facts, credentials, or files the attacker wants.

Where it fits

Reconnaissance — before Initial Access

Primary target

Employees with useful knowledge or access: HR, finance, IT, engineers, executives.

Main risk

Credentials, personal data, org charts, and process details exposed before any compromise.

Contents

    1

    Overview

    T1598 covers every way an attacker uses phishing-style messages to collect information instead of breaking in. This section explains the idea, contrasts it with the Initial Access technique T1566, and defines the words used in the rest of the guide.

    What this technique is

    The adversary sends a message that looks normal and asks for something specific: a password, an org chart, a tool name, a phone number, or a filled-in form. The employee answers, and the attacker records the answer as intelligence.

    MITRE ATT&CK splits this behavior into four sub-techniques by channel. T1598.001 Spearphishing Service uses chat or direct messages on a third-party platform. T1598.002 Spearphishing Attachment uses a clean document that asks questions. T1598.003 Spearphishing Link uses a link to a fake form or login page. T1598.004 Spearphishing Voice uses a phone call.

    Why it matters

    Everything the attacker learns here makes the next stage stronger and cheaper. A team name becomes a believable sender; a tool name becomes a good lure; a stolen password becomes a login attempt against your identity provider (IdP, the service that checks users in).

    Because the exchange happens on ordinary channels, there is often no exploit, no payload, and no endpoint alert. Detection therefore depends on mail, SaaS, network, and telephony telemetry, plus one very powerful sensor: the employees themselves.

    Where it fits in the attack chain

    Reconnaissance→Initial Access→Execution→Persistence→Credential Access

    T1598 lives in Reconnaissance (tactic TA0043), the phase where the attacker studies your organization from the outside. The answers it collects feed Initial Access moves such as T1566 Phishing or a direct login with an elicited password.

    T1598 vs T1566

    The same message can serve two different goals. What separates the techniques is what the attacker wants out of the exchange, and therefore what you should log for.

    AspectT1598 — Phishing for Information (Reconnaissance)T1566 — Phishing (Initial Access)
    GoalGather facts: credentials, names, structures, processes, files.Get inside: run code, deliver a payload, create a foothold.
    Typical ask“Which VPN do you use?”, “Please fill this form”, “What is your team called?”“Open this invoice”, “Enable macros to view this document”.
    Success looks likeA reply, a filled form, a shared file, an MFA code read out over the phone. No malware event.A download, a process start, an implant beacon, an account used.
    Primary logsMail gateway and SaaS audit logs, DLP (data loss prevention), DNS and proxy, telephony records.Endpoint telemetry, process and file events, mail detonation results.

    Terms in this guide

    Spearphishing

    A phishing message written for a specific person or small group, using details that make it feel personal. It is more targeted than mass spam.

    Elicitation

    The art of getting information out of someone through normal-looking questions, so the person does not notice they are disclosing anything.

    Pretext

    The fake story that explains the contact: a recruiter, IT support, a vendor, or an HR survey. The pretext answers “why is this person asking me?”.

    IdP

    Identity provider: the service that checks usernames, passwords, and multi-factor authentication, such as Entra ID or Okta. Elicited credentials usually end up here.

    MFA

    Multi-factor authentication: a second proof of identity, such as an app push or a one-time code. Attackers elicit codes by voice and SMS (text message), not just passwords.

    DLP

    Data loss prevention: tools and rules that spot sensitive data leaving the organization, for example an org chart sent to a personal mailbox.

    3
    Red Team Perspective
    
      
    This section describes how attackers plan and run phishing-for-information campaigns, at a planning level only. The goal is defensive understanding: if you know what the attacker needs, you know what to log and where to intervene.
    

      
    
        
    
          
    When and why attackers use it
    
          
    Elicitation is cheap, quiet, and needs no exploit. It works before any packet touches your network, so many technical defenses never see it.
    
          
    Attackers choose T1598 when they need facts that make later attacks believable: team names, tool names, schedules, or a working password. It also serves as a low-risk test of who replies, how fast staff answer, and which pretexts your culture tolerates.
    
          
    The answers feed everything that follows: T1566 Phishing for access, T1656 Impersonation of help desk or executives, and direct logins with stolen credentials.
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Preconditions
    
          
    The attacker needs three things before the first message: a target worth asking, a channel to reach them, and a story that explains the ask.
    
          
      
            
    • Public information about your organization: staff lists, org charts, job posts, press releases.
      • 
            
    • A working contact path: corporate email, a professional social platform, a web form, or a phone number.
      • 
            
    • A believable persona: name, photo, history, and a reason to know you.
      • 
            
    • Optionally, staging infrastructure: a lookalike domain, a fake form, or a virtual number.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    How it is done
    
      
      
        
    1. Pick a person and a reason — choose an employee whose role holds the desired answer: finance for payment data, IT for tooling, HR for staff details, engineers for projects.
      2. 
        
    2. Build the pretext — write the story and the persona behind it: recruiter with a real-looking profile, IT support from the right vendor, or an HR benefits survey.
      3. 
        
    3. Prepare the stage — register a lookalike domain, host a form or fake login page, or set up a virtual number that shows a friendly caller ID.
      4. 
        
    4. Send the first message — open the conversation with something small and easy to answer, so a reply feels harmless.
      5. 
        
    5. Build trust — keep the chat alive over days or weeks; share plausible documents; mirror the target’s tone and schedule.
      6. 
        
    6. Make the ask — request the specific information: a form filled in, a team name, a document, a code read aloud, or banking details confirmed.
      7. 
        
    7. Record and repeat — store the answers in the target profile, then ask again through another channel or hand the data to the access team.
      8. 
      
    

      
    Where it applies
    
      
    
        
    Email and mailboxes
    The classic channel for T1598.002 and T1598.003. Mail is cheap to send at scale, and gateway logs give defenders their richest records.
    
        
    Social platforms and chat
    Professional networks carry T1598.001. Recruiter personas work because career talk is normal there and platform telemetry rarely reaches your SOC.
    
        
    Web forms and fake portals
    A survey or “benefits enrollment” page collects answers in a structured way. The form can sit on a public form service or a lookalike domain.
    
        
    Voice calls and SMS
    T1598.004 adds pressure: a calm voice with authority and a deadline. Caller ID can be spoofed, so the displayed number proves nothing. A related variant, callback phishing, sends a message that asks the victim to call a number; the elicitation then happens live on the call.
    
      
    

      
    
        Tip — Lab only
        
    You can study the mechanics safely in an isolated lab. Run GoPhish (an open-source phishing simulation tool) with Mailpit (a local test mail trap) in Docker on one offline machine, and send only to test mailboxes you own. Never point the exercise at real people or real domains.
    
      
    
      

      
    Common variations
    
      
    
        
    The job offer
    A recruiter praises the target’s work and asks about their team, tools, and goals. Weeks later the “offer package” asks for a filled form, or arrives with a payload.
    
        
    The IT check
    “We are auditing VPN (virtual private network) access; which client and version do you use?” The ask is small, and the answer is a map of your remote access.
    
        
    The vendor question
    A supplier “updates its records” and asks to confirm invoice and banking details. The reply hands over process data used for payment fraud.
    
        
    The internal survey
    A mass HR-style survey harvests names, dates of birth, and job titles in one pass. Public form services make hosting free and anonymous.
    
      
    

      
    
        Warning — Mistakes attackers make that create noise
        
      
          
    • Mass sends leave gateway telemetry: identical subjects, one sender domain, hundreds of recipients in minutes.
      • 
          
    • Lookalike domains appear in Certificate Transparency logs (public records of newly issued certificates) soon after registration, before most mail lands.
      • 
          
    • Reply-to addresses differ from the display name, and header quirks survive forwarding.
      • 
          
    • Impatient scripts ask several sensitive questions in one thread instead of spreading the asks.
      • 
          
    • The same phone number or form URL is reused across targets, which clusters the whole campaign.
      • 
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    4
    Blue Team Perspective
    
      
    Defenders rarely see the first message arrive. What they do see is a trail of ordinary records: a delivery, a click, an outbound reply, a sign-in. This section shows where to look and what to do in the first hour after a report.
    

      
    Where defenders can see it
    
      
    
        
    
          
    SignalWhere you see itWhy it helps
    
          
            
    User phishing reportMail reporting tool or SOC mailboxThe earliest and cheapest sensor; the forwarded message carries full headers and links.
    
            
    Lookalike or new domainCertificate Transparency feed, domain intelligenceShows staging infrastructure days before the lure lands.
    
            
    Delivery recordsEmail gateway logs, SaaS audit logsRecords sender, subject, authentication results, and every recipient of a campaign.
    
            
    Outbound answersDLP alerts, mail-flow and SaaS logsCatches the exit side: replies, files, and form data that carry internal details out.
    
            
    Identity anomaliesIdP and MFA logsShows whether an elicited credential or code was actually used, and from where.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Artifacts this technique leaves behind
    
      
    
        
    The lure message
    Gateway and mail audit logs keep the sender address, display name, subject, authentication results, and attachment names. This is your evidence base.
    
        
    The click
    DNS queries, proxy fetches, and mail URL-click events record who opened the fake page, from which device and IP address, and when.
    
        
    The answer
    DLP and mail-flow logs show the outbound reply or uploaded file: an org chart to personal mail, a filled form, or banking details in a message body.
    
        
    The reuse
    IdP logs show what the attacker did with the data: sign-in attempts from new networks, MFA pushes approved at odd times, new device enrollments.
    
      
    

      
    
        Note — How this fits the attack chain
        
    A successful elicitation rarely ends the operation; it starts the next one. Expect T1566 access attempts with better lures, T1656 voice impersonation of help desk or executives, or direct credential use at your IdP within hours to weeks.
    
        
    If the ask failed, the same persona often retries on another channel. If it succeeded, watch the named users closely even though no malware was involved.
    
      
    

      
    First-hour triage flow
    
      
      
        
    1. Preserve the evidence — keep the reported message with full headers, links, and attachments in the case record before anything changes.
      2. 
        
    2. Extract indicators — list sender addresses, display names, domains, URLs, and attachment names or hashes; write them defanged (dots shown as [.] so nothing is clickable), for example brand-sso[.]com.
      3. 
        
    3. Hunt for other recipients — search all mailboxes for the same sender, subject, or links to size the campaign in minutes.
      4. 
        
    4. Ask the right questions — did anyone reply, fill a form, open the attachment, or approve an MFA push? A short message template keeps answers fast.
      5. 
        
    5. Check identity and network logs — review IdP sign-ins, MFA events, DNS, and proxy records for the named users over the last 24 hours.
      6. 
        
    6. Contain and brief — block the sender domain and lookalikes at the gateway, then send affected users a plain-language note on what to watch for next.
      7. 
      
    

      
    
        Good — One quick win
        
    Deploy a one-click “Report phishing” button that forwards the complete message, headers included, straight to the SOC. It converts every employee into a sensor for T1598 and costs almost nothing to run. Campaigns reported by users are also the cheapest source of real examples for training.

    The ranking below orders the five most common phishing-for-information threats by expected damage in a typical enterprise. Severity reflects worst-case impact if the threat succeeds unmitigated.

              It converts one message into live credentials, so real damage can follow soon after Reconnaissance.

              SaaS, Email

    5.4 Logs and Patterns
    
      
    Each threat below comes with its main log sources, the fields that matter, what normal looks like, and one realistic example event. All hostile indicators are defanged (dots replaced with [.]) and all addresses come from documentation ranges, so nothing here is clickable or live.
    

      
    Threat 1 — Targeted credential and personal-data elicitation from named employees
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Microsoft 365 audit log (URL click events), email gateway trace, IdP sign-in logs.
    
        
    Key fields
    Operation, Url, UserId, Ip, UserAgent, ClickAllowed, sign-in result.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Clicks go to well-known corporate SaaS domains from office or VPN address ranges; sign-ins match known devices.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    A click lands on a fresh lookalike domain that imitates your brand or sign-in page; a sign-in from a new network follows within the hour.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert when a user clicks a URL on a domain mimicking your brand or a risky new top-level domain, then correlate that user’s IdP activity for the next hour.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 2 — Fake HR survey or benefits-form mass campaign
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Email gateway logs (Proofpoint or Mimecast class), SaaS form audit logs, DLP alerts on submitted data.
    
        
    Key fields
    sender, subject, spf/dkim/dmarc results, attachments, recipient count per wave.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Real HR sends surveys from the internal tenant domain with passing authentication, usually to defined groups.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    The same survey subject hits hundreds of mailboxes in minutes from a lookalike “HR” domain with failing authentication and a fresh form link.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert when benefits or survey keywords appear on an inbound message whose sender fails authentication and whose wave covers many recipients at once.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 3 — Recruiter persona mapping org structure and tooling
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Microsoft 365 audit log (Send events with attachment metadata), DLP alerts, mail-flow rules, HR or IT tickets reporting odd career contacts.
    
        
    Key fields
    Operation, Subject, AttachmentFiles, Recipients, SizeBytes, recipient domain.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Employees share drafts with colleagues on corporate domains; external sends go to known partners and customers.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    Files named like org charts, team structures, or tooling inventories leave to freemail or first-contact personal domains, often after weeks of small talk.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert when outbound mail carries organization-structure keywords in subject or file names and the recipient domain is not a known partner.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 4 — Vendor or bank impersonation collecting invoice and payment details
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Email gateway impersonation protection logs, mail authentication results, accounts-payable system change logs.
    
        
    Key fields
    sender, senderDomainAgeDays, display name, subject, spf/dkim/dmarc, verdict.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Real vendors mail from long-lived domains that pass DMARC (the email authentication standard that proves the sending domain) and match registered supplier records in your vendor master list.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    Banking-detail or invoice subjects arrive from lookalike or days-old domains, with DKIM (a cryptographic email signature) or DMARC failures, or a display name matching a real vendor.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Alert when payment keywords combine with failed authentication or a lookalike sender domain, and route the message to a finance security review queue.
    
      
    
      

      
    Threat 5 — Multichannel pressure: email followed by phone or SMS
    
      
    
        
    Log sources
    Telephony call detail records (Teams voice or PBX, a company’s private phone system), SMS gateway logs, mail logs for the triggering email, IdP logs for later code use.
    
        
    Key fields
    calling number, called user, durationSec, result, timing relative to email delivery.
    
        
    Normal behavior
    Help desk calls match open tickets; individual staff rarely receive several short external calls from one number.
    
        
    Suspicious behavior
    One external number calls many employees in sequence within an email campaign window, with short repeated calls and a caller ID name mimicking IT.
    
        
    Detection logic
    Correlate email-campaign recipients with inbound calls or texts from common numbers within 24 to 48 hours and alert on the pattern, not the single call.
    
      
    
      

      
    5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL
    
      
    Sigma is a YAML rule format that many SIEM (security information and event management) products can translate into their own queries, so a rule written once can run almost anywhere. SPL is Splunk’s search language and is shown here for teams already on Splunk.
    
      
    Each rule below targets one of the five threats from 5.3 and matches the log example from 5.4. Each rule is followed by its SPL version plus a short table of assumptions. All hostile indicators stay defanged; convert [.] back to a dot only inside your own tooling.
    

      
    Rule 1 — Lookalike authentication-domain click (critical)
    
      
    This rule addresses threat 1 and runs at critical severity, because a click on a fake sign-in domain usually precedes account takeover.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectDetail
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsAssumes Microsoft 365 audit events in index=o365 sourcetype="ms:o365:audit" with fields Operation, Url, UserId, Ip. The top-level domain list is an example set; adjust it to your own domain intelligence.
    
            
    False positivesGenuine marketing or vendor portals on modern top-level domains clicked from normal mail.
    
            
    TuningMaintain an allowlist of known corporate and vendor sign-in domains, and require domain age under 30 days where domain intelligence is available.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 2 — Mass HR survey wave (high)
    
      
    This rule addresses threat 2 and runs at high severity, matching the campaign’s bulk personal-data impact.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectDetail
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsAssumes gateway events in index=email sourcetype="proofpoint:pps:message" with fields Direction, Dmarc, Subject, Sender. The raw example in 5.4 shows gateway tokens dir= and dmarc=; rename them to Direction and Dmarc at ingest. Each event covers one recipient, so the event count equals the recipient count. On Mimecast, swap the sourcetype to mimecast:log and use its DMARC field name.
    
            
    False positivesLegitimate bulk HR mail from the internal tenant; annual enrollment notices from real benefits vendors.
    
            
    TuningAllowlist your HR platform domains and raise the recipient threshold only after measuring one week of baseline volume.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 3 — Org-structure file leaving to personal mail (high)
    
      
    This rule addresses threat 3 and runs at high severity; it catches the recruiter persona’s payoff, the outbound document.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectDetail
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsAssumes M365 audit Send events with Subject, Recipients, AttachmentFiles, SizeBytes. Replace corp.example with your tenant domains; add a second allowlist for approved external partners.
    
            
    False positivesApproved recruiters or consultants with contracts on file; employees emailing their own personal drafts.
    
            
    TuningKeep the keyword list narrow and reviewed monthly, and confirm alerts with the sending user’s manager before escalation.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 4 — Payment-detail impersonation (high)
    
      
    This rule addresses threat 4 and runs at high severity, because successful variants end in direct financial loss.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectDetail
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsAssumes gateway events in index=email sourcetype="mimecast:log" with DmarcResult and Subject. The raw example in 5.4 shows the gateway token dmarc=; rename it to DmarcResult at ingest. On Proofpoint, use proofpoint:pps:message and its DMARC field name instead.
    
            
    False positivesSuppliers with misconfigured DMARC; legitimate invoice threads between finance and real vendors.
    
            
    TuningMatch senders against your vendor master list and require either a lookalike score or fresh domain age in addition to the DMARC failure.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    Rule 5 — MFA pressure storm per user (medium)
    
      
    This rule addresses threat 5 and runs at medium severity; it flags the identity side of multichannel pressure before misuse begins.
    
      
      
      
    
        
    
          
    AspectDetail
    
          
            
    Mapping and field assumptionsAssumes Okta system log events in index=okta sourcetype="OktaIM2:log" with eventType, actor.alternateId, outcome.result. On Entra ID, use index=aad sourcetype="azure:aad:signin" and the MFA status fields instead.
    
            
    False positivesMulti-device enrollment days; authenticator sync problems; shared kiosk accounts.
    
            
    TuningSet the threshold from your own baseline (three is an example), and enrich with telephony logs so an active call queue suppresses the alert only when a verified ticket exists.
    
              
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    6
    Real-World Case Study
    
      
    This case study is an educational composite. It merges patterns from public reporting on recruiter-persona campaigns between 2020 and 2022. Vendors call this activity family Operation Dream Job and associate it with Lazarus Group. It does not describe one specific company, and no real person or victim organization is named.
    

      
    Case snapshot
    
      
    
        
    Victim sector
    Aerospace, defense, and media organizations.
    
        
    Year
    2020–2022 (campaign wave).
    
        
    Threat actor
    Recruiter-persona cluster associated by vendors with Lazarus Group.
    
        
    Technique used
    T1598.001 Spearphishing Service plus T1598.003 Spearphishing Link, leading to T1566 payload delivery.
    
        
    Outcome
    Weeks of elicitation ended in a delivered payload; a user report and lookalike-domain telemetry exposed the campaign.
    
      
    

      
    Timeline
    
      
    
        
    
          
    WhenWhat happenedDefender view
    
          
            
    Week 0Fake recruiter profiles appear on professional platforms with stolen photos and borrowed histories.Nothing in company logs; the persona exists only on the third-party platform.
    
            
    Week 1Connection requests go to staff in aerospace, defense, and media roles, praising public work.Staff may accept; no alert exists for platform activity you do not control.
    
            
    Weeks 2–6Long friendly chats about careers, salaries, and frustrations; the persona shares believable documents to look real.Invisible to the SOC; trust building produces no network events.
    
            
    Weeks 6–8The persona asks about team structure, tools, and schedules; the talk moves to personal email or private chat.A policy alert may fire if a work device mails a personal account; usually silence.
    
            
    Week 8A “confidential job spec” or encrypted archive arrives; it holds a downloader, not a document.The gateway or endpoint tool may catch the file; the earlier clean messages all passed.
    
            
    Week 8, day 2One employee reports the whole approach to the SOC with screenshots of the chat.The user report becomes the case trigger and hands over the thread as evidence.
    
            
    Weeks 8–9The SOC hunts sender domains, blocks lookalikes, files platform takedown requests, and sends a staff advisory.Certificate Transparency and proxy telemetry confirm the domain cluster; hunt closes remaining mailboxes.
    
              
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Attack chain
    Persona, trust, elicitation, channel switch, payload. Each step stayed within normal human behavior, which is why only the final payload step produced classic security events.
    
        
    Detection point
    The decisive signal was the user report, supported by lookalike-domain telemetry that tied several sender domains to one campaign weeks earlier in the data.
    
        
    Containment
    The SOC blocked sender domains at the gateway, removed the delivered message from mailboxes, and reset the reporting user’s credentials as a precaution.
    
        
    Eradication
    Endpoint scans found no persistence beyond the caught downloader; the team deleted quarantined files and revoked the session tokens of affected accounts.
    
        
    Recovery
    Mail flow returned to normal the same day, and the staff advisory used the real (sanitized) chat as its example, which doubled report volume for weeks.
    
        
    Impact and consequences
    No confirmed breach resulted, but team structures and tool names had been shared in chat, so the organization treated the incident as a near miss and updated policy.
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Team response and communication
    
        
    The SOC told affected users within hours and gave them one clear instruction: keep the thread, and answer follow-up questions only by phone. HR and legal joined early because personal career details had been shared with a stranger.
    
        
    Leadership received a one-page summary the same week, and a short note went to the sector ISAC so peer organizations could hunt the same domains. Public communication stayed internal; the platform takedown request was filed quietly through the vendor’s abuse channel.
    
      
    

      
    Lessons learned
    
      
      
        
    • Treat recruiter outreach that asks about internal tooling as a security event, not a career opportunity.
      • 
        
    • The user report is the best sensor on third-party platforms; make reporting effortless and thank reporters.
      • 
        
    • Watch work devices talking to personal mail and file-sharing services; that policy violation carried this campaign.
      • 
        
    • Hunt lookalike domains in Certificate Transparency before the lure lands, not after.
      • 
        
    • Rehearse a playbook for off-network social engineering, including voice, chat, and form channels.
      • 
        
    • Brief high-risk roles with realistic examples drawn from your own sanitized cases.
      • 
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    7
    Mitigation and Defense
    
      
    You cannot patch a conversation, so mitigation mixes people, process, and telemetry. The cards below group the controls an enterprise needs; the table at the end tells you what to do first.
    

      
    
        
    Policy and people
      
          
    • Publish one simple rule: never share org charts, tooling names, or staff lists with unverified outsiders.
      • 
          
    • Require a second channel to verify any unusual ask, even from a known name.
      • 
          
    • Make reporting a single click and praise fast reporters publicly.
      • 
          
    • Run realistic elicitation exercises at least twice a year, with voice included.
      • 
        
    
        
    Identity
      
          
    • Enforce phishing-resistant MFA such as FIDO2 security keys or passkeys (login methods a thief cannot reuse) for high-risk roles.
      • 
          
    • Use number matching for push approvals, so a thief cannot approve a prompt without seeing the number.
      • 
          
    • Alert directly on new MFA device enrollments and legacy authentication.
      • 
        
    
        
    Endpoint
      
          
    • Keep mail clients patched and macros disabled by default.
      • 
          
    • Log document and script starts so the payload that follows a lure is visible.
      • 
          
    • Deploy endpoint detection that flags archives and downloaders on first run.
      • 
        
    
        
    Network
      
          
    • Filter DNS with lookalike-domain detection and block fresh domains by default.
      • 
          
    • Log all DNS and proxy traffic; clicks are your earliest strong signal.
      • 
          
    • Apply safe-link rewriting so click events land in your logs.
      • 
        
    
        
    Cloud and SaaS
      
          
    • Enforce DMARC with rejection so direct domain spoofing fails.
      • 
          
    • Disable automatic mail forwarding to personal addresses.
      • 
          
    • Alert on external sharing of org-sensitive files and forms that collect staff data.
      • 
        
    
        
    Logging
      
          
    • Retain mail audit and URL-click logs for at least 90 days, longer if policy allows.
      • 
          
    • Keep gateway verdicts and attachment hashes for retro-hunts.
      • 
          
    • Centralize IdP, telephony, and proxy logs in the SIEM with shared timestamps.
      • 
        
    
        
    Monitoring
      
          
    • Run the five detections from section 5.5 and review them monthly.
      • 
          
    • Alert on certificates for lookalike domains of your brand terms.
      • 
          
    • Correlate campaigns across email, chat, and voice, not per channel.
      • 
        
    
        
    Backup and response
      
          
    • Rehearse the phishing playbook quarterly, including voice cases.
      • 
          
    • Pre-position blocks, session revocation, and reset steps as runbooks.
      • 
          
    • After each case, update one rule and one training example.
      • 
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Home and small office
    
        
      
          
    • Be careful with strangers who ask about your workplace details in chat or by phone.
      • 
          
    • Verify requests by calling back on a number you looked up yourself.
      • 
          
    • Keep your phone and computer updated; most lures need old habits, not old software.
      • 
          
    • Use app-based MFA or passkeys, and never read a one-time code aloud to a caller.
      • 
          
    • When a recruiter you never met asks about your employer’s systems, treat it as a signal, not small talk.
      • 
        
    
      
    

      
    Prioritized actions
    
      
    
        
    
          
    PriorityActionEffortOwnerExpected risk reduction

    Common Mistakes and Best Practices
    
      
    Most failures with this technique are process failures, not tool failures. The table lists twelve mistakes seen in real programs, including the classic detection-engineering traps, with the fix and the habit that prevents each one.
    

      
    
        
    
          
    MistakeSymptomRoot causeFixPrevention
    
          
            
    Treating reported phishing as spam and closing the ticketThe same campaign returns next month; reporters stop reportingNo owner for report handlingEvery report opens a case with a one-hour triage targetAutomate report-to-case creation
    
            
    Hunting only for malware attachmentsClean questionnaire documents pass; answers leak days laterDetections built for T1566 payload habits, not T1598Add rules for clean files and forms that ask questionsReview rules against the technique, not only the payload
    
            
    Trusting caller ID on voice asksThe help desk “verifies” the attacker in personAssumption that the phone network shows the true numberVerify with a callback on a number from the staff directoryTeach caller ID spoofing in every exercise
    
            
    Blocking the sender address onlyThe actor returns next week from a new mailboxBlocklists stored per addressBlock at domain and infrastructure level, including lookalikesMaintain a domain-based blocklist with expiry reviews
    
            
    No lookalike-domain monitoringBrand impostors appear with no warning at allNobody watches Certificate Transparency or new registrationsSubscribe to certificate and domain feeds for brand termsAssign the feed to threat intel with a weekly review
    
            
    Awareness training with generic examples onlyStaff fail realistic tests weeks after training dayTraining shows obvious scams, not real pretextsTrain with local, realistic pretexts like the ones in this guideRefresh examples quarterly with sanitized real cases
    
            
    Over-broad subject keyword rulesFinance complains; the rule gets switched offSingle keywords like “invoice” match all normal mailRequire keyword plus failed authentication or lookalike contextTest every new rule against a week of real mail first

    • Triage every user report within the hour.
      • 
          
    • Check the lookalike-domain feed for new brand matches.
      • 
          
    • Scan IdP logs for MFA anomalies and odd enrollments.
      • 
          
    • Glance at the outbound DLP summary for sensitive files leaving.
      • 
        
    
        
    Weekly habits
      
          
    • Tune the five rules from section 5.5 with the week’s data.
      • 
          
    • Review blocked senders and domains for expiring entries.
      • 
          
    • Spot-check gateway logs for mass-campaign shapes.
      • 
          
    • Update the threat list from section 5.3 with fresh intel.
      • 
        
    
        
    Monthly habits
      
          
    • Replay one rule against recorded logs to prove it still works.
      • 
          
    • Refresh one training example from a real sanitized case.
      • 
          
    • Confirm every detection still has a named owner.
      • 
          
    • Report volumes, hits, and gaps to leadership in one page.

    Cheat Sheet and Quick Reference
    
      
    The essentials of this guide in one place: key facts, the five detections to remember, an eight-step response checklist, and every official link you need.
    

      
    
        
    
          
    Key facts
    
          
    
            
    ATT&CK ID
    T1598 (Reconnaissance, TA0043)
    
            
    Sub-techniques
    T1598.001 Service, T1598.002 Attachment, T1598.003 Link, T1598.004 Voice
    
            
    Platforms
    PRE (pre-attack; delivery runs over email, SaaS, and voice)
    
            
    Data sources
    Application logs (email, SaaS), Network traffic, Telephony logs
    
            
    Contrast
    T1566 is the Initial Access twin: same tricks, different goal
    
            
    Best sensor
    User phishing reports plus gateway and SaaS audit logs
    
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Top detections
    
          
      
            
    1. URL clicks to lookalike authentication domains, with IdP correlation (critical).
      2. 
            
    2. Mass HR-survey subjects from senders failing DMARC across many recipients (high).
      3. 
            
    3. Org-structure or tooling files mailed to personal and freemail domains (high).
      4. 
            
    4. Invoice or banking-detail asks from lookalike or failing-auth senders (high).
      5. 
            
    5. Repeated MFA attempts per user, correlated with calls or texts (medium).
      6. 
          
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    Response checklist
    
        
      
          
    • Preserve the reported message with full headers in the case record.
      • 
          
    • Extract sender, domains, URLs, and attachment names; write indicators defanged.
      • 
          
    • Hunt all mailboxes for the same sender, subject, and links.
      • 
          
    • Ask named users whether they replied, filled a form, or approved an MFA push.
      • 
          
    • Check IdP, proxy, and telephony logs for those users over the last 24 to 48 hours.
      • 
          
    • Block the sender domain and lookalikes at the gateway and DNS.
      • 
          
    • Brief affected users in plain language on what to watch for next.
      • 
          
    • Close the case with one updated rule and one updated training example.
