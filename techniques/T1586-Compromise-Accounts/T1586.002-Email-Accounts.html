Compromise Accounts: Email Accounts T1586.002

An email inbox is the master key of digital life: business conversations, invoices, contact lists, and password reset links all live there. When an attacker takes over a mailbox — personal or corporate — they can read secrets, send phishing as a trusted person, and hijack other accounts through those reset links. This guide explains the technique step by step, shows an animated 8-step attack story, and gives defenders ready-to-use detections. It is written for people who are new to security operations.

    1Overview

    What T1586.002 means, why a stolen mailbox is so valuable, and what this guide teaches you.

    Compromise Accounts: Email Accounts (T1586.002) is a MITRE ATT&CK sub-technique in the Resource Development tactic. In plain words: an attacker takes control of an email mailbox — often before the real attack starts — and then uses that mailbox as a tool. The mailbox may be a personal Gmail account or a work mailbox in Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace. Once the attacker reads the mail, they can speak as the owner, and they can open doors to other services.

    ATT&CK lists the platform for this technique as PRE (pre-compromise). PRE means the activity happens before an attack touches the target's own systems. The attacker is preparing a resource for a later attack, and the email account is that resource, wherever it is hosted.

    ATT&CK lists this technique under Resource Development, which is the stage where attackers gather what they need for a future attack. A compromised email account is exactly that kind of resource. It usually feeds later stages: it enables Initial Access (phishing sent from a trusted address) and Collection (reading secrets out of the mail). Some groups also use compromised mailboxes to register or vouch for new attacker infrastructure, such as domains that look legitimate.

    The mailbox is a target

    Mail holds contracts, invoices, customer lists, and business plans. Reading it quietly is already a serious data loss, even if nothing else happens.

    The mailbox is a launchpad

    Mail sent from a real, trusted address gets opened. Attackers reply inside long-running conversations, so the malicious message looks like a normal continuation.

    The mailbox is a reset hub

    Password reset links for banks, payroll, VPNs, and other mailboxes all arrive by email. Whoever reads the mailbox can take over those services too.

    Why this matters

    Business email compromise (BEC — fraud that uses a real, trusted mailbox to trick people into paying or sending data) causes billions of dollars in reported losses every year according to the FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3). Most BEC cases start with a compromised or spoofed mailbox. The money transfer is the last step of a chain that began weeks earlier with a quiet sign-in, a hidden inbox rule, or a malicious app consent. Defenders who watch mailbox behavior can catch that chain early. ATT&CK places this technique in the pre-compromise space and points to mitigation M1056 Pre-compromise — controls that act before a takeover, which Section 7 of this guide details.

    Words to know

    Credential stuffing
    Replaying passwords stolen from old breaches against many login pages, hoping users reused them.
    Info stealer
    Malware that quietly collects saved passwords and cookies from infected computers and sells them in logs.
    Inbox rule
    An automatic action a mailbox applies to incoming mail, such as moving messages to a folder. Attackers abuse it to hide warning mail.
    Forwarding rule
    A setting that copies every incoming message to another address — often an attacker's mailbox.
    OAuth consent
    Permission a user grants to an application, for example the right to read their mail. It survives password resets.
    Impossible travel
    Two sign-ins from distant places in a time too short for a real trip — a strong takeover signal.
    BEC
    Business email compromise: fraud that uses a real or look-alike mailbox to trick people into paying or sending data.
    Session token
    A temporary pass that keeps a user signed in. Stealing one lets an attacker skip the password step.

    Written version of the simulation

    The same story as the animation above, in plain text, for screen readers and printing.

    1. Step 1 — Buy leaked passwords. The attacker buys a list of stolen email passwords from a criminal market. What defenders see: None. Lists circulate from old breaches and info-stealer infections, outside company systems.
    2. Step 2 — Credential stuffing on webmail. The attacker replays the list against the webmail login. One password still works and the session opens. What defenders see: A burst of failed sign-ins from one IP (the numeric address of a device on the internet), then one success. Impossible-travel risk engines may flag it.
    3. Step 3 — Hide with an inbox rule. The attacker creates an inbox rule that moves security and quarantine mail into a hidden folder. What defenders see: A New-InboxRule audit event appears. This is the single best early signal of a taken mailbox.
    4. Step 4 — Harvest the mailbox. The attacker searches and reads mail in bulk: threads, invoices, contacts, and contracts. What defenders see: MailItemsAccessed events spike (needs E5 licensing), or volume anomalies show up in other mailbox telemetry.
    5. Step 5 — Reply inside a real thread. The attacker adds a short, believable reply with a malicious link to an ongoing conversation. What defenders see: The mail gateway sees unusual external sends from an internal account. Recipients start reporting odd replies.
    6. Step 6 — Set forwarding to exfiltrate. The attacker adds a forwarding rule that copies incoming mail to an outside mailbox they own. What defenders see: A new forwarding rule to an external domain is created. Most tenants alert on this as high severity.
    7. Step 7 — Chain password resets. The attacker uses reset links in the mailbox to take over the user's bank, payroll, and other accounts. What defenders see: Reset notices pile up in the mailbox. Changes on external services are not logged by the company.
    8. Step 8 — SOC response. The SOC revokes sessions, resets the password, purges rules, and warns the people who got malicious mail. What defenders see: Mail flow rules are purged, sessions revoked, and recipients warned. A tenant-wide hunt for more rules follows.
    Note

    This story is a teaching model. It merges several common attacker behaviors into one path so you can see how the signals build up. The example in the canvas uses fake company and domain names only.

    3Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    Why attackers pick email accounts, how the takeover works at a concept level, and where they slip up. All examples here are conceptual and lab-only.

    Why a mailbox, specifically

    Email gives the attacker three things at once: history (threads full of context), identity (mail sent from the real address carries trust), and reach (reset links for other services). Very few single resources offer all three. That is why this technique sits in Resource Development — it is an investment made before the main attack.

    When attackers use it

    Common uses: business email compromise that redirects payments; reply-chain phishing that spreads inside a supplier network; quiet collection of deal, customer, or research data; and takeover of second accounts through reset mail. The mailbox can also warm up a look-alike domain or vouch for new infrastructure.

    How the takeover works — conceptual stages

    The table below describes attacker behavior at a concept level only. It is written so defenders can recognize the stages. It is not an operational recipe, and you should test it only in your own lab against accounts you own.

    Stages of a mailbox takeover and the evidence each stage leaves behind
    StageWhat the attacker does (concept)Evidence it leaves
    1. Get a footholdBuy credentials from info-stealer log markets, replay leaked passwords (credential stuffing), phish the user, or buy an aged account that already looks human.Failed sign-in bursts, sign-ins from new countries, gateway traces of the phishing mail.
    2. Stay hiddenCreate inbox rules that move security alerts, quarantine notices, and financial mail into forgotten folders; mark those messages as read.New-InboxRule / Set-InboxRule audit events.
    3. Learn the personaRead threads, calendars, and contacts to copy writing style, learn ongoing deals, and pick the next victims.Bulk MailItemsAccessed events (with E5 licensing), search activity.
    4. Use the accountReply inside real conversations with a link or changed bank details; send invoice fraud from executive mailboxes.Unusual external sends, recipient complaints, gateway anomalies.
    5.     Variations the technique covers

    • Executive invoice fraud (BEC): reply from a finance or executive mailbox and change the bank details on a real invoice or payroll file.
    • Thread hijacking: append a malicious link or attachment to an ongoing conversation, so the message rides on trust the thread already has.
    • Spam-for-hire: use taken mailboxes to send mass scam mail, or rent them out to other criminals.
    • Buying credentials: purchase mailbox logins from breach dumps, info-stealer log markets, or access brokers.
    • Paying insiders: the official ATT&CK description notes that adversaries may pay employees, suppliers, or business partners for access to credentials.

    Prerequisites the attacker needs

    • Working credentials for the mailbox, or a stolen session token or OAuth grant.
    • A way past MFA: push-bombing, a fake consent page that relays codes, or an account with no MFA at all.
    • Knowledge of the target's habits — usually read directly from the mailbox itself.
    • Patience: the best attackers stay quiet for days before sending anything.

    Where the technique applies

    • Consumer webmail (Gmail, Outlook.com, Yahoo) — personal reset hub, often the path into a business through a chief executive's (CEO) private mail.
    • Corporate mailboxes in Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace — richest data and internal reach.
    • Shared and role mailboxes (billing@, hr@) — often weaker monitoring and delegated access (mailbox rights given to another user).
    • Aged or purchased accounts (see T1585.002 Establish Accounts: Email Accounts) — ready-made trust.

    Attacker mistakes that help defenders

    • Signing in from a country or network never seen for that user — impossible travel and atypical location alerts.
    • Creating inbox rules with careless names like "spam move" minutes after a takeover.
    • Pointing forwarding at a fresh, unknown domain instead of a plausible-looking one.
    • Reusing the same VPN (a tool that hides a device's real network location) exit node, user-agent (the software name a browser or app sends when connecting), or cookies across many victims, which links incidents together.
    • Enabling the rule set and sending within the same hour — a clear burst of changes from one new session.
    Danger

    This technique is powerful because it abuses trust, not software flaws. A takeover alone rarely triggers endpoint tools. Defenses must therefore be behavioral: sign-in risk, mailbox audit events, and mail flow anomalies — not just antivirus.

    Note

    Lab-only reminder: security teams and red teams may simulate this technique on mailboxes and tenants they own, with written permission. Attempting any of this against accounts you do not control is illegal in most jurisdictions.

    4Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    How defenders notice a taken mailbox, which artifacts it leaves, and a triage flow from alert to closure.

    How defenders notice

    • Audit events that change mail behavior: New-InboxRule, Set-InboxRule, New-TransportRule (a transport rule is a mail-flow rule an admin sets for the whole organization), Set-Mailbox with forwarding.
    • Identity signals: impossible travel, unfamiliar sign-in properties, legacy authentication, anonymous IP or anonymous VPN sign-ins.
    • App signals: Consent to application events asking for Mail.Read or Mail.ReadWrite.
    • Mail flow signals: unusual external send volume from one mailbox, replies with links appended to old threads.
    • Human signals: users reporting strange replies "from themselves", partners asking about odd requests, bounced mail piling up.

    Artifacts a compromised mailbox leaves

    Where to look (data sources)

    • Microsoft 365 unified audit log — index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity in Splunk. It carries New-InboxRule, New-TransportRule, Set-Mailbox, MailItemsAccessed, and Consent to application events (some need Audit Premium / E5).
    • Entra ID sign-in logs — index=azure sourcetype=ms:aad:signin.
    • Entra ID audit logs — index=azure sourcetype=ms:aad:audit for MFA methods, delegate grants, and app registrations.
    • Mail gateway or Defender for Office 365 traces — delivery verdicts, URL clicks, outbound volume. The sourcetype is environment-specific.
    • Proxy logs — visits to harvest kits and attacker domains (environment-specific sourcetype).
    • DMARC, SPF, and DKIM (three email standards that prove a message really comes from your domain) — aggregate reports show who is sending as your domain.
    • User reports through a one-click "report phish" button — the cheapest sensor you have.

    Why it matters for the SOC

    A compromised mailbox rarely announces itself. There is no malware to scan and often nothing to quarantine. The SOC's best chance is a small set of behavioral alerts (rules, forwarding, consents, risk sign-ins, send volume) wired to a clear playbook. This technique also explains many later incidents: the phishing mail that "came from finance" or the payroll change "from the CEO" usually began here.

    Triage flow: from alert to closure

    • Step 1 — AlertPick up the signal. Typical triggers: New-InboxRule with security keywords, external forwarding, high-risk sign-in, OAuth consent with mail scope, or a send burst.
    • Step 2 — ValidateConfirm it is real. Check the session: IP, country, client, time. Compare with the user's normal pattern. Ask the user through a second channel, never the mailbox itself.
    • Step 3 — ContainCut the attacker out. Revoke sessions and refresh tokens (the long-lived passes that create new sessions), reset the password, and if MFA was tampered with, re-enroll the user with phishing-resistant MFA.
    • Step 4 — Sweep the mailboxList the persistence. Enumerate inbox rules, forwarding settings, delegate permissions, registered devices, and OAuth grants. Remove everything not owned by the user.
    • Step 5 — Measure the readingBound the collection. Use MailItemsAccessed and search events to work out what was read, so you know which customers, contracts, or payment details are exposed.
    • Step 6 — Fix the outbound damageChase the sent mail. Pull the messages the attacker sent, warn recipients, recall or block what is possible, and check for replies that continued the fraud.
    • Step 7 — Hunt widerCheck the tenant. Run the same searches for every user: rules, forwarding, consents, and risk sign-ins. One taken mailbox is often not the only one.
    • Step 8 — Close and learnDocument and harden. Record the timeline, notify stakeholders per policy, and feed the gaps you found (audit coverage, alert tuning, MFA strength) back into Section 7 mitigations.
    Good practice

    Two signals together beat any single alert: for example, a risky sign-in followed by a new inbox rule within an hour is a near-certain takeover, while either one alone could be noise. Correlate before you page.

    How to apply intel: a four-step loop

    1. Collect. Subscribe to two or three sources from the table above and assign an owner for each.
    2. Analyze. Ask for every report: does this touch our mail stack? Which of our six threats in 5.3 does it match?
    3. Deploy. Turn findings into tuning and new rules — new keywords for the inbox-rule rule, new look-alike domains for the gateway, new thresholds.
    4. Review. Every quarter, drop rules that never fire and record why, so the loop stays lean.

    5.3 Threat Ranking and Categorization

    Below are the six threats this page tracks. The rank mixes how much damage each threat does, how often it is seen in public reporting, and how early it can be detected. Each threat reappears in 5.4 (logs and patterns) and 5.5 (Sigma and SPL pair).

    Ranked threats for compromised email accounts (T1586.002)
    #ThreatSeverityPlatformsPreconditionsImpactWhy this rank
    1 Business email compromise (BEC) — invoice and payment fraud sent from an executive or finance mailbox. Paired detection: S3 impossible travel, S6 send burst. Critical Email Identity Access to an executive or finance mailbox; enough thread history to sound credible. Direct wire fraud — the FBI IC3 reports billions of dollars in yearly BEC-related losses. Highest direct financial damage and the most common costly outcome of a taken mailbox.
    2 Reply-chain phishing — malware or credential links appended to real, ongoing conversations. Paired detection: S1 suspicious inbox rule. High Email Mailbox access plus real threads to hijack; recipients who trust the sender. Internal and partner compromise; the attack spreads with the trust of your brand. Very high success rate because the mail continues an existing thread; damage multiplies across organizations.
    3 Hidden forwarding or transport rule for quiet, long-term exfiltration. Paired detection: S2 external forwarding. High Email An active session or an app consent that can change mailbox settings. Long-term loss of every incoming message, often unnoticed for months. Survives password resets, so it turns a short incident into a long breach.
    4 Malicious OAuth consent granting Mail.Read or Mail.ReadWrite. Paired detection: S5 risky OAuth consent. Critical Cloud Email Consent phishing while the user is signed in; tenant settings that allow user consent. Steady mailbox access through the app's API (a machine-to-machine interface) that survives password resets and MFA changes. Hardest to see and strongest persistence; API access bypasses the login path your tools watch.
    5 Password-reset takeover of external services through reset mail in the mailbox. Paired detection: S4 MailItemsAccessed volume. High Identity Read access to reset mail for banks, payroll, insurance, or personal accounts. The compromise expands beyond the company to money and identity services. Outside your logging radius, so it usually surfaces only through victims or banks.
    6 Mass spam and scam sends from tenant mailboxes, damaging domain reputation. Paired detection: S6 send burst. Medium Email Network Compromised accounts at scale, or one account with a weak sending limit. Deliverability damage, blocklisting, and lost trust for all outbound mail. Painful but recoverable; often a side effect of the threats ranked above.
    Good practice

    Notice the overlap: threats 1, 2, and 6 all start with the same first act — someone new signs in and changes mail behavior. Detect the common acts well (rules, forwarding, consents, risk sign-ins, send volume) and you cover most of the table.

    Paired detection: Sigma S4 (MailItemsAccessed bulk anomaly) in 5.5 — bulk reading is how the attacker finds the reset mail worth using.

    Pattern 6 — Mass spam and scam sends from tenant mailboxes

    Paired detection: Sigma S6 (send burst) in 5.5.

    5.5 Sigma Rules and Splunk SPL

    Sigma is an open rule format for detections, written in YAML (a plain-text format for structured rules), that you can convert with the free Sigma tools into the query language of your SIEM (security information and event management software — the central platform that stores logs and raises alerts). SPL (Search Processing Language) is the query language of Splunk. Below, every threat from 5.3 gets one Sigma rule and one matching SPL query. Each SPL query states its field assumptions in an assumption eval, because add-on versions extract fields slightly differently.

    Targets threat 2 (reply-chain phishing) and any takeover where the attacker hides warning mail. Fires when a new inbox rule mentions security, quarantine, spam, or phishing text in its conditions.

    Mapping note
    logsource microsoft365 / exchange → index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity
    Warning

    False positives and tuning for S1: users create harmless rules daily, often with the word "spam" in the name. Keep the keyword list tight (security, quarantine, phishing first), alert only on New-InboxRule and Set-InboxRule from new sessions, and suppress rules whose UserId matches a documented service account. A rule that deletes mail (rather than moving it) deserves a higher severity.

    S2 — Automatic Email Forwarding to an External Domain High

    Targets threat 3 (hidden forwarding). Covers mailbox-level forwarding, inbox-rule forwards, and transport rules that redirect or blind-copy. Related technique: T1114.003 Email Forwarding Rule.

    Mapping note
    logsource microsoft365 / exchange → index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity
    Warning

    False positives and tuning for S2: the filter_internal block is your allowlist. Maintain it as data (a lookup table), not as edits to the rule. Where possible, block automatic external forwarding at the tenant level so that any event this rule sees is already policy-breaking. Transport rules created by administrators deserve a separate, lower-noise review path.

    S3 — High-Risk or Impossible-Travel Sign-in to Mail Access High

    Targets threat 1 (BEC takeover). Fires on successful sign-ins that Entra ID scores medium or high risk, or that carry the unlikelyTravel risk event, on mail-related apps.

    Mapping note
    logsource azure / signinlogs → index=azure sourcetype=ms:aad:signin
    Warning

    False positives and tuning for S3: travelers and VPN users generate most of the noise. Keep a short-term allowlist for announced travel, and raise the response when the same session also creates rules or grants consents (correlate with S1, S2, and S5 by userPrincipalName and time). If your tenant enforces MFA everywhere, treat any successful medium/high-risk mail sign-in as investigation-worthy.

    S4 — Bulk MailItemsAccessed Activity on One Mailbox Medium

    Targets threat 5 (reset chaining) and bulk harvesting. Needs Audit (Premium) / E5 licensing. The threshold is per user per hour and must be tuned to your baselines.

    Mapping note
    logsource microsoft365 / exchange → index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity; the Sigma timeframe: 1h maps to the 1-hour bins shown below
    Warning

    False positives and tuning for S4: 400 items per hour is a starting point, not a truth. Baseline per department for two weeks, exempt known service principals by AppId, and alert harder when the volume comes with a new IP or a search-heavy pattern. Document the E5 dependency in your coverage matrix so nobody assumes this detection exists on E3.

    S5 — User Consent to OAuth App with Mail Permissions Critical

    Targets threat 4 (malicious OAuth consent). Fires when a non-admin user consents to an application whose scope grants mail read or write access. Such access survives password resets, which is why this rule is critical.

    Mapping note
    logsource microsoft365 / azure_activedirectory → index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity
    Danger

    False positives and tuning for S5: the best fix is preventive — disable user consent to apps tenant-wide (or cap it to a low-risk publisher set) so this alert becomes rare and meaningful. Note that Mail.Read as a substring also matches Mail.ReadWrite; that is intentional, because both scopes expose the mailbox. When this fires, revoke the app's service principal grants, not just the user's password.

    S6 — Send Burst to Many External Recipients from One Mailbox Medium

    Targets threat 6 (mass spam) and the send-out phase of threat 1 (BEC). Fires when one user sends more than 30 messages inside a 10-minute window.

    Mapping note
    logsource microsoft365 / exchange → index=o365 sourcetype=o365:management:activity; without E5, run the same threshold on your mail gateway's outbound volume (environment-specific sourcetype)
    Warning

    False positives and tuning for S6: legitimate bulk senders will trip this rule at first. Build a per-user baseline, exempt the marketing account, and watch the dc(Subject) field: one subject across 30 sends is campaign-like, while 30 different subjects is more human. For recipient-level precision, join with gateway or message-trace data and count external recipients instead of messages.

    Note

    Deployment order that works well: S1, S2, and S5 first (low noise, high value), then S3, then S4 and S6 once baselines exist. Re-check all six after tenant policy changes, because thresholds and allowlists drift over time.

    6Real-World Case Study

    The 2016 compromise of Democratic National Committee (DNC) email — the most public example of T1586.002 behavior, reconstructed from public reporting.

    Note

    Based on public reporting (source links); details simplified for teaching. Key sources: the CrowdStrike intrusion analysis, the July 2018 US DOJ indictment of twelve GRU officers, and the US Senate Intelligence Committee report on Russian interference. Attribution statements below reflect those public findings.

    In spring 2016, hackers linked by US investigators to Russian military intelligence (GRU Unit 26165, tracked as APT28 or FANCY BEAR) targeted staff of the DNC, the governing body of the US Democratic Party. They sent convincing spearphishing emails with shortened links to a fake Google security page. The page collected the staffers' Gmail passwords and, critically, their multi-factor authentication codes as they were typed. With those mailboxes, the attackers read months of private conversation, collected opposition-research files, and later leaked the mail to shape public opinion during the presidential election campaign. This is T1586.002 in its purest form: the email accounts were the resource — target, launchpad, and evidence locker at once.

    Attack chain in ATT&CK terms

    1. Research and persona building, then spearphishing (T1566) with look-alike Google domains.
    2. Credential capture on the fake page, including live MFA codes.
    3. Compromise of email accounts (this technique, T1586.002) and valid account use (T1078) to read mail quietly.
    4. Compromised accounts used to phish further — trusted addresses sent more lures to new victims.
    5. Collection from mailboxes and shared drives (T1114 and related), then exfiltration of large archives.
    6. Leakage through the DCLeaks persona, Guccifer 2.0, and WikiLeaks (linked to GRU Unit 74455 by the DOJ indictment).

    Timeline

    • Fall 2015External warning. The FBI contacts the DNC about suspicious activity tied to Russian actors; the inquiry does not turn into a full investigation at that time.
    • Late 2015 – early 2016Preparation. Per the DOJ indictment, GRU Unit 26165 researches victims and registers look-alike Google domains for credential capture.
    • Early April 2016Spearphishing wave. Hundreds of DNC and campaign staff receive emails with shortened links to the fake Google security page.
    • April 2016Mailboxes taken. At least one staffer enters credentials and an MFA code on the fake page. The attacker now owns a real, trusted mailbox.
    • April 6 – late June 2016Quiet collection. The attacker reads mail, moves to the network, and exfiltrates large archives, including opposition research.
    • April 29, 2016Detection point. DNC technology staff spot suspicious activity near opposition-research data and call CrowdStrike — weeks after the mailboxes fell.
    • May 2016Attribution. CrowdStrike identifies two intruders: FANCY BEAR (APT28, present since about April 6) and COZY BEAR (APT29, present since about summer 2015).
    • June 2016Containment attempt. Credentials are reset and access is cut, but the attackers keep trying re-entry with fresh phishing.
    • June 14 – 15, 2016Public exposure. The breach becomes public; the "Guccifer 2.0" persona claims credit and posts stolen documents the next day.
    • July 22, 2016Leak peak. WikiLeaks publishes roughly 20,000 DNC emails days before the party convention.
    • July 24 – 25, 2016Leadership fallout. The DNC chair resigns after leaked mail shows bias concerns.
    • October 7, 2016Second leak wave. WikiLeaks begins releasing a campaign chairman's stolen mail; the same day, US agencies publicly attribute the operations to Russia.
    • July 2018Legal follow-up. The DOJ indicts twelve GRU officers for the hacking and leaking operations.
    Detection point — how it was actually caught

    The mailbox takeover itself was never caught by an email alert, because in 2016 the DNC had no mailbox-behavior telemetry. Discovery came on April 29 from network monitoring: suspicious activity around opposition-research data. The detection gap lasted weeks — exactly the gap that Section 5 detections (risk sign-ins, inbox rules, forwarding, bulk reading) are designed to close today.

    Containment

    Once engaged, CrowdStrike and the DNC reset compromised credentials, cut attacker access, and deployed endpoint sensors across the environment. The attackers were evicted from the network, though re-entry attempts by phishing continued.

    Eradication

    Analysts removed attacker implants and tooling, blocked known indicators, and tightened access. The two intruder groups required separate handling because their footholds and timelines differed.

    Recovery

    The DNC operated under continuous monitoring through the election. Mail, identity, and endpoint controls were hardened, and the organization publicly acknowledged the breach and cooperated with investigators.

    Impact and consequences

    Months of private mail became public, shaping the campaign's news cycle. The party chair resigned. Trust inside and around the organization suffered lasting damage, and the event drove years of investigations, indictments, and policy change.

    Team response and communication

    Three parties had to work together: the DNC as victim, CrowdStrike as the retained response firm, and the FBI as the government agency. Public reporting shows both the value and the friction of that arrangement — leadership briefings happened, evidence was shared with investigators, but the Senate committee later criticized slow coordination and missed early warnings between the parties. The takeaway for any organization: agree in advance on who calls whom, what is shared, and how fast.

    Lessons learned

    • Phishing-resistant MFA for high-risk people. The fake page captured MFA codes in real time. Password-plus-code was not enough for the people attackers want most.
    • Monitor executive mailboxes as production systems. Risk sign-ins, rules, forwarding, and bulk reading are all visible today — none were watched then.
    • Alert on mailbox changes, not just network events. A New-InboxRule or external forwarding alert would have flagged the takeover weeks earlier.
    • Watch for your brand being leaked. Executive mail appears on leak sites; monitoring for your own domain and staff names gives early notice.
    • Assume re-entry after eviction. Resetting credentials is not the end; the same lures keep working until the human layer and the domain are hardened.
    • Pre-agree the escalation path. External warnings (like the FBI's 2015 contact) need an owner and a defined next step, not an unread inbox.

    7Mitigation and Defense

    ATT&CK lists no single preventive control for T1586.002. The answer is defense in depth: make the takeover hard, make it loud, and make it short.

    Note

    ATT&CK's official mitigation mapping for this technique is M1056 Pre-compromise. Its official note, paraphrased: this technique cannot be easily mitigated with preventive controls, because it is based on behaviors performed outside of the scope of enterprise defenses and controls. That is why the cards below group many smaller controls instead of one big fix.

    Identity controls

    • Phishing-resistant MFA — FIDO2 security keys or passkeys (sign-ins bound to the real website, so a fake page cannot collect them) — for executives, finance, and admins.
    • Number matching and risk-based conditional access for everyone else.
    • Disable legacy authentication protocols; they bypass MFA.
    • Block or step up sign-ins from anonymizers and unfamiliar countries.

    Email platform controls

    • Block automatic external forwarding at the tenant level.
    • Enable mailbox auditing for all users and verify it stays on.
    • DMARC at p=reject plus SPF and DKIM, so others cannot spoof your domain directly.
    • External-sender banners and impersonation protection for display names.

    Cloud and app controls

    • Disable or limit user OAuth consent; route app approvals through admins.
    • Review third-party app grants quarterly; revoke what is unused.
    • Alert on consents granting mail scopes (rule S5 in Section 5.5).

    Monitoring and response

    • Ship the M365 unified audit log and Entra sign-in logs to your SIEM; keep at least one year.
    • Deploy the six detections in Section 5.5 with the tuning notes.
    • Write a BEC playbook: revoke sessions, purge rules, notify recipients, verify payments out-of-band (through a second channel, such as a phone call to a known number).
    • Run a yearly tabletop (a guided practice exercise on paper, not on live systems) with executives acting as victims.

    People and process

    • Train staff to report suspicious mail with one click — and thank them when they do.
    • Require out-of-band verification for any bank-detail or payroll change, even from a known executive.
    • Quarterly access review of delegates, shared mailboxes, and forwarding.

    Home and small office

    Without an IT team: turn on two-factor authentication with an authenticator app or passkey for every important account, keep recovery email and phone current, use different passwords everywhere (a password manager helps), check account "recent activity" pages monthly, and be suspicious of any email that asks for a code — even mid-conversation. If a mailbox feels taken over, change the password from a trusted device first, then sign out all sessions.

    8Common Mistakes and Best Practices

    Twelve blue-team mistakes seen again and again around mailbox compromise — each with a symptom, the root cause, the fix, and prevention.

    Mistake 1: Assuming mailbox audit covers everyone.

    Symptom: An investigation finds no MailItemsAccessed or Send events for the victim.
    Root cause: Advanced audit events need E5 or the add-on; the team assumed tenant-wide coverage.
    Fix: Map licensing per user, and route Patterns 2, 5, and 6 through gateway telemetry where licensing is missing.
    Prevention: Keep a licensing-to-detection coverage matrix and recheck it each contract year.

    Mistake 2: Alerting only on transport rules.

    Symptom: Forwarding exfiltration ran for months with no alert.
    Root cause: Only admin-level New-TransportRule was monitored; the attacker used a mailbox-level rule.
    Fix: Monitor Set-Mailbox forwarding, New-InboxRule forwards, and transport rules together (rule S2).
    Prevention: Alert on the behavior (any forward target outside the allowlist), not on one cmdlet.

    Mistake 3: Ignoring OAuth consents.

    Symptom: Mail kept leaking after two password resets.
    Root cause: A mail-scoped app grant survived every reset; nobody reviewed consents.
    Fix: Revoke the app's service-principal grants and deploy rule S5.
    Prevention: Disable user consent tenant-wide; review grants quarterly.

    Mistake 4: Using impossible travel as the only signal.

    Symptom: Alert fatigue plus a real takeover that used a domestic VPN and never fired.
    Root cause: One signal, one threshold, no context.
    Fix: Correlate risk sign-ins with rule changes, consents, and send volume (S1, S2, S5, S6).
    Prevention: Build compound detections and keep travel/VPN allowlists small and current.

    Mistake 5: No recipient notification process.

    Symptom: A partner paid a fraudulent invoice days after containment ended.
    Root cause: The team cleaned the mailbox but never chased the attacker's sent mail.
    Fix: Add "list and warn recipients" as a required playbook step.
    Prevention: Track outbound mail from the compromise window and confirm acknowledgments.

    Mistake 6: Allowlist sprawl.

    Symptom: The forwarding alert fires on real attacks but nobody acts on it, because "it always fires".
    Root cause: Exceptions were added one by one without a review and never removed.
    Fix: Move allowlists to lookup tables with owners and expiry dates.
    Prevention: Monthly allowlist review; every entry needs a reason and an owner.

    Mistake 7: Shared and delegate mailboxes unmonitored.

    Symptom: billing@ was the launchpad, and no human "owned" it.
    Root cause: Shared mailboxes often fall outside per-user auditing and alerting assumptions.
    Fix: Inventory shared and delegate mailboxes; apply the same six detections to them.
    Prevention: Quarterly access review of delegates, with named owners for every mailbox.

    Mistake 8: Purging rules without evidence capture.

    Symptom: The responder deleted the malicious rule instantly, destroying the timeline evidence.
    Root cause: No "capture before clean" habit.
    Fix: Screenshot and export the rule, consent, and forwarding settings before removal.
    Prevention: Put evidence steps before cleanup steps in the playbook.

    Mistake 9: Log retention too short.

    Symptom: The hunt cannot go back past 30 days, but the fraud started 90 days ago.
    Root cause: Default retention kept, nobody priced the risk.
    Fix: Extend audit and sign-in log retention to at least one year in the SIEM.
    Prevention: Make retention an annual review item with the compliance owner.

    Mistake 10: No owner for mailbox alerts.

    Symptom: High-severity forwarding alerts sit unassigned for days.
    Root cause: Nobody owns "email security" as a product; alerts route to a shared queue.
    Fix: Assign an owner and escalation path per detection (rule, channel, and response-time target).
    Prevention: Include alert ownership in the quarterly detection review.

    Mistake 11: Trusting internal-looking mail by default.

    Symptom: Staff reply to a "colleague" whose display name was spoofed externally.
    Root cause: Internal relay and display-name matching bypassed anti-spoof checks.
    Fix: Enable impersonation protection and external banners; train users to check the address, not the name.
    Prevention: Test spoofing of executive names quarterly with a controlled exercise.

    Mistake 12: Treating Resource Development as low priority.

    Symptom: A taken mailbox used only for quiet reading gets closed as "informational".
    Root cause: Triage tiers map to impact only, not to attacker intent.
    Fix: Treat confirmed mailbox takeover as a P1 (the highest urgency ticket level) regardless of what the attacker did next.
    Prevention: Write the intent rule into the triage matrix: compromised accounts are always incidents.

    Habits that keep the detections honest

    Daily

    • Review new inbox rules and forwarding changes.
    • Check high-risk and impossible-travel sign-ins.
    • Review any new OAuth consent alerts.

    Weekly

    • Hunt bulk MailItemsAccessed outliers (E5).
    • Review external send-volume anomalies.
    • Verify alert routing fired to a human, not a void.

    Monthly

    • Review delegates, shared mailboxes, and app grants.
    • Check DMARC aggregate reports for spoof attempts.
    • Tune thresholds and allowlists; drop stale exceptions.

    Key facts

    Technique
    T1586.002 — Compromise Accounts: Email Accounts (Resource Development)
    One-line meaning
    An attacker takes a mailbox to read it, send as its owner, and reset other accounts through it
    Fastest signals
    New inbox rule with security keywords · external forwarding · high-risk sign-in · mail-scoped OAuth consent
    Main log source
    M365 unified audit log (o365:management:activity) + Entra sign-ins (ms:aad:signin)
    Licensing trap
    MailItemsAccessed and Send need Audit Premium (E5)

    Response checklist

    • Revoke sessions and refresh tokens for the account; force re-authentication everywhere.
    • Reset the password and re-enroll MFA with a phishing-resistant method.
    • Export and screenshot all inbox rules, forwarding settings, and delegates before deleting them.
    • Purge malicious inbox rules, forwarding rules, and transport rules tenant-wide.
    • Revoke OAuth grants for any app the user consented to during the compromise window.
    • Determine what was read (MailItemsAccessed, gateway logs) and what was sent.
    • Warn recipients of attacker-sent mail and ask finance to re-verify recent payment changes.
    • Check for reset chaining into banks, payroll, and personal accounts; advise the user privately.
    • Hunt the same patterns across all users; one mailbox is rarely the only one.
    • Document the timeline and notify stakeholders per your policy and legal duty.
    • Feed gaps found (licensing, tuning, MFA strength) into the mitigation plan in Section 7.
