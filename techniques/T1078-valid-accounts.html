T1078 – Valid Accounts

🎯Tactics: Initial Access · Persistence · Privilege Escalation · Defense Evasion 💻Platforms: Windows, Linux, macOS, Containers, ESXi, IaaS, SaaS, Office Suite, Identity Provider, Network Devices 🧩Sub-techniques: .001 Default · .002 Domain · .003 Local · .004 Cloud 🛠Complexity: Low 🔎Detection difficulty: High 📊Technique version: 3.0

Most people think hackers break in by finding software bugs. In reality, many just log in. Valid Accounts (T1078) means the attacker uses a real username and password, session token, or API key that belongs to someone else. The credentials may be stolen, guessed, bought from a criminal market, or left behind by a worker who left the company. Because the login uses the front door and a trusted identity, there is no exploit, no crash, and often no malware. That is why this technique is one of the hardest to catch. It is also one of the most reused: MITRE ATT&CK lists dozens of groups that use it, from APT29 and APT41 to LAPSUS$ and Scattered Spider. A famous example is the Colonial Pipeline attack of May 2021: criminals entered through one old VPN account with a leaked password and no second factor, and fuel stopped flowing on the US East Coast for days. Valid Accounts is rare in one more way: it appears in four tactics at once. The same login can give Initial Access, then Persistence, then Privilege Escalation, and it helps Defense Evasion too, because activity from a real user does not look like an attack. This guide shows the attack step by step, then gives you the logs, Sigma rules, and Splunk queries to catch it.

Think like the attacker

Red Team / Hacker Perspective

3.1 When attackers use this

Valid Accounts is not a single moment in an attack. Attackers reach for it at the very start, when they want a quiet way in, and again later, when they want to move up or hide. Criminal groups buy lists of leaked passwords in bulk and test them against company logins every night. Espionage groups such as APT29, APT28, and APT41 prefer valid accounts because long, quiet access is their goal. Ransomware groups such as Akira, BlackByte, Play, and INC Ransom use stolen VPN or RDP logins to get inside, and Volt Typhoon even keeps its persistence mostly with valid credentials instead of malware. The classic timing is a weekend or a holiday night, when nobody watches the login. The entry point is usually anything that accepts a username and password from the internet: VPN, remote desktop, Outlook Web Access and other webmail, SSO portals, cloud consoles, and network device SSH.

3.2 How attackers do it, step by step

  1. Collect credentials. They buy breach dumps on criminal markets, steal passwords with info-stealer malware, read them from public code repositories, or trick staff with phishing. LAPSUS$ and Scattered Spider became famous for simply calling the helpdesk and asking for a password or MFA reset.
  2. Prepare the target list. They harvest real usernames from LinkedIn, from company email format guessing, or with username enumeration against the login page. A leaked list only pays off if it matches the target's staff.
  3. Validate quietly. They run a password spray: one weak password, such as a season plus a year, against hundreds of usernames. Each user gets one or two tries, so no account locks. Then they rotate the password and repeat.
  4. Beat the second factor. Where MFA is on, they send push alerts again and again until someone approves (MFA fatigue), use a fake login page that steals the session cookie in real time, or talk the helpdesk into registering their own MFA device.
  5. Log in and look normal. They use the VPN, RDP, webmail, or SSO portal exactly like an employee. No exploit runs, so endpoint alarms stay silent. They check email rules, watch the network, and learn how the company works.
  6. Climb to more accounts. From the first machine they dump cached passwords and tokens, then reuse them on servers and admin consoles. One valid account becomes five. They may also create fresh accounts for persistence.
  7. Keep the door open. They add their own MFA device to the stolen account, register new OAuth app consents, or create cloud access keys, so they stay in even if the password changes.
  8. Finish the job. They stage and steal data, then often trigger ransomware with a valid admin login. The final blow usually looks like an administrator doing ordinary work.

3.3 Where it works best

🔑Legacy remote access

Old VPN concentrators and RDP gateways without MFA. Colonial Pipeline fell this way. Attackers scan for them every day.

☁Cloud and SaaS

M365, Google Workspace, AWS, and Azure consoles. A single valid cloud login can read every mailbox or delete every server.

👥Default and shared accounts

Appliances, printers, IoT, and service accounts that still use factory passwords. ATT&CK lists this as sub-technique .001.

🚪Dormant accounts

Accounts of people who left the company. Nobody types their password, so nobody complains when an attacker does.

🏢Helpdesk and identity providers

If the service desk resets passwords without strong checks, the front door is the phone line. Scattered Spider proved it at scale.

🧩Contractors and partners

Third-party logins are often weaker, shared, and barely monitored, yet they connect straight into company systems.

3.4 Why attackers like this technique

  • No bugs needed. A valid password beats the newest patch. Patched systems do not stop it, because the front door works exactly as designed.
  • It looks like business as usual. The logs show a real user doing a real login. Security tools tuned to find malware find nothing.
  • It carries four tactics. One stolen login is Initial Access, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, and Defense Evasion at the same time.
  • Cheap and scalable. Credential lists cost a few dollars. One spray run can test an entire company in minutes from a rented server.
  • It hides the attacker's skill level. Even a beginner with a purchased list can get the same access as a trained intruder.

3.5 What attackers need first

  • A source of credentials: breach dumps, info-stealer logs, phishing kits, or a friendly insider.
  • A list of real usernames for the target, or a guessable naming pattern such as first.last.
  • Basic tooling: a rented VPS, a spray script or checker, and a proxy service to hide their IP.
  • Patience for low-and-slow testing, so failed logins stay under lockout and alert limits.
  • Knowledge of the target's login pages: VPN portals, OWA, SSO, cloud consoles, and device SSH.
  • No exploit development skill is required — that is exactly why this technique is everywhere.

Ethics and law — read this first

Trying passwords against accounts or services you do not own is a crime in most countries under computer misuse and unauthorized-access laws. It can also break your employment contract and put you in prison. Red teams do this work only with written permission, a defined scope, and rules of engagement. If you are learning, use your own lab, your own tenant, or free legal ranges made for practice. If a real user account locks out during your test, that is a real person locked out of real work.

Think like the defender

Blue Team / SOC Perspective

4.1 What defenders can see

A valid login itself is normal. What is not normal is its context: where it comes from, when it happens, what it does next, and how often it fails first. A single event rarely proves anything. The skill is joining weak signals: a failed-login burst, then a success, then activity that does not fit the account's history. Modern identity providers help a lot — Azure AD and Okta score sign-in risk and record device, country, and MFA details on every attempt. Windows keeps exact records of who logged in, from where, and with which logon type. Cloud platforms record every console session and every new access key. None of these alone says "attacker", but together they draw a clear picture.

4.3 Where to look

  • Identity provider logs: Azure AD / Entra ID sign-in and audit logs, Okta system log, Google Workspace login audit.
  • Windows Security event log: 4624, 4625, 4648, 4672, 4688, 4720, 4728, 4732, 4740.
  • Sysmon: Event ID 10 for LSASS access, Event ID 1 for suspicious child processes after a remote login.
  • VPN and remote access logs: concentrator, RDP gateway, and firewall VPN records with source IP and geo data.
  • Cloud audit trails: AWS CloudTrail, Azure activity logs, GCP audit logs, M365 Unified Audit Log.
  • Appliance and network device logs: firewall admin logins, switch and router SSH sessions, default-account use.
  • Threat intelligence feeds: leaked-credential monitoring services and breach databases that match your domains.

4.4 Why this matters to the SOC

One stolen account is not one alert — it is a whole campaign waiting to happen. Because the same login covers Initial Access, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, and Defense Evasion, the blast radius (everything the attacker can reach through one identity) is the full power of that account. A standard user account can reach email, files, and internal apps. A service account or admin account can reach servers, backups, and the domain itself. Attacks built on valid accounts also create less noise: no exploit crashes, no malware detonations, so mean time to detect often stretches to weeks or months. The cost shows up later: exfiltrated data, encrypted systems, fraud, and long recovery. Detecting account abuse early is one of the highest-value jobs a SOC can do, because the same detections catch both cheap criminal sprays and patient espionage teams.

4.5 Where it fits in the attack chain

Before: attackers get credentials through Phishing (T1566), Brute Force and password spraying (T1110, especially .003), OS Credential Dumping on an already-lost host (T1003), Steal Web Session Cookie (T1539), or Multi-Factor Authentication Request Generation (T1621). During and after: the valid account powers Account Discovery (T1087), Permission Groups Discovery (T1069), Lateral Movement (moving from one machine to others) over remote services such as SSH and SMB (T1021), Data Staged (T1074) and Exfiltration (T1041), and Impact (T1486 ransomware, T1565 data destruction). It also maps to Persistence via Account Manipulation (T1098) and to Defense Evasion, because malicious actions come from a trusted identity. In practice, treat T1078 as the center of the attack: even when it starts elsewhere, most real intrusions pass through a valid account at some point.

5.2 Threat Intelligence

Where threat intel comes from

📰Open sources (OSINT)

Vendor blogs (Mandiant, Microsoft, Talos, Volexity), CISA and FBI advisories, abuse.ch, AlienVault OTX, Shodan, and VirusTotal. These tell you which credentials-stealing tools and spray infrastructures are active this month.

🎯ATT&CK-based intel

The MITRE group and software pages for T1078 list dozens of confirmed users: APT28, APT29, APT41, LAPSUS$, Scattered Spider, Volt Typhoon, Wizard Spider, Sandworm Team, Leviathan, Fox Kitten, and more. Each page links the original report.

💳Commercial feeds

Subscription intel gives curated actor profiles, leaked-credential databases matched to your domain, and early warning about dark web markets selling your staff's passwords.

🏠Internal telemetry

Your own IdP risk scores, EDR data, past incident tickets, and purple-team results. This is the intel that matches your real environment — often the most valuable and the most ignored.

The intel cycle for this technique

  1. Define priorities. Decide which account abuse scenarios hurt most: VPN sprays, cloud console takeover, dormant accounts, MFA fatigue. Rank them by business impact.
  2. Collect. Pull sign-in and audit logs from IdPs, Windows, VPN, cloud, and appliances. Add external feeds: new IOCs, spray infrastructure lists, breach dumps mentioning your domain.
  3. Process. Normalize everything into common fields: user, source IP, country, device, result, error code. Bad parsing kills good detections.
  4. Analyze. Compare actor behavior with your telemetry. If Volt Typhoon lives on valid accounts, do you even alert on first-use of an old VPN account?
  5. Produce. Turn findings into the Sigma rules of 5.5, hunt queries, and watchlists of high-value accounts.
  6. Feedback. Measure hits and false positives, tune thresholds, and feed lessons back into the priorities. The cycle repeats.

Threat #1 — Password spray across many users

An attacker picks one weak password, such as Spring2025!, and tries it against hundreds of usernames from one or a few source IPs. Each user gets only a few attempts, so lockouts never trigger, but the pattern is loud: one IP, many different users, the same error code. In Azure AD the tell is errorCode 50126 ("invalid username or password") repeated across distinct UserPrincipalNames. On-premises, the same story appears as Windows Event 4625 bursts.

Threat #2 — Impossible travel / anomalous sign-in that passes MFA

The attacker has working credentials and a way to satisfy the second factor (stolen token, real-time proxy page, or a helpful push approval). The sign-in succeeds — which is exactly why it slips past failure-based alerts. The pattern that betrays it is geometry and history: the same user signs in from two countries within minutes, or from a device and network that have never been seen for that account. Azure AD flags such sessions with identity-protection risk levels; on Windows servers, a remote logon type 10 from a new IP tells the same story.

Threat #3 — MFA fatigue / push bombing until approval

The attacker already has the correct password, so every login attempt stops at the MFA step. They press "send push" over and over — in Okta each press is a user.mfa.okta_verify.push event that ends in FAILURE while the phone buzzes nonstop. Eventually the tired user taps Approve, and the next push (or the same session) succeeds. The signature is volume: many unanswered or failed pushes for one user in a few minutes, followed by a success. In Azure AD the same story shows as repeated 500121 / 50158 MFA-denied errors before a success.

Threat #4 — Pass-the-hash / LSASS credential theft and account reuse

Once an attacker is inside one machine, they want more accounts. Windows keeps password hashes and tickets in the memory of the LSASS process (the Local Security Authority Subsystem Service, which checks logons on every Windows host). Tools that read LSASS memory, or that log on with a stolen hash instead of a password — called pass-the-hash — turn one valid account into many. Sysmon Event ID 10 records every process that opens another process's memory; normal software almost never touches lsass.exe, and when it does, the access rights (GrantedAccess) usually differ from what dumping tools request.

  • CallTrace containing UNKNOWN(...) or unsigned module addresses — injected or unsigned code inside the caller.
  • Windows pairing: 4624 with LogonType 9 (NewCredentials) or NTLM logons for accounts that normally use Kerberos.
  • Aftermath: the dumped account signs in to hosts it never touched before — join Sysmon 10 with later 4624s.
Threat #5 — Dormant or retired account suddenly active

When people leave the company, their accounts sometimes stay enabled for months. Attackers love this: they buy or leak old credentials, log in, and nobody feels the double-push or the password change — the real owner is gone. The detection is a history question: this account existed for two years, and then it logged in yesterday. Windows Event 4624 shows the logon with its source IP and type; the Microsoft 365 Unified Audit Log shows the same through cloud logins. Both need your HR leaver list (or an activity baseline) joined against live logons.

Threat #6 — Cloud console / session abuse: new keys, ConsoleLogin without MFA

Cloud accounts are accounts too — sub-technique .004. An attacker who has a stolen cloud password or hijacked session cookie signs into the web console or mints long-lived access keys (username-plus-secret pairs that programs use to call the cloud API). Threat #7 — Default or unchanged credentials on appliances and devices

Firewalls, printers, cameras, ESXi hosts, and IoT gadgets ship with factory passwords like admin/admin. Many are never changed, or the change was skipped during a rushed install (sub-technique .001). Attackers scan the internet for these logins — APT28 famously used default passwords on VOIP phones and printers as an entry path. The log below is a FortiGate admin login event in its native key=value form. The same shape exists on almost every appliance: a login action, a user, a success status, a source IP.

  • Inventory cross-check: every device should have an owner; a login from a device you cannot map is itself a finding.
Threat #8 — Rogue account creation plus group escalation, then use

To survive a password reset, attackers often create their own account and quietly add it to privileged groups. Windows records each step: Event 4720 (account created), 4728/4732/4756 (added to global/local/universal security groups), and later 4624 logons with that account. A new account created by an admin at 03:00 that joins "Domain Admins" twelve minutes later is not subtle — but you must be watching these three events together. This is also how the rogue account gets used for Persistence and Privilege Escalation in one move.

references:
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1078/
    - https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1110/003/
author: Detection Guide Team

Learn from a real incident

Real-World Case Study

The incident

On the morning of Friday, 7 May 2021, an employee at Colonial Pipeline, the company that runs the largest fuel pipeline in the United States, found a ransom note on a control-room computer. Overnight, the DarkSide ransomware group — an affiliate using DarkSide's ransomware-as-a-service — had encrypted corporate Windows systems. Colonial made the rare and costly decision to shut down the entire 5,500-mile pipeline itself, fearing the attackers could also reach its operational systems. Fuel stopped flowing to the US East Coast for nearly a week. The case became the defining example of a "boring" initial access vector: the criminals did not hack in. They logged in.

Mandiant, the incident response firm brought in on day one, later reported that the intruders had entered days earlier through a legacy VPN account. The account had no multi-factor authentication, and its password had appeared in a previous breach dump — the exact combination this guide's Threats T2 and T5 describe. The attackers exfiltrated roughly 100 GB of data before encryption. Colonial paid about 75 bitcoin (around $4.4 million at the time); in June 2021 the US Department of Justice announced it had recovered 63.7 of those bitcoin (about $2.3 million) from the attackers' wallet.

How the attack chain worked

  1. Valid Accounts (T1078): the affiliate authenticated to the legacy VPN with a former employee's leaked username and password. No exploit, no malware, no MFA in the way.
  2. Discovery and credential access: from inside the corporate network, they mapped systems and harvested additional credentials to widen access.
  3. Exfiltration: they staged about 100 GB of corporate data and sent it out — leverage for double extortion (encrypt systems and also threaten to publish the stolen data).
  4. Impact (T1486): DarkSide ransomware encrypted corporate Windows systems and left ransom notes. The OT side was never proven breached, but the fear of it shut the pipeline down.

How it was detected

Not by a fancy rule. The first signal was a human finding a ransom note at ~05:00, alongside a VPN sign-in alarm that same morning. Only afterwards did forensics reconstruct the earlier, quiet entry: a valid VPN logon for an account whose owner had left, from attacker infrastructure, on a portal without MFA. Every ingredient of the detections in Section 5 existed in the environment — the logon event, the stale account, the missing second factor — but nothing joined them before Day 0.

Containment

  1. Shut the pipeline and large parts of IT to protect OT — a business continuity decision, not a forensic one.
  2. Isolate and rebuild affected corporate systems; engage Mandiant for the investigation.
  3. Revoke and reset credentials, kill attacker access paths, and add MFA to remote access.
  4. Share indicators with CISA and the FBI, which pushed protective guidance to the whole sector.

Recovery

  1. Restore systems from clean backups and rebuild trust in the corporate environment before reconnecting it to operations.
  2. Restart the pipeline in sections starting 12 May; verify product quality and pressure as lines returned.
  3. Resume normal delivery within about a week; keep enhanced monitoring and manual procedures in place during stabilization.
  4. Handle the ransom: ~75 BTC (~$4.4M) paid; DOJ recovered 63.7 BTC (~$2.3M) in June 2021.

  • Incident response lead (Mandiant): ran forensics, traced the VPN entry, and published the finding that the account lacked MFA and its password was from a breach dump.
  • Threat intel: tied the payload to DarkSide's affiliate model; later helped explain the group's collapse and rebrand attempts.

Lessons learned

  • Put MFA on every remote access path — especially legacy VPNs that predate your identity program. This single control would very likely have stopped the intrusion.
  • Decommission accounts on exit day: an account belonging to a former employee should not accept logins months later. Automate offboarding with an SLA.
  • Check breach dumps for your own domain: the password was already public. Leaked-credential monitoring would have forced an early reset.
  • Segment IT and OT: the shutdown was voluntary, but only network segregation made it possible to choose safety without gambling.
  • Alert on the quiet signals: a never-before source IP logging into a dormant account is Threat T5 — build the join before the incident, not after.
  • Plan for manual operations: the pipeline ran by hand during the outage; that resilience saved the recovery.
  • Exercises beat assumptions: tabletop the exact chain — leaked credential → VPN → lateral movement → ransomware — with IT, OT, legal, and comms in the room.
Why this case belongs to T1078

No zero-day was used. No exploit crashed a server. The entire national incident started because one valid username and password still worked on one unmonitored portal. That is the whole message of Valid Accounts: identity is the new perimeter, and every stale, default, or unwatched credential is an open door with your name on it. The detections in Section 5 and the mitigations in Section 7 exist precisely to make this attack chain loud and expensive.

7
Layered defenses that work

Mitigation & Defense

Defense in depth

No single control stops credential abuse. The goal is layers: make stolen passwords hard to use, make unused accounts disappear, and make every login observable. ATT&CK's official mitigations for T1078 — M1032 Multi-factor Authentication, M1013 Application Developer Guidance, M1015 Active Directory Configuration, M1017 User Training, M1018 User Account Management, M1026 Privileged Account Management, M1027 Password Policies, and M1036 Account Use Policies — map almost one-to-one onto the layers below.

Enterprise defenses

🔐Access control & MFA (M1032, M1036)

Put phishing-resistant MFA on every login — VPN, cloud console, email, devices. Use conditional access policies (IdP rules that check device health, location, and risk before granting access) and number matching in push approvals so MFA-fatigue storms fail.

♻Identity lifecycle (M1018)

Disable accounts the day someone leaves, expire unused credentials automatically, and audit monthly for dormant or never-used accounts. The Colonial Pipeline door was an account that should not have existed.

👑Privileged account management (M1026, M1027)

Separate admin from daily accounts, use a PAM (privileged access management) vault with just-in-time elevation, and never let service accounts log in interactively. Tier administration so one password cannot reach everything.

🧱Directory hardening (M1015, M1027)

Disable legacy authentication protocols that cannot do MFA (POP, IMAP, basic SMTP, NTLM where possible), enforce password managers and long passphrases, and use Kerberos-based, credential-guard-protected Windows so LSASS dumping yields little.

📡Monitoring & detection

Ship IdP, Windows, VPN, cloud, and appliance logs to your SIEM and deploy the Sigma rules from 5.5. Add UEBA baselines so "this account never logs in at 03:00" becomes computable, and alert on new MFA device enrollments.

👥User training (M1017)

Train everyone: staff to report unexpected MFA pushes instead of approving them, and helpdesk staff to verify identity before resets with callbacks and manager approval — the Scattered Spider defense.

🌐Third-party & vendor accounts

Vendor and contractor logins get their own MFA, scoped rights, and expiry dates. Monitor them like employee accounts — attackers buy these credentials on the same markets.

🧯Response readiness

Know how to force global sign-out, disable an account everywhere in minutes, and restore identity services. Pre-write the runbook: who can kill a session in the IdP, in M365, and in AWS.

Home user defenses

  • Use a password manager so every site has a unique password — one breach then cannot open your other doors.
  • Turn on MFA everywhere, and prefer an authenticator app or security key over SMS codes.
  • Never approve an MFA push you did not start. Decline it, change the password, and report it.
  • Check your email addresses at haveibeenpwned.com and change any password that appears in a leak.
  • Change default passwords on your router, cameras, and smart devices the day you install them.
  • Keep phones and computers updated; info-stealer malware loves old browsers.
  • Treat helpdesk calls and "urgent" password-reset emails with the same suspicion as phishing links.
  • If a service offers passkeys (passwordless login), take it — nothing to steal, nothing to spray.

Best practices for day-to-day SOC work

  1. Write hypotheses, then hunt. Example: "If credentials were dumped on a workstation, that account will log into a server it never touched." Turn each hypothesis into a scheduled search.
  2. Purple-team the rules from 5.5. Have a red teammate (in an authorized exercise) spray a test tenant, push-spam a test account, and use a dump tool in a lab VM — then confirm each rule fires and pages the right person.
  3. Build the account-context lookup. Join employment status, admin group membership, and device ownership so an analyst sees "former employee, has VPN access" instantly.
  4. Track MTTD for identity incidents. Measure how long a planted stale-account test login survives. Improve the number every quarter.
  5. Feed findings back to engineering. Every true positive should end with a control change — MFA here, offboarding fix there — not just a closed ticket.
  6. Document what "normal" means. Keep a living note of expected logon patterns per account group; it makes tuning fast and defensible during audits.
  7. Rehearse the kill switch. Twice a year, practice disabling an account and forcing global sign-out across IdP, email, and cloud in under 15 minutes.
Keep going

Valid Accounts will never fully disappear, but it can stop being invisible. Layer the mitigations from Section 7 so stolen passwords stop working, and run the detections from Section 5 so that when someone still gets in, the SOC sees it in minutes instead of weeks. Do those two things consistently and the attacker's cheapest weapon becomes their most expensive one.
