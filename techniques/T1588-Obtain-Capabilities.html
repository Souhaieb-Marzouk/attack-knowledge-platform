T1588 Obtain Capabilities

Before an attack starts, the attacker needs weapons. Instead of building them, adversaries often buy, download, or steal malware, tools, exploits, and certificates. This guide explains the technique in plain language, shows an animated example, and gives defenders practical detections, Sigma rules, and Splunk queries.

Overview

What this technique is, why it matters to defenders, and where it sits in the attack chain.

What it is

Obtain Capabilities covers the way attackers collect the tools of an attack before they use them. A capability is any ready-made asset that helps an intrusion: malware, hacking tools, code signing certificates, website certificates, working exploits, or knowledge about unpatched weaknesses.

The attacker has three main ways to get these assets. They can buy them on criminal marketplaces, download free or open-source software, or steal them from other criminals, from vendors, or from security companies. The result is the same: the attacker starts the attack better armed and with less work done in the open.

Why it matters

Modern attacks are often assembled, not written. A ransomware crew can rent the malware, buy access tools, and reuse a stolen certificate. This makes attacks faster, cheaper, and harder to link to one actor, because many groups share the same purchased parts.

For defenders this changes the focus. You will rarely see the purchase. You will see the effects: a fresh certificate for a lookalike domain, a binary signed with a strange certificate, or a tool hash that threat intelligence already knows. Learning to spot these echoes is the core skill this page teaches.

Where it fits

This technique lives in the Resource Development tactic (TA0042), the phase before any contact with the target. It sits next to its sibling techniques: T1583 (set up infrastructure), T1584 (hijack infrastructure), and T1587 (build capabilities).

After the capability is ready, it flows into the attack chain: initial access, execution, and later stages. Detection engineers treat T1588 as a pre-positioning signal. It rarely triggers an alert on its own, but it decides what you should hunt for tomorrow.

    The same story in words

    This written version covers exactly the same eight steps as the animation. Read it if you prefer text, use it with a screen reader, or quote it in a training session.

    Step 1

    Planning the campaign

    A threat actor picks a target industry and lists what the attack needs: a remote access trojan, a lateral movement tool, and a valid code signing certificate so files look trusted. No packet has touched the victim yet, so the defender sees nothing.

    Step 2

    Buying malware on a criminal market

    The actor pays in cryptocurrency for a subscription to a malware-as-a-service stealer. The seller gives a builder panel and support. Vendors later report that groups such as LAPSUS$ used the same stealer, which is how the family enters threat intelligence.

    Step 3

    Buying a stolen code signing certificate

    The actor buys a code signing certificate that was stolen from a small software company. Signing malware with it removes one warning dialog and raises trust. This single purchase shapes the best detection opportunities later, because the certificate identity becomes a fixed marker.

    Step 4

    Setting up staging infrastructure

    The actor rents one virtual server, uploads the toolkit, and requests a free TLS certificate for a fresh lookalike domain. At this moment the defender CAN see something: certificate transparency logs publish the new certificate within minutes. This is the earliest reliable signal in the whole chain.

    Step 5

    Testing the capability

    The actor runs the stealer on a clean virtual machine and confirms the beacon works. Sandboxed uploads of the sample reach malware repositories, so researchers can now assign a hash and publish a report. The sample leaves the private world of the attacker.

    Step 6

    Delivering to the target

    The actor sends phishing mail with a link to the staging server. The payload arrives correctly signed, so the mail security gateway sees a valid signature but an unknown sender reputation. One weak signal appears, but nothing stops the message yet.

    Step 7

    First beacon from the victim

    One user opens the attachment. The implant calls the staging server over HTTPS. The proxy log now holds a connection to a domain that is only days old. The certificate transparency alert from step 4 and this connection now form a matched pair.

    Step 8

    The SOC joins the dots

    A threat intelligence alert ties the hash, the certificate, and the domain together. The SOC starts a hunt with the certificate identity as the pivot and finds the infected host within one day. The lesson: each event alone looked weak, but correlation across intelligence and telemetry turned noise into a case.

    How to read the animation

    Red flows are attacker actions. Amber flows carry the capability or payload itself. Blue elements belong to the defender side. The panel on the right of the canvas lists what a security operations center (SOC) could see at that exact moment, including the steps where the honest answer is nothing.

    What is realistic here

    This story is a composite of public reporting on purchased malware and stolen certificates. The prices, forums, and timings are simplified for teaching. The important part is the shape: capability acquisition happens early, mostly out of sight, and the best detections appear when the capability first touches the internet or the target.

    3

    Red Team / Hacker Perspective

    Why attackers prefer buying over building, how the acquisition is done in principle, and where the process leaks traces.

    ?Ethics and scope

    This section explains attacker behavior so defenders can detect it. It stays at the level of concepts and public reporting. It gives no operational instructions for attacking real systems. Any hands-on practice belongs in an isolated lab you own, with fake data, and clearly labeled as lab-only.

    An attacker chooses the cheapest path to a working capability. Writing a reliable implant takes skilled people and weeks of work. Renting one costs a few hundred dollars and works the same evening. Market research in vendor reporting keeps showing the same economics: malware-as-a-service subscriptions, one-off tool sales, and access brokers dominate because they cut time and skill requirements at once.

    Buying also gives the attacker deniability — it becomes harder to say who did the attack. When twenty crews use the same stealer, defenders and law enforcement have a harder time linking an incident to one group. That is why even capable state-linked teams use commercial tooling: MuddyWater has used publicly available malware to blend in, and the ATT&CK entry for T1588.002 lists tool reuse by Turla, Inception, and many others.

    Acquisition channels

    Criminal marketplaces and forums

    Dedicated markets sell builders, loaders, botnets, and credentials, usually for cryptocurrency. Reputations and escrow make them work like normal e-commerce. Buyers test samples before use, which is exactly the step that sometimes leaks a sample to researchers.

    Malware-as-a-service (MaaS)

    The seller operates the malware and the buyer rents it, often with a web panel for building payloads and reading stolen data. This model spreads one malware family across dozens of crews and produces the shared artifacts that intelligence teams track.

    Free and open-source software

    Legitimate security tools, open-source implant frameworks, and public proof-of-concept code fill many needs for free. ATT&CK documents tool acquisition by dozens of groups. The trade-off is that defenders know these tools very well.

    Stealing from others

    Attackers steal capabilities from vendors, from other criminals, and even from security companies. Public examples include stolen code signing certificates, leaked exploit frameworks, and the theft of red team tools from a security firm in 2020, which later appeared in criminal hands.

    Conditions and preparation

    Acquisition has prerequisites of its own. The actor needs a payment method that survives law enforcement takedowns, usually cryptocurrency. It needs infrastructure to test and host what it bought. And it needs a minimum of skill to wire the parts together: purchased malware still needs delivery, configuration, and a place in the attack chain. Groups with fewer skills buy more complete packages; capable groups buy components and assemble bespoke attacks.

    iLab-only practice example

    A safe classroom exercise looks like this: in an offline lab, download a well-known open-source security tool from its official repository, run it against a deliberately vulnerable test machine you own, and record every artifact it creates on disk and the network. You are simulating what a blue team sees after an attacker uses an obtained tool, without touching any real target. Label the exercise and the lab network clearly.

    Variations and common mistakes attackers make

    VariationWhat changesTrace it leaves
    Buy or subscribeFast, supported, shared with other crewsFamily signatures, panel infrastructure, watermark strings
    Download free toolingNo seller, but well-known to defendersKnown hashes, default configurations, public signatures
    Steal certificates or codeHigh trust, but the victim may notice and revokeCertificate identity, revocation events, victim-side intrusion logs
    Test in rented cloudCheap scalable stagingNew cloud accounts, bulk TLS certificate issuance, scan traffic

    Mistakes that expose the acquisition

    Sellers water down trust by reselling the same samples, so hashes reach repositories early. Buyers skip changing default ports, default certificates, or watermark strings, so the shared family signature survives. Staging servers get certificates on lookalike domains and land in certificate transparency logs minutes later. Reused infrastructure across campaigns lets analysts link two incidents that the buyer believed were separate. Each mistake is a pivot for the defender.

    4

    Blue Team / SOC Perspective

    What the technique looks like from the inside, which artifacts survive, and a short triage flow your team can follow.

    One honest fact first: the purchase itself happens on servers you cannot see, so no SIEM alert fires when money changes hands. MITRE itself notes that most of this activity stays outside the visibility of the target organization. Good teams therefore work on two layers. The first layer is intelligence: you learn that a capability exists before it ever reaches you. The second layer is telemetry: you detect the capability the moment it touches your endpoints, your mail, or your network.

    Artifacts the technique leaves behind

    Certificate artifacts

    Newly issued TLS certificates for lookalike domains appear in certificate transparency (CT) logs within minutes. Stolen code signing certificates carry a fixed issuer and subject that you can inventory and hunt for across the estate.

    File artifacts

    Purchased malware and tools eventually land on disk: installers in download folders, staged archives in temp directories, or binaries signed by an unexpected certificate. Hashes of shared samples accumulate in public repositories.

    Network artifacts

    Staging infrastructure needs DNS names, TLS handshakes, and beacon traffic. Fresh domains, certificate mismatches, and default tool fingerprints such as known TLS client signatures all show up in proxy, DNS, and firewall logs.

    Where to look

    SourceWhat it showsWhy it helps
    Certificate transparency logsEvery public TLS certificate issuedEarliest public trace of staging and phishing infrastructure
    Threat intelligence feedsHashes, families, actor reports, CISA advisoriesTells you which purchased capabilities are active in your sector
    Malware repositoriesSamples, signatures, YARA hitsLets you hunt for a family before and after it hits you
    EDR telemetryFile events, signatures, module loadsConfirms whether a known capability reached an endpoint
    Proxy, DNS, firewall logsContact to staging and C2 infrastructureConnects endpoints to the fresh domains and certificates
    Code signing inventoryEvery signer trusted in the estateMakes an unknown or abused signer stand out immediately

    Why this matters for the attack chain

    Catching a capability early collapses the rest of the chain. A stealer blocked at delivery never reaches credential theft. A beacon that never connects means no lateral movement. That is why the Resource Development tactic, although pre-compromise, has real detection value: it defines the exact markers your downstream detections should expect. Teams that track families and certificates see attacks days earlier than teams that only watch endpoints.

    Short triage flow

    1. Receive or find the marker. A TI report, a CT log alert, or an EDR match gives you a hash, a certificate identity, or a domain.
    2. Enrich it. Check the hash in malware repositories, the domain in registration databases, the certificate in CT logs. Note the age and the overlap with your industry.
    3. Check exposure. Search your own telemetry for the marker over the last 90 days: proxy, DNS, EDR, mail gateway. One query per source, saved as a scheduled hunt.
    4. Validate hits. For each hit, decide benign or malicious using the full context: file path, signer, process ancestry, user, timing. Never trust a hash alone for blocking decisions without context.
    5. Escalate or document. Real hits become an incident with scope, containment, and eradication steps. No hits become an intelligence note with the next review date, so the hunt stays alive.

    ✓Good practice

    Turn every credible public report into one scheduled hunt plus one saved query, and record the owner and the review date. Detection coverage for T1588 is built from many small, maintained hunts, not from one perfect rule.

    iWhat ATT&CK officially says

    MITRE rates direct detection of T1588 as difficult and lists the mitigation Pre-compromise [M1056], because the buying happens outside your boundary. The official detection strategy DET0850 — Detection of Obtain Capabilities gives one main analytic, AN1982: use services that track newly issued certificates, and certificates in use on internet sites. The sub-technique pages add more analytics — for example AN1977 (monitor contextual data about malicious payloads: compilation metadata, hashes, watermarks) and AN2007 (analyze code signing certificates by thumbprint, algorithm, validity period, common name, and issuing authority). For the exploits, vulnerabilities, and AI sub-techniques, MITRE is honest that most activity stays invisible until it is used.

    Read the official T1588 page for the full strategy text. The rest of section 5 turns these official hints into concrete rules.

    Confidence levels

    Every item you ingest needs a confidence level, or your alerts will argue with each other. Use three levels and write them into the detection metadata.

    LevelMeaningTypical source qualityHow you act
    HighMultiple independent reports, or observed in your own telemetryTwo vendors plus a repository sampleBuild a detection within days, treat hits as incidents
    MediumOne credible report or partial corroborationSingle vendor blog with technical detailSchedule a hunt, watch for corroboration

    2. Analyze each item: which T1588 sub-technique does it touch, which platforms, which sectors, what confidence.
    3. Apply by turning markers (hashes, certificate identities, domains) into hunts and detections, then link each detection back to the intelligence item.
    4. Measure quarterly: which intelligence items produced detections, which detections fired, and which feeds never produced anything useful. Drop weak feeds, keep strong ones.

    5.3Threats ranking and categorization

    The six threats below are the realistic ways this technique turns into risk for an organization. They are ranked and categorized by platform. The same six threats are used in 5.4 and 5.5, so the section stays practical.

    ThreatRankCategoryOne-line summary
    TH-1HighEndpoint · WindowsPurchased malware or tooling staged on a workstation from web or mail delivery
    TH-2HighNetwork · IntelCapability staging on freshly registered domains with new TLS certificates
    TH-3MediumNetwork · EndpointContact to threat-intelligence-listed staging or panel infrastructure
    TH-4MediumCloud · IdentityFraudulent cloud and AI service accounts used to build or test capabilities
    TH-5HighEndpoint · WindowsPayload signed with an abused or stolen code signing certificate
    TH-6CriticalNetwork · CloudObtained exploit aimed at an internet-facing edge appliance

    High TH-1 purchased capability staged on an endpoint

    Description. An employee receives or downloads a purchased loader, stealer, or hacktool. The file lands in a download or temp path and waits for execution. Preconditions. User interaction or a vulnerable downloader; attacker already bought the capability. Impact. Credential theft, ransomware staging, or full workstation compromise. Justification. The most common real-world outcome of acquisition, seen across many actor reports, and reliably detectable from file events and signatures, so a High rank with good detection odds.

    High TH-2 staging on fresh domains with new certificates

    Description. The attacker registers a lookalike domain, gets a free TLS certificate, and hosts the payload or a phishing page. Preconditions. Cheap registrar access and free certificate authorities; nothing on your side. Impact. Trusted-looking delivery infrastructure for the purchased capability. Justification. Extremely common in public reporting and visible early in certificate transparency logs, which makes it High even though it needs log enrichment to alert well.

    Medium TH-3 contact to listed staging infrastructure

    Description. A host in your network talks to an IP or domain that intelligence already flagged as a seller panel or staging server. Preconditions. The capability is already inside, or a user reached a scam page. Impact. Confirms active compromise or exposes users interacting with criminal infrastructure. Justification. High value when it fires but it depends fully on feed quality, so a Medium rank with low false-positive volume.

    Medium TH-4 fraudulent cloud and AI service accounts

    Description. Attackers open cloud or AI platform accounts with stolen payment data to build, obfuscate, or test capabilities, or abuse trial credits. Preconditions. Payment fraud and identity reuse; your exposure is indirect. Impact. Better armed attackers; sometimes abuse of your own tenant via social engineering into AI services. Justification. Growing fast in 2025-2026 reporting, but harder to see from inside the target, so Medium.

    High TH-5 payload signed with an abused certificate

    Description. The attacker signs a purchased payload with a stolen or fraudulently bought code signing certificate, or with a paid signing service that does not check buyers. The file shows a valid signature chain, which lowers endpoint suspicion. Preconditions. A stolen key, an impostor company registration, or an abusive signing service. Impact. Malware that passes casual signature checks and even some application control lists. Justification. Repeatedly documented, from Stuxnet in 2010 to recent loader families. The signature identity is stable, so a signer inventory plus reputation turns this into a reliable High-value detection.

    Critical TH-6 obtained exploit against an edge appliance

    Description. The attacker obtains a working exploit for an internet-facing device, such as a VPN gateway or file transfer appliance, and runs it before a patch is in place. Preconditions. The flaw is real, a working exploit exists, and the asset is exposed. Impact. Direct foothold, often with device-level credentials and a path to identity attacks. Justification. This is how many of the most damaging 2023-2026 incidents started. Ranked Critical because a single hit usually means active compromise, not preparation.

    5.4Logs and patterns

    For each threat: where to look, which fields matter, what normal looks like, what suspicious looks like, and one realistic event-shaped example. Field names follow the most common logging formats; adjust them to your stack.

    TH-1 — purchased capability staged on an endpoint

    Log sources
    Sysmon Event ID 11 (file create) and 1 (process create); EDR file events; mail gateway logs
    Key fields
    UtcTime, Image, TargetFilename, Hashes, User, ProcessGuid
    Normal behavior
    Executables appear in Program Files, managed software folders, or signed installers from known vendors
    Suspicious behavior
    Executable written to user-writable temp paths, double extensions, or an unsigned binary in an archive staged by the browser or mail client
    Detection logic
    Executable file creation in user temp or download paths, combined with a known-bad hash list or an unexpected signature. (Tip: some SIEM add-ons add an is_executable enrichment field to Sysmon data. That field comes from the add-on, not from Sysmon itself, so treat it as optional.)

    TH-2 — staging on fresh domains with new certificates

    Log sources
    Certificate transparency streams (crt.sh, CertStream); proxy logs; DNS logs; passive DNS
    Key fields
    common_name, issuer, not_before, domain, domain_age_days, resp_mime_types, action
    Normal behavior
    Your own brand gets certificates from a few known CAs on known domains; users visit established domains
    Suspicious behavior
    A certificate issued yesterday for a domain containing your brand plus extra words; executable downloads from domains younger than 30 days
    Detection logic
    CT stream match on brand keywords joined to proxy records showing users reaching that domain, or executable MIME from a young domain

    TH-3 — contact to listed staging infrastructure

    Log sources
    Sysmon Event ID 3 (network connection); firewall and next-gen firewall logs; Suricata or Zeek records
    Key fields
    DestinationIp, DestinationPort, DestinationHostname, rule_category, alert_signature, src_ip
    Normal behavior
    Business traffic to known SaaS ranges; occasional flagged IPs that belong to documented internal services
    Suspicious behavior
    Repeated connections to an IP on a TI watchlist for seller panels, especially from servers or non-user endpoints
    Detection logic
    Destination IP in TI watchlist, enriched with feed confidence, matched against connection logs from all endpoints

    The IP in the example uses the reserved documentation range 203.0.113.0/24, which is safe for teaching material and must be replaced by your real watchlist in production.

    TH-4 — fraudulent cloud and AI service accounts

    Log sources
    AWS CloudTrail; Azure sign-in and audit logs; SaaS admin logs; proxy logs to AI service domains
    Key fields
    eventName, sourceIPAddress, userIdentity.type, MFAUsed, user_agent, account age
    Normal behavior
    Console logins from corporate ranges with MFA; AI service usage from documented teams and apps
    Suspicious behavior
    Console login without MFA from a hosting provider range; sudden AI API usage from a service account that never used it; uploads of code archives to AI endpoints
    Detection logic
    ConsoleLogin success where MFAUsed is No and the source IP is not in corporate ranges, or new AI service usage by a dormant identity

    TH-5 — payload signed with an abused certificate

    Log sources
    Sysmon Event ID 7 (image load) and 6 (driver load); EDR signature fields; code signing inventory lookups
    Key fields
    Image, Signed, Signature, SignatureStatus, Hashes, Company, User
    Normal behavior
    Signers match the approved publisher inventory; SignatureStatus is Valid for known vendors
    Suspicious behavior
    A valid signature from a publisher that is not in your inventory, or a subject that intelligence has linked to abused or stolen certificates
    Detection logic
    Signed equals true, and the Signature value sits on a threat intelligence signer watchlist or fails the publisher inventory check

    The publisher name in this example is synthetic, and the file path continues the TH-1 story: the staged file now runs, and its valid signature comes from a publisher nobody in your estate has ever shipped. In production the watchlist values come from your threat intelligence platform, not from a fixed list in the rule.

    TH-6 — obtained exploit against an edge appliance

    Log sources
    Suricata or Zeek alerts at the perimeter; appliance web logs; WAF logs; CISA KEV catalog as enrichment
    Key fields
    event_type, alert.category, alert.signature, src_ip, dest_ip, http.url, http.http_user_agent
    Normal behavior
    Low, steady volumes of administrative logins from corporate and VPN ranges; scanner noise filtered at the IDS
    Suspicious behavior
    An exploit-class alert aimed at an edge appliance address, especially a signature family that matches a flaw listed in the CISA KEV catalog
    Detection logic
    Alert category attempted-admin or web-application-attack, destination inside your edge address space, source not on the authorized testing list

    Both addresses are reserved documentation ranges: the source stands in for the attacker and the destination stands in for your edge appliance. The signature name follows the style of the Emerging Threats ruleset but is written for teaching, without naming a real CVE. In production, pair each fired signature with its KEV entry so the ticket carries the official context.

    5.5Sigma rules and Splunk SPL

    One Sigma rule and one matching Splunk query per threat. The Sigma logic and the SPL logic match by design. Replace index names, watchlist contents, and hash lists with your own values before deploying, and record the mapping from rule to threat in your detection inventory.

    TH-1 — Sigma: suspicious executable staged in user paths

    SPL for TH-1. Assumes Sysmon data lands in index=sysmon with sourcetype=XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational; adjust to your environment.

    TH-2 — Sigma: executable download from young or high-risk domain

    The custom field domain_age_days must come from an enrichment lookup of your domain registration or passive DNS data. If you do not enrich, keep only the TLD selection. Assumes proxy data in index=proxy with sourcetype=stream:http plus a lookup domain_intel carrying the age field.

    TH-3 — Sigma: connection to listed staging infrastructure

    SPL for TH-3. Assumes Sysmon in index=sysmon and a KV-store lookup ti_staging_ips with columns ip and confidence.

    TH-4 — Sigma: cloud console login without MFA from new infrastructure

    SPL for TH-4. Assumes CloudTrail in index=aws with sourcetype=aws:cloudtrail. The example uses the reserved documentation ranges 198.51.100.0/24 and 203.0.113.0/24 as the "outside corporate ranges" selection, exactly like the Sigma rule above; swap them for your real out-of-range logic (for example NOT cidrmatch("10.0.0.0/8", sourceIPAddress) over your corporate CIDRs).

    TH-5 — Sigma: image loaded with a watchlisted signer

    SPL for TH-5. Assumes Sysmon in index=sysmon. In production, replace the fixed list with a lookup such as ti_signers that carries the subject, the confidence, and a review date.

    TH-6 — Sigma: exploit-class IDS alert against the edge

    SPL for TH-6. Assumes EVE JSON from Suricata lands in index=ids with sourcetype=suricata (for example through the Splunk add-on for Suricata). Replace 203.0.113.0/24 with your real edge ranges and 10.99.0.0/16 with your testing network.

    Tuning guidance

    Start every rule in search-only mode for two weeks and review hits with the data owner. Add allowlists for your software deployment paths and break-glass accounts instead of lowering severity. For TH-2 keep the TLD list short and local; for TH-3 refresh the watchlist monthly with feed confidence; for TH-1 review the top downloaded legitimate installers and allowlist them explicitly; for TH-5 keep the signer list in a lookup with confidence and review dates; for TH-6 join fired signatures with the CISA KEV catalog so tickets carry the official context.

    Validation in the lab

    Test each rule in a lab before production: trigger the event once with a harmless action, such as renaming a text file to end with .hta in your own Downloads folder for TH-1, and confirm the rule fires with the right fields. Keep the test steps in the rule ticket so the next engineer can re-run them. The example IP ranges in TH-3 and TH-4 are reserved documentation ranges, safe for demos.

    Real-World Case Study

    Anatomy of a bought attack: eight documented events that show every face of capability acquisition, from stolen certificates to deepfake video calls.

    iHow to read this case

    This is a composite case. Every event below is real and publicly documented, but they happened to different victims at different times. We line them up in one story to show the full anatomy of Obtain Capabilities: what was bought or stolen, how it was used, and what defenders could see. Each timeline item names its public source.

    Context

    No single intrusion covers all seven sub-techniques. But across the last fifteen years, the same pattern repeats: an actor needs a capability, finds a seller or a victim to steal from, pays or raids, and then runs the capability against a target. The events below were chosen because each one documents a different acquisition path, and each one left public traces that defenders still study today.

    June 2010

    Stuxnet runs on stolen signing certificates

    The Stuxnet worm signed its driver files with valid certificates stolen from the hardware vendors Realtek and JMicron, so Windows trusted the files at the highest level. Security vendors published the certificate details, and the private keys were revoked. This is T1588.003 in its purest form: steal the signing capability. Detection point: a driver signed by a vendor that never shipped it.

    March 2017

    Microsoft patches the flaw before the leak

    Microsoft released security bulletin MS17-010 on 14 March 2017, fixing a remote code execution flaw in the SMBv1 protocol used for file sharing on Windows networks. The patch existed weeks before most organizations applied it. This is the precondition every obtained exploit hopes for: a real flaw and a slow patch cycle.

    April 2017

    A leak hands working exploits to everyone

    The group calling itself the Shadow Brokers published stolen exploit tools, including EternalBlue, the exploit for MS17-010. Overnight, a military-grade capability became free for anyone to download. This is T1588.005 by theft and leak: no purchase, no research, just a download link.

    May 2017

    WannaCry turns the leak into a global event

    The WannaCry ransomware used EternalBlue to spread by itself across unpatched networks. Press and agency reporting estimated more than 200,000 systems in over 150 countries, with the United Kingdom's National Health Service among the hardest hit, cancelling appointments across many hospitals. A patch released in March could have stopped almost all of it. Detection point: IDS signatures for SMB exploit patterns, published within days of the leak.

    August 2018

    Lazarus dresses a fake site in TLS

    The United States agency CISA, in advisory AA18-224A, documented the Lazarus Group campaign AppleJeus: fake cryptocurrency trading sites that delivered trojanized installers, with valid TLS certificates making the sites look legitimate in browsers. This is T1588.004: buy or obtain website certificates so the victim's trust bar stays green. Detection point: certificate transparency logs showing certificates for brand-new domains.

    2021 – 2022

    Conti buys its toolbox, then the receipts leak

    The Conti ransomware syndicate used Cobalt Strike, Mimikatz, and many other off-the-shelf tools, as documented in the United States advisory AA21-265A. In early 2022, a disgruntled member leaked thousands of internal chat messages, showing how the group paid for tooling and licenses. This is T1588.002 at criminal scale: buying proven tools instead of writing them. Detection point: Cobalt Strike default beacon settings and public licensing records.

    2023

    AI itself becomes a product on criminal markets

    Security vendors reported subscription services such as WormGPT and FraudGPT, sold on criminal forums to write phishing content in fluent language. This is T1588.007: the capability being obtained is model access, not code. Detection point so far is weak, which is why the defense leans on process controls, shown in the mitigation section.

    January – February 2024

    A deepfake video call moves 200 million dollars

    The engineering firm Arup confirmed in May 2024 that an employee in its Hong Kong office joined a video call with digitally faked versions of senior colleagues, and then carried out about fifteen transfers worth roughly 200 million Hong Kong dollars, about 25 million United States dollars, as press reporting described. The fake faces and voices were the obtained capability. Detection point: the payment process itself, which lacked an out-of-band verification step.

    Containment, eradication, and recovery

    Containment in a bought-capability incident starts with cutting what the capability needs. That means blocking the known hashes and domains at mail, proxy, and firewall, isolating hosts that ran the payload, and revoking active sessions for affected users. In the WannaCry case, containment also meant closing the protocol door itself: disabling SMBv1 on every remaining machine, because the capability attacked a service, not a person.

    Eradication removes the capability and the door it used. Teams remove implants and staged files, patch the exploited flaw, rotate exposed credentials and signing material, and rebuild affected systems from clean images. When the capability is a stolen certificate, eradication includes the certificate authority: revoke the certificate, publish the update, and re-verify every binary that carries the signer. When the capability is AI-assisted fraud, eradication is mostly process: add out-of-band verification for payments and approvals.

    Recovery proves the door is closed. Teams watch a clean telemetry window, restore data from offline backups where ransomware landed, and re-open services in stages. The strongest teams close with a review that answers three questions: which capability did the attacker have, where did our telemetry first touch it, and which of the six threats in section 5.3 would have caught it sooner.

    Impact and consequences

    The direct costs repeat across the timeline: hospitals cancelled care, manufacturers stopped production lines, and one finance department lost about 25 million dollars in an afternoon. The indirect costs last longer: trust in software updates and video calls took real damage, regulators wrote new rules for build pipelines and payment controls, and insurers raised the bar for ransomware coverage.

    Team response and communication

    Every event needed a team, not a tool. Threat intelligence tracked the families and the sellers. The SOC triaged alerts and ran the hunts. Incident response led containment and rebuilt systems. IT and vulnerability management owned the patching race. Legal and communications handled extortion contact and public statements, and finance owned payment verification. Write these roles into your playbook before you need them, with one decision-maker per stage.

    Lessons learned

    • Stolen trust is the highest-value capability. Inventory your signers and monitor their use, not just their storage.
    • A leaked exploit plus a slow patch cycle is a global incident. Patch KEV-listed flaws on internet-facing systems first.
    • Fresh TLS certificates on fresh domains are the earliest reliable trace of staged attacks. Watch certificate transparency.
    • Tooling purchases leave fingerprints: default settings, shared watermarks, and licensing records. Hunt for the defaults.
    • AI capabilities attack people and process, not code. Out-of-band payment verification and staff code words are detections too.
    • Share what you learn. The leaks and advisories in this case gave every defender the same homework at the same time.

    Mitigation and Defense

    You cannot stop the purchase, but you can make purchased capabilities fail. Enterprise controls, home advice, and a prioritized action list.

    ATT&CK is direct about this technique: the official mitigation is Pre-compromise [M1056], and its description says the technique cannot be easily mitigated with preventive controls because the behavior happens outside enterprise defenses. Defense therefore has two goals. First, reduce what a purchased capability can do once it arrives. Second, shorten the time between a capability appearing in the wild and your organization knowing about it.

    !Common trap

    Do not allowlist software by signer name alone. Stolen certificates sign malware with a perfectly valid chain. Combine signature checks with path, publisher inventory, and reputation so a valid-but-unknown signer still gets reviewed.

    Response checklist

    • Pivot the marker (hash, certificate identity, domain) across 90 days of proxy, DNS, EDR, and mail logs.
    • Enrich: malware repository for the hash, registration data for the domain, CT logs for the certificate, KEV catalog for the CVE.
    • Confirm scope: which hosts, users, and mailboxes touched the capability; note first and last seen.
    • Contain: block the domain and hash, isolate affected hosts, revoke sessions for affected users.
    • Eradicate: remove implants and staged files, patch the exploited flaw, rotate exposed credentials and signing material.
    • Recover: restore from offline backups, watch a clean telemetry window, then reopen services in stages.
    • Report and prevent: record the case, update detections and watchlists, share sanitized indicators with your ISAC.
